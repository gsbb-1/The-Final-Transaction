# Swaps and central clearing: background for the novel

Research note written by an agent on 2026-10-09 for the author. It answers the finance questions in [to-verify.md](to-verify.md) and in the "To verify in research" list of [outline/act-1.md](../outline/act-1.md), and gives the texture a writer needs for the risk room at Walbrook. Figures come from the Bank of England, LCH's public material, the BIS, and the financial press; each is dated, because margin numbers move. Anything marked **unconfirmed** could not be pinned to a primary source and should be treated as a working assumption, not a fact.

## Summary

The mechanism the book relies on is real, and 2022 proved it: when gilt prices fall fast, pension funds and their LDI vehicles must post cash within hours against swaps and repo they used to hedge and to lever up, and if they cannot find the cash they sell gilts, which pushes prices down and produces the next call. Between 23 and 28 September 2022 thirty-year gilt yields rose more than 100 basis points in four days, twice the largest move since 2000, and the Bank of England judged that without intervention £50bn of gilts would have been dumped into a market that trades £12bn a day. What the book stretches is *where* the fragility sits and *who can see it*. A clearing house only counts a bond as collateral once the bond has actually settled into its account, so a bond cannot literally be "pledged to Walbrook" and "out on repo" at the same time; what can happen is that a fund promises the bond, fails to deliver it because it is out on repo, and covers the gap with cash or another bond while telling everyone it is "timing". The same gilt line held by two members is also not evidence of anything: gilts are fungible and a single line has £30bn or more in issue. The honest version of Jonas's insight is not "I can see the same bond twice" but "I can see the fingerprints of a system in which every gilt is working two or three jobs at once: fails that come in pairs, cash paid at nine and swapped for bonds at three, members whose collateral arrives late on exactly the days the repo market is tight." The drafts also need two mechanical corrections throughout: variation margin at a swaps CCP is paid in cash, never in bonds, and the morning call is an automated direct debit that lands at the members' banks around 8am with a 9am deadline, not a 7am phone call.

## 1. Interest-rate swaps, and why falling bond prices mean margin calls

An interest-rate swap is a promise between two parties to exchange interest payments on a notional sum for a number of years: one pays a fixed rate, the other pays a floating rate (in sterling, SONIA). Nothing is lent. The swap's value is zero on day one and then moves with market rates. If you *receive* fixed and rates rise, your swap loses value, because you are locked into receiving less than the market now pays. A pension fund has liabilities that behave like a very long bond, so it hedges them by receiving fixed on long swaps and by holding long gilts. This is liability-driven investment (LDI). To hedge more liabilities than it has cash, the fund borrows against its gilts in the repo market, or uses swaps, which need only collateral rather than the full sum; before 2022 leverage of two to three times was ordinary. The Bank put the LDI sector at over £1 trillion of hedged liabilities, with LDI funds themselves holding about £300bn of assets; many smaller schemes sit in "pooled" LDI funds run by asset managers.

Who else is in the market: banks (as dealers and as clearing members), hedge funds (relative-value trades, often short gilts via repo, which is what Crane is), insurers, and sovereign wealth funds and reserve managers who hold gilts and hedge their rate exposure. Overseas investors hold roughly 30% of the £2.7trn gilt market (Knight Frank, Q3 2025, 33%; ING, 30%). The UK does not publish holdings by country, so "one of the five or six largest foreign holders" in chapter 5 is **unconfirmed** and should stay vague.

Why falling prices mean calls. Gilt prices and yields move inversely; a thirty-year gilt has a duration of roughly 17 to 20, so a 100 basis point rise in yield is a fall of around 17 to 20% in price. A receive-fixed swap loses the same way. The clearing house revalues every position daily and collects the day's loss as variation margin (VM), in cash, that morning. Repo lenders do the same thing bilaterally: when the gilt you borrowed against falls, you post more. So a rate shock hits an LDI fund three ways on the same morning: VM on cleared swaps, collateral on bilateral swaps, and a margin call on repo, while the gilts it would sell to raise cash are the very thing that is falling. In 2022 the Bank's 5 October letter to the Treasury Committee described this plainly: "the rise in yields caused the net asset value of LDI funds to fall significantly ... reflected in margin calls, which the LDI funds had to meet", and pooled funds could not get capital from dozens of pension-scheme investors fast enough, so they sold gilts. That is the doom loop. The scene-level version: a fund has twenty-four hours to find cash or sell; trustees need a meeting to approve a capital injection; the gilt desk is selling into a market with no bids.

## 2. Central clearing: what Walbrook actually does

A CCP stands between buyer and seller on every cleared trade and guarantees both sides. It does not trade, set prices, or "open"; exchanges open, a CCP runs a daily cycle. The real London analogue for Walbrook is LCH Ltd (SwapClear for swaps, RepoClear for gilt repo), supervised by the Bank of England.

**Members and clients.** Only clearing members (about forty to sixty banks at a large CCP) face the CCP. A pension fund, LDI fund or sovereign fund is a *client* of a member and posts collateral *to the member*, which posts to the CCP. Whether the CCP knows who the client is depends on the account: in an individually segregated account (ISA) the client "must be known" to the CCP; in a gross omnibus account the member reports positions and collateral per client; in a net omnibus account the CCP sees only the member's aggregate. A sovereign fund with a large book would almost certainly be in ISAs, so Walbrook's collateral team would know the name. Chapter 5's "our bonds are posted in their client accounts" is right.

**Initial and variation margin.** Initial margin (IM) is the cushion against the cost of closing a defaulter's book over a five-day (members) or seven-day (clients) holding period, recalculated nightly from ten years of history (LCH's model is called PAIRS). IM can be posted in cash or in eligible securities: high-quality government bonds, including gilts, subject to haircuts. LCH's older public schedules ran from about 4% for short bonds to over 10% for the longest, plus an FX add-on; the current schedule is confidential, so use "between four and twelve per cent depending on maturity" and mark it approximate. In stress the model raises IM: UK CCPs' IM rose 31% in March 2020; GBP swap IM rose up to 65% after the mini-budget (Clarus estimate). **Variation margin is the day's profit and loss and at SwapClear it is settled in cash, in the currency of the exposure.** No bonds. A member meets a sterling VM call with sterling in its PPS bank account by 9am or it is in default.

**The daily rhythm (LCH Ltd timetable, public).** End-of-day margin run overnight. Overnight calls sent to the members' PPS banks by 08:00 London; PPS banks confirm by 09:00; excess cash auto-returned from 09:15. Intraday margin runs through the day; a UK intraday call must be confirmed within one hour; UK PPS window 09:00 to 16:00, US PPS 16:00 to 21:00 for dollar calls after London banks close. The "Protected Payments System" is a direct debit, not a phone call: the CCP instructs, the member's PPS bank confirms, and a screen in the risk room turns a line green. On a stress day there are several intraday calls; "a second scheduled call at three" is wrong only in the word "scheduled": the intraday run is routine and calls whenever exposure breaches a threshold, so on a bad day there may be three or four.

**Collateral.** Securities are lodged through the collateral management system (CMS) into the CCP's account at the CSD (CREST for gilts, via Euroclear UK & International) under a deed of charge (a security interest; the member keeps ownership, the CCP gets a charge) or into a title-transfer account. A lodgement is credited **only when it settles**. Until then it does not exist as collateral and the member must cover with cash. EMIR forbids a CCP from re-using securities IM; cash IM may be invested in gilts and reverse repo. Realistic cut-offs: gilt DvP settlement in CREST ends mid-afternoon (14:55 in the Bank's 2016 timetable), delivery-by-value repo settlement runs 15:00 to 17:30, free-of-payment to 18:00; triparty same-day deadlines at LCH around 16:30. The Thursday check in chapter 4 therefore is not "promised but not arrived by the cut-off" but "instructed, failed, cancelled, re-instructed", a fails list.

**Default waterfall.** Defaulter's IM; defaulter's default-fund contribution; CCP's own capital ("skin in the game", tens of millions, around €68m for LCH Ltd in 2020); survivors' default fund (SwapClear's is about €7bn in 2025, sized to "cover 2": the two largest members defaulting in extreme-but-plausible stress); then unfunded assessments on survivors; then VM gains haircutting and tear-up; then resolution by the Bank under FSMA 2023 Schedule 11. Survivors' IM is never used.

**Default management.** When a member misses the 9am deadline the CCP's operations team chase the PPS bank and the member's treasury; declaring a default is a decision for the CCP's executive within hours and the supervisor is on the call. Then: port clients to backup members, hedge the defaulter's book with the Default Management Group (seconded traders from other members), and auction the hedged portfolios to surviving members. Lehman at SwapClear: declared 15 September 2008, hedged over the first week, 90% of risk neutralised by 23 September, five auctions 24 September to 3 October, three weeks total, $9 trillion notional and 66,390 trades, using 35% of Lehman's IM and none of the default fund. So chapter 14's "Walbrook sells what the member had" by nine is too fast; what happens by nine is that the market knows a defaulter is being hedged, and the hedging itself moves prices.

**Can a CCP delay its open?** It has no open. It can declare a non-business day, refuse new trades, or extend a deadline, but the act III beat "Walbrook announces it may not open" should become "Walbrook tells the Bank it cannot guarantee its obligations" or "the Bank announces it is considering resolution".

## 3. Collateral re-use: how "the same bond twice" really happens

Repo is a *sale* with an agreement to buy back: title passes to the lender, who may sell the bond on, repo it again, or deliver it to a CCP as margin. Securities lending is the same with fees. Under English law there was no limit on how much client collateral a prime broker could re-use, which is why Lehman routed its rehypothecation through London in 2008 and why its hedge-fund clients found themselves unsecured creditors. The IMF's Manmohan Singh estimates that pledged collateral is re-used two to three times ("collateral velocity"), so the claims on a bond exceed the bond. In the UK: net gilt repo borrowing is around £200bn, about £85bn of it by hedge funds; pension schemes' own repo stood at £202bn at the end of September 2022. The Bank is consulting on tougher gilt repo rules (policy expected 2027).

Where the fiction stretches: nobody can pledge a bond they have already repo'd out, because they no longer have it; the settlement system will not deliver what is not there. What exists is (a) a *chain*, A repos to B, B repos to C, C lodges at the CCP, each link legitimate, so that one bond backs three promises and when A wants it back the chain has to unwind through everyone; (b) *promised* collateral that fails to settle because the holder has it out on loan and expects it back "by two"; (c) *collateral upgrade* and substitution games, where a member pays cash at nine and swaps in bonds by four, after the repo market has given them back; (d) fails that cascade, the Bank's Money Markets Committee complained in 2022 about the level of gilt repo fails and "counterparties in a chain failing thus causing a ripple effect". Also Archegos (2021): eight prime brokers each saw only their own slice of one client's exposure; the same opacity applies between a CCP and the repo market. **What Walbrook can see:** every lodgement, fail and substitution in its own books; which member lodged which ISIN and how much; payment timing. **What it cannot see:** who the beneficial owner is in an omnibus account, whether that owner has repo'd the rest of the line, what is lodged at other CCPs or in bilateral margin, and any chain beyond its own leg. The same ISIN at two members proves nothing on its own.

Recommended framing for the book: Jonas's record is a log of *correlated fails and late substitutions*, the behavioural trace of over-pledged collateral, built over four years from his own fails list plus Priya's position reports from members who should not have sent them. The memo at 37 said: the system's gilts are working two or three jobs; when a shock makes everyone ask for the bond on the same morning, the chain cannot unwind in a day. That is defensible, and it is what the Bank itself worried about in 2022.

## 4. Scale and speed

**Variation margin.** The Bank reported that daily VM calls at UK CCPs in March 2020 ran about five times the January–February average. SwapClear's largest single-day total VM paid to the CCP was $26.3bn in Q1 2020 against a previous peak of $17.9bn (Q3 2019); its largest single call to one member was €6.2bn in Q3 2020. A normal day at a very large swaps CCP is therefore low single-digit billions across all currencies; for a sterling-centred CCP like Walbrook, "a few hundred million to a billion or two" is right for a quiet day, £9.6bn on the first shock day is at the high end but defensible for a record, and the £21–22bn implied in chapter 11 (fourteen or fifteen payable, gap of seven) is a plausible all-time record, roughly SwapClear's 2020 peak in sterling terms. Record IM calls were $6.6bn on a single day at SwapClear in Q1 2020; total SwapClear IM was about £145bn at end-September 2022 (Risk.net, year **unconfirmed** from the snippet).

**Gilt moves.** September 2022: thirty-year yields rose roughly from 3.5% to just over 5% between 23 and 27 September; two daily rises of more than 35bp against a previous record (since 2000) of 29bp; 67bp intraday on 28 September before the Bank's announcement knocked yields down by more than 100bp the same afternoon. Over four days the move was "more than twice as large as the largest move since 2000", and "more than three times larger than any other historical move" (Cunliffe, 5 October 2022). In price terms that is a fall of around 20% in a thirty-year gilt in four days. LDI funds sold about £25bn of gilts over five weeks, 30% of it in the first five days; the Bank bought £19.3bn against a £65bn envelope and sold it all back by 12 January 2023. So "another four points down" at Monday's open in chapter 11 is modest by 2022 standards; a 10–15 point fall in long gilts over the weekend is within precedent.

**Sterling.** 24 June 2016: minus 7.6% on the day, the worst since the float and the ninth-worst since 1862. 26 September 2022: minus 4.85% intraday to $1.0327 in Asian hours. A 6% fall by 8am on the Monday of chapter 14, with a sovereign fund publicly leaving and a clearing member failing, is on the edge but not beyond the record; 4–5% by eight and 7–8% by lunch would read as more precise.

**Oil.** Brent's largest recent one-day gains: +10.25% (27 August 2015), +9.6% on a Hormuz scare (date **unconfirmed**), +7% settlement on the June 2025 Israel–Iran strikes (intraday peak lower). In August 1990 oil roughly doubled over two months, not overnight. A sunk tanker and a closed strait is bigger than any of those, so "oil up 19% in Asia and still going" in chapter 6 is aggressive but not absurd; 12–15% would be unimpeachable. The transmission in chapter 6 is right in direction: oil up, inflation expectations up, expected Bank Rate up, gilt yields up, and sterling down because the UK is a net energy importer whose terms of trade fell 6.2% in 2022, the worst since the 1970s, and because gilts already carry a UK-specific premium (the move in 2026 was "materially larger" in gilts than in Bunds). "Further than anyone else's" is an assumption, not a law; "further than most" is safer.

**Sovereign fund unwind.** There is no notice period to close cleared positions: the fund's executing dealers trade offsetting swaps, which clear and net down, IM falls, and excess collateral is released the next morning through CMS and PPS. Gilt sales settle T+1. The constraints are market depth (£12bn a day in long gilts in 2022) and the clearing agreements with its banks, which have contractual notice periods (typically weeks, **unconfirmed**) for termination but not for reducing positions. So the realistic version of chapter 9 is not "give notice to Walbrook" but "instruct the three clearing banks to start closing out from the open, in size order, and tell the custodian to move proceeds", and the point that panics the market is the leak of that instruction, not a formal notice. A freeze: UK sanctions are made by regulations under SAMLA 2018 and can be laid in days; proceeds sitting with a London custodian would be caught; selling ahead of a designation is lawful, and moving proceeds to a non-Western custodian is what a fund would do (legal detail **unconfirmed**).

## 5. Oversight

The **Bank of England** supervises UK CCPs (its Financial Market Infrastructure directorate; the PRA supervises banks, the FCA conduct). Since FSMA 2023 the Bank writes the CCP rulebook itself and runs the CCP resolution regime (Schedule 11; HM Treasury Code of Practice, January 2024). "The regulator" throughout the drafts is therefore the Bank, and the chapter 10 line "two chairs and one institution" is exactly right; the Governor would attend a crisis meeting of that kind; the FCA chief executive might, for market-conduct reasons, but need not.

**Treasury and Chancellor.** The Treasury has no power to direct a CCP or its staff, and no power to demand an employee's papers. Its real levers: it indemnifies emergency operations (it indemnified the 2022 gilt purchases), it alone commits public funds, and under section 61 of the Financial Services Act 2012 the Chancellor may *direct the Bank* once the Bank has given a "public funds notification" and there is a serious threat to financial stability; the 2025 MoU on financial crisis management sets this out. So Strand can make the Bank's intervention conditional, and she can refuse the indemnity, which is a far better weapon in act II than any power over Jonas. The demand for his notes in chapter 10 is correctly written as a request with an implied threat (theft of the employer's confidential material; the police), not an order.

**Bank liquidity tools.** Short-Term Repo and the Indexed Long-Term Repo for banks; the Discount Window; the Contingent Term Repo Facility; the Contingent NBFI Repo Facility (CNRF), opened for applications in January 2025, which lends cash against gilts to insurers, DB pension schemes and LDI funds when the Bank activates it in a gilt-market stress; and, as in 2022, outright temporary gilt purchases. A CCP itself can hold an account at the Bank and borrow against collateral. The act III "final transaction" could plausibly route through a CNRF activation or an indemnified purchase operation.

**Whistleblowing.** The Bank is a "prescribed person" under the Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998 for "the functioning of clearing houses (including central counterparties)". The route is the Bank's Intelligence and Whistleblowing Team: whistleblowing@bankofengland.co.uk, confidential line 020 3461 8703, Threadneedle Street; a case officer is assigned and confidentiality promised. There is no special "named individual at a clearing house" procedure; it is the general whistleblowing regime, and the CCP's own policy will say to raise it internally first. PIDA protects the employee from dismissal for a protected disclosure, which does not stop a firm reporting a theft.

## 6. Jargon, screens, rhythm, and what fiction gets wrong

A clearing risk room is quiet. Nobody shouts prices; there are no prices to shout. The screens show: the margin run status (has the end-of-day run completed, is it late); the PPS call sheet, one line per member per currency, grey to green as banks confirm; the fails and unmatched list from CREST and the triparty agents; collateral balances, cash versus securities, by member, with excess and shortfall; concentration limits (no member may post more than X% of its IM in one issuer or one line); backtesting exceptions; and a news feed and Bloomberg on the wall. People say "the run", "the call's gone out", "Barclays hasn't confirmed", "they've paid", "we're waiting on one PPS bank", "I'm calling intraday", "they want to substitute", "that line's special" (a gilt in heavy repo demand, which is itself a sign it is being re-used), "cover 2", "what's our skin in the game", "porting", "the DMG", "the waterfall", "margin period of risk", "add-ons", "wrong-way risk" (a member's collateral falling in the same event that hurts its positions, which is the gilt-collateral problem in one phrase). The day: overnight run; 06:00 settlement opens; 08:00 calls out; 09:00 deadline; 09:15 excess returned; hourly intraday runs; 14:55 gilt DvP closes; 15:00–17:30 DBV repo settlement, when bonds come home and substitutions land; 16:00 UK PPS closes; dollar calls to US PPS until 21:00; then the end-of-day run.

| Term | Meaning in the room |
| --- | --- |
| IM | Initial margin: the cushion against closing a defaulter out; cash or bonds |
| VM | Variation margin: today's loss, paid in cash by 9am |
| PPS | Protected Payments System: the direct-debit route to members' banks |
| The run | The margin calculation; "the run's late" is bad news |
| Call / intraday call | The demand for margin; an instruction, not a phone call |
| Haircut | Discount applied to a bond's value as collateral; 4–12% for gilts by maturity (approx.) |
| ISIN | The identifier of a bond line; the 2045 gilt is one ISIN with tens of billions in issue |
| Fail | A delivery that did not settle |
| Substitution | Swapping one piece of collateral for another |
| Excess / shortfall | Collateral above or below requirement, per member |
| Repo / reverse repo | Sale and repurchase of a bond for cash; title passes |
| Special | A bond in such demand in repo that it lends below the general rate |
| DBV | Delivery by value: the CREST overnight repo mechanism, 15:00–17:30 |
| ISA / OSA | Individually segregated / omnibus client account |
| Porting | Moving a defaulter's clients to another member |
| DMG | Default Management Group: seconded traders who hedge the defaulter's book |
| Cover 2 | Default fund sized for the two biggest members failing at once |
| SITG | Skin in the game: the CCP's own capital in the waterfall |
| Waterfall | The order in which losses are absorbed |
| MPOR | Margin period of risk: 5 days members, 7 days clients |
| Wrong-way risk | Collateral that falls in the same shock as the position it secures |
| The Bank | The Bank of England; never "the BoE" aloud |
| LDI | Liability-driven investment; the pension funds' leveraged hedging |
| Doom loop | Calls force gilt sales which force calls |

Common mistakes in fiction: a CCP with a trading floor; margin paid in bonds; the CCP "opening"; the clearing house seeing everyone's positions across the market; a margin call as a shouted phone demand; a clearing house "collapsing" in an hour (it would be resolved, over days); "the regulator" as an unnamed body separate from the Bank; confusing yields with prices; calling the risk officers "traders"; and ignoring that the Chancellor's "the system is sound" is addressed to the gilt market, not to voters.

## What the drafts get wrong or stretch

Items from [to-verify.md](to-verify.md) and the act I verify list that fall in this subject. Chapter-and-line references are to the drafts of 2026-10-09.

- **Ch 2 (10, 14), compliance filter.** Outside this subject. Data-loss-prevention tools do exist that route an alert to the recipient's line manager for a business/personal decision, and modern phone mail clients attach text recognised in a photo in some workflows; both plausible, both **unconfirmed**. Simplest fix: Jonas typed the entry out as well as photographing it.
- **Ch 4 (18), daily reconciliation and cut-off.** Yes: the collateral team works a fails and unmatched list every morning from CREST and triparty reports. Change "promised and has not arrived by the cut-off" to "instructed and failed", with cut-offs of 14:55 (gilt DvP) and 18:00 (free of payment).
- **Ch 4 (20), pension scheme posting gilts via a member; two-day fail.** The route is right, but add the LDI vehicle: the scheme's LDI fund, through its clearing bank. A fail that persists two days is plausible (fails rose in 2022). But the bond is not "posted" until it settles, so the member has been covering the shortfall in cash since Tuesday, which it would rather not. That is what makes the member ring the fund.
- **Ch 4 (42), demanding cash.** Reverse it: the member is already paying cash because an uncredited lodgement counts for nothing; Priya's threat should be that she will refuse the re-lodgement and report the line to the member's credit officer, or tell the member its client is instructing bonds it does not hold.
- **Ch 4 and 6 (82), seeing the same ISIN at two members.** Walbrook sees ISIN and nominal per member account, so it *can* see the same line in two places, but that is normal and proves nothing. Rewrite #212 so the evidence is behavioural: the same fund's bonds arriving through two members on the same day, both failing, both "timing", and the fund's own position report showing the line out on repo. - **Ch 6 (22), normal day's VM.** "A few hundred million" is low for a large all-currency CCP, fine for a sterling-centred one. Suggest "a billion or so on an ordinary day".

The draft's order (Wellington open two hours, Sydney in eight minutes, Tokyo an hour later) does not fit either season. At 11.52pm GMT in winter: Wellington open nearly three hours, Sydney open fifty minutes, Tokyo in eight minutes.
- **Act I list: Jonas's vantage point.** Realistic only in the reframed form above (fails, substitutions, late cash-to-bond swaps, members' leaked position reports), never as a direct view of double pledging.
- **Act III: "Walbrook may not open".** Reframe as inability to meet obligations / Bank resolution.
