# Information security: governance, confidentiality, records and the law of who may know what

Research note written by an agent on 2026-10-09 for the author. It covers information security as a corporate function and as a legal and governmental practice, not the technical side (networks, encryption, forensics), which is in a separate note. Everything here was checked against the sources listed at the end unless marked **unconfirmed**. Where the law is summarised, it is England and Wales law as it stands in 2026; it is background for fiction, not legal advice.

## Summary

The single most useful fact for this book is that in English law **information cannot be stolen**. Since *Oxford v Moss* (1978) confidential information is not "property" under the Theft Act, so a firm that says an employee "stole confidential material" is using a press phrase, not a legal one. What the firm actually has is a civil claim (breach of contract and of confidence, an injunction, an order for "delivery up" of the documents) and, if it can find one, a criminal hook that is not theft: fraud by abuse of position, a Computer Misuse Act charge, or a Data Protection Act offence. Around that fact the rest falls into place. A regulated firm's policies (classification, need-to-know, clean desk, acceptable use, monitoring) make a private notebook of work facts a disciplinary offence and a civil wrong, not a crime. Whistleblowing law protects a worker from the employer for telling the regulator; it is thin protection for telling a journalist and none against the police. A three-year-old external memo at the Treasury is a dull, retrievable, FOI-able document and not an official secret, because the Official Secrets Act 1989 has no category for economic information. Police can arrest, hold for 24 hours (36 with a superintendent), seize devices and keep them "for as long as is necessary", and a department can ask them to investigate, as the Home Office did with Damian Green in 2008, which ended in no charges and two inquiries into the police. Most of the manuscript's plot points are realistic as drafted; the ones that need a nudge are the length of Priya's detention, the biro note on the memo, and the routing of the compliance alert.

## 1. The information-security function in a regulated financial firm

**Who owns it.** A chief information security officer (CISO), usually under the chief operating or technology officer, owns the controls and the policy set. Compliance owns the regulatory obligations and runs communications surveillance. Legal owns confidentiality clauses and injunctions. HR owns discipline. In an incident the four argue about ownership for an hour and then a lawyer takes it. ISO/IEC 27001 is the standard UK financial firms certify against (Euronext Clearing, a real CCP, publishes its ISO 27001 statement of applicability); NIST's framework is the American equivalent.

**Classification.** ISO 27001 controls 5.12 and 5.13 require information to be classified and labelled. The usual corporate scheme is **Public / Internal / Confidential / Restricted**. Member codes, margin figures and collateral holdings would be Confidential at least; the morning margin call before it is issued, Restricted. Government uses OFFICIAL (with the OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE marking), SECRET and TOP SECRET (2023 policy, updated 2024). A Treasury memo on financial stability would be OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE, the marking for the bulk of government paper not meant for publication. Nothing in this novel would be SECRET; a character who says "classified" about a Treasury finance memo is making the commonest fiction mistake in this field.

**Need-to-know and information barriers.** The FCA's Market Watch newsletters criticise firms whose insider lists are too long and whose access controls do not enforce need-to-know. "Chinese walls" is the old name for what SYSC 10.2 calls **information barriers**. In a CCP the live barrier is between the risk function, which sees every member's position, and anyone who could trade on it. A head of collateral risk sees everything by design; the question is never whether Jonas may know it but whether he may write it down somewhere the firm does not control.

**Clean desk, acceptable use, personal notes.** ISO 27001 control 7.7 is the clear-desk rule. Every regulated firm has an **acceptable use policy** staff sign: the systems and everything on them are the firm's, communications may be monitored, firm information must not go to personal accounts or off the premises. Firms rarely have a policy headed "personal notes", but contract, acceptable use policy and records policy together make a notebook of member codes kept at home a breach, however the notebook was bought. *Simpkin v Berkeley Group* (2017): a finance director who emailed a personal document from work to home could not even claim it was private against the employer, because he had signed the IT policy.

**Monitoring and the notice staff get.** Employers may read communications on their own systems for business purposes under the Investigatory Powers (Interception by Businesses etc.) Regulations 2018 (SI 2018/356), provided staff have been told; the ICO's October 2023 guidance on monitoring workers requires transparency and proportionality. The notice is a paragraph in the contract and another in the acceptable use policy. Regulated firms go further because MiFID II and the Market Abuse Regulation require them to record and monitor business communications; most run a **lexicon-based surveillance** tool that flags watch-words, security identifiers and large sums, with false-positive rates industry commentary puts above 90%. Alerts are reviewed by a surveillance team inside compliance, not by line managers; the reviewer closes most with a one-line disposition ("personal, no action") and escalates the few that matter to the head of compliance, who decides whether HR, legal or the business head needs to know. The audit trail is itself a regulatory record. Routing an alert straight to the line manager, as chapter 2 has it, is unusual: a design choice a compliance officer would have to defend (see Plot implications).

**What a CEO is expected to do.** In 2016 Barclays' chief executive, Jes Staley, twice tried to identify the author of an anonymous letter about a colleague. In May 2018 the FCA and PRA fined him £642,430, their first case under the Senior Managers Regime: he should have left it to the people with "expertise and responsibility for whistleblowing". The expected behaviour is hands off: the CEO is told, asks whether compliance and legal are handling it, and does not direct what happens to the employee. A CEO who tells the CRO to close the alert as "no action" while acting on its contents, then phones a Treasury contact about an employee's private diary, is doing what the regime exists to prevent; it is realistic that he does it, and it is ammunition for later. A compliance review screen is a queue: alert ID, policy hit, sender, recipient, risk score, reviewer, status; the verbs are "clear", "escalate", "hold".

## 2. Who owns an employee's private notes about their work

**The law in three layers.** *Faccenda Chicken v Fowler* (1986) sorts what an employee knows into trivial or public information (free), "mere" confidential information (not to be used or disclosed during employment, but part of his own knowledge afterwards unless the contract says otherwise) and trade secrets (the employer's for ever). The Trade Secrets Regulations 2018 define the third category. The employee's general skill and experience, the "mental toolkit", is always his own.

**Applied to Jonas's notebooks.** The books are his: he bought them, and under *Oxford v Moss* the information in them is not property and cannot be stolen. The thesis that collateral is pledged several times over is his judgement. But a line reading "member code, stock name, nominal, dates of overlap, report signed G.P." is Walbrook's confidential information, arguably a member's trade secret held under contract, and keeping it at home breaches his contract and the duty of confidence. *Brandeaux v Chadwick* (2010) is the closest real case: a compliance officer emailed company documents to her private account, used none, and said she might need them for her job and for a tribunal claim; the judge held she had no right to do it, doubted that wanting to report to the regulator would justify it either, and upheld her summary dismissal and an order to return everything.

**What Walbrook can do.** Summary dismissal (certain). An interim injunction and an order for **delivery up** of the notebooks and copies, which a High Court judge will hear at short notice, even on a Sunday by telephone, if the firm pleads urgency (likely, and a strong scene). Damages, possibly worth nothing: in *Marathon Asset Management v Seddon* (2017) two employees copied thousands of files, used almost none, and were ordered to pay £1 each, with the claimant paying most of the costs. A report to the Bank of England as supervisor and a notification to affected members. A report to the Information Commissioner under s.170 of the Data Protection Act 2018 (obtaining or retaining personal data without the controller's consent), prosecuted a few dozen times a year with fines in the hundreds or low thousands; but member codes and nominals are not personal data, so this fits Priya's laptop better than Jonas's notebooks.

**What Walbrook cannot do.** It cannot have him charged with theft of information. The police can record "theft of confidential material" as an allegation, but a custody sergeant or the CPS will look for a real offence: fraud by abuse of position (Fraud Act 2006 s.4, needing dishonest abuse of a position of trust with intent to gain or cause loss, which the £3m offer in chapter 12 could be twisted to supply), or unauthorised access under the Computer Misuse Act s.1 (which fails, because Jonas was authorised to see what he saw; *DPP v Bignell*, *ex parte Allison*). Walbrook cannot search his home; only police with a warrant or the High Court with a search order can. It cannot stop him remembering. And the notebooks remain evidence that it did nothing for four years, which is why a real firm's lawyers would advise against a public fuss.

**Typical contract wording.** "All documents, notes, memoranda and records (in whatever medium) relating to the business of the Company made or acquired by you in the course of your employment are the property of the Company and shall be returned on request and in any event on termination." That is the clause Haldane's "Walbrook's property" in chapter 10 leans on. It is in nearly every City contract and enforceable as a contract term; it does not make the breach a crime.

## 3. Whistleblowing

**The statute.** The Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998 (Part IVA of the Employment Rights Act 1996). A **qualifying disclosure** is information the worker reasonably believes shows a criminal offence, a breach of a legal obligation, a danger to health and safety, or concealment of these, and believes is in the public interest. It is **protected** if made to the employer (s.43C); to a **prescribed person** (s.43F), which for clearing houses is the Bank of England; or, under s.43G, to anyone else including a journalist, but only if the worker reasonably believes it substantially true, does not act for gain, has already raised it with the employer or regulator or reasonably fears victimisation or a cover-up, and the disclosure is reasonable in all the circumstances. Section 43H covers "exceptionally serious" failures with fewer preconditions. Protection means uncapped compensation for dismissal or detriment. It does not stop the dismissal, oblige anyone to act on the warning, or defend against a criminal charge or a civil injunction.

**The regulators' regime.** The FCA and PRA rules (SYSC 18, 2016) require banks and insurers to appoint a non-executive "whistleblowers' champion", tell staff they may go straight to the regulator, report to the board annually, and tell the regulator if they lose a tribunal case to a whistleblower. **Unconfirmed:** whether the Bank of England imposes equivalent rules on the CCPs it supervises; I found none. Confirmed: the Bank is a prescribed person for "the functioning of clearing houses (including central counterparties)", publishes an annual report on disclosures, and takes them by email (whistleblowing@bankofengland.co.uk), telephone (020 3461 8703) or post to the Legal Directorate, Threadneedle Street, promising confidentiality. Chapter 7's "form I had bookmarked" is this. The Bank would not tell Walbrook who had filed; the substance of a filing from a head of collateral risk would identify him anyway.

**Real cases.** Paul Moore, head of group regulatory risk at HBOS, warned the board in 2004 that its sales culture was dangerous, was dismissed by the chief executive, James Crosby ("the role was redundant"), settled for over £500,000 with a gagging clause, and in February 2009 used parliamentary privilege before the Treasury Committee to say it all; Crosby resigned as deputy chairman of the FSA within days. Moore said afterwards that he had "wished I was dead". Paul Carlier, a foreign-exchange trader, followed his bank's policy and still lost at tribunal on protection. The pattern: the whistleblower is usually right, usually fired, usually paid off and silenced, and heard years later in a forum the employer cannot reach.

**Journalists.** Disclosures to the press are protected far less often. A worker who tells a reporter before the employer or regulator has a weak s.43G case; one who tells them the same afternoon as a regulatory filing has a better one. Priya's order in chapter 11 ("to the regulator and to a journalist") is right. Protection is only against the employer; it does nothing about an arrest.

## 4. Government records and the Treasury

**Registries, files and minutes.** Whitehall kept paper **registered files** until roughly the 2000s: a reference (Treasury series carry the prefix T), a jacket, numbered folios on the right and a **minute sheet** on the left where officials wrote dated, initialled notes. An incoming paper was registered, "put up" to the responsible official, minuted, and sent back to be "put by". The Treasury private office kept its own files of ministerial correspondence. Today it is electronic: documents live in a records management system (EDRM), are "declared as records" into a file plan, and retrieval leaves an audit log, not a biro note. Post is scanned and date-stamped ("RECEIVED HM TREASURY" with the date is accurate). A **departmental records officer** oversees it. Under the Public Records Act 1958, as amended in 2010, records of historical value go to the National Archives at 20 years (fully phased in by 2023); the rest is destroyed to a schedule.

**"Noted. File."** Accurate as a minute, though more likely typed than handwritten now. A memo from a clearing house's CRO with a covering note calling it "one analyst's view" would be handled at Grade 7 or deputy director level in the financial stability directorate: read, perhaps minuted to the team, perhaps mentioned to the Bank, filed. The Chancellor sees only what the private office puts in the box, and the box holds what the department "submits" with a recommendation. So Strand's later denial that she saw it (act II, ch. 25) is almost certainly true and also irrelevant: under the Carltona doctrine the official's knowledge is the department's.

**Retrieving it three years later.** Anyone in the directorate can find it by search in seconds; a paper file comes back from off-site storage in a day. Retrieval is logged. A printed copy's footer or cover sheet shows who printed it and when: the modern equivalent of chapter 10's second biro note.

**Who can see it, and FOI.** Inside the Treasury, anyone with a business need. Outside, it is FOI-able: a request for "any correspondence from Walbrook Clearing House concerning collateral reuse" must be answered within 20 working days. The Treasury would consider s.29 (prejudice to the economy; real Treasury decision notices show it argued and sometimes upheld), s.35 (policy formulation, the "safe space"), s.36 (effective conduct of public affairs, needing a "qualified person's" opinion), s.41 (information provided in confidence) and s.43 (commercial interests), most subject to a public-interest test. In a crisis it would refuse under s.29 and s.35 and the requester would appeal to the ICO, which takes months. A journalist with a leaked copy is faster.

**What a private office can and cannot ask of a private citizen.** Nothing binding. The Chancellor has no power to compel a private individual to hand over his own documents, sign a statement, or stay silent. A private secretary can ask, and the asking carries weight because of what else the state can do; that is how chapter 10 plays it, and the VERIFY note there is answered. The lawful routes to the notebooks run through a court (Walbrook's claim for delivery up, or a search order) or the police (a PACE s.8 warrant, or s.19 seizure on arrest). A private office could realistically add a request that Jonas sign a confidentiality undertaking "pending the review", a device used with external advisers, giving the Treasury a contractual lever it otherwise lacks. The Official Secrets Act does not bind him: he is not a Crown servant or government contractor.

**Government requests to police.** Ministers cannot direct police investigations; operational independence is a constitutional convention. Departments do ask the police to investigate leaks, and the request comes from the permanent secretary, not the minister. On 8 September 2008 the Home Office permanent secretary wrote to the Metropolitan Police; Christopher Galley, a junior official, was arrested on 19 November and Damian Green MP on 27 November 2008 for conspiring to commit misconduct in public office; Green was held nine hours and his home and offices searched; on 16 April 2009 the DPP announced no charges; the Home Affairs Committee found that senior civil servants had exaggerated the national-security threat to get the police to act, and HMIC said "noble intentions are not good enough". In May 2019, after the Cabinet Office's Huawei leak inquiry cost Gavin Williamson his job, Assistant Commissioner Neil Basu said no offence had been committed under the Official Secrets Act or misconduct in public office and the Met would not investigate. That July, over the leaked Kim Darroch cables, Basu warned editors that publication "may also be a criminal matter", was condemned across politics, and partially retracted. These episodes are the pattern for Carrow's request about Priya and for Calloway's second story.

## 5. Police and the state

**Arrest and detention.** Under PACE s.24 an officer needs reasonable suspicion of an offence and reasonable grounds to believe arrest is **necessary** (Code G); since 2012 the Code says an officer who wants an interview must consider whether a voluntary one would do, so a real force would first ask Priya to come in. At the station the clock runs from when detention is authorised: 24 hours without charge, 36 with a superintendent's authority for an indictable offence, beyond that only on a magistrates' warrant, up to 96. Reviews by an inspector at six hours, then every nine. Forty-eight hours over a confidential-information allegation would need a magistrates' court to agree, which is implausible; overnight and release the next day "under investigation" or on pre-charge bail is the realistic shape. Interview is under caution with a solicitor; "no comment" is lawful and common.

**Seizure of devices.** PACE s.19 lets an officer seize anything reasonably believed to be evidence; s.20 extends it to data on computers; s.22 lets police keep it "for so long as is necessary", with no time limit, though they should return it once a copy would do and must give the owner a copy of the data on request (s.21). In practice a phone goes into a forensics queue measured in months. Journalistic material is "excluded" or "special procedure" material obtainable only through a circuit judge under Schedule 1, and s.10 of the Contempt of Court Act 1981 protects sources, so Calloway's notes are safer than Priya's laptop. **Passcodes:** a RIPA s.49 notice can require a PIN or key; refusal carries up to two years (five for national security). Without a notice, refusing is not an offence, but phones are routinely imaged without the PIN. Owners can apply to the magistrates for return under the Police (Property) Act 1897.

**The legal basis the police would claim.** Not theft. A realistic first allegation is fraud by abuse of position, with the Kessler offer as the "gain"; for Priya, a Data Protection Act s.170 or Computer Misuse Act s.1 allegation if her download exceeded her access rights; or simply "an allegation received from HM Treasury" that an officer did not look at hard enough, which is the Green pattern.

**Cabinet Office and vetting as texture.** Treasury officials hold at least the Baseline Personnel Security Standard (BPSS); many hold **SC** (Security Check, the commonest clearance, for regular access to SECRET); a few in the Chancellor's private office hold **DV** (Developed Vetting, for TOP SECRET, with a long interview about one's life, money and associations). UK Security Vetting, in the Cabinet Office, runs it. Clearance is about the person, classification about the paper; nothing in a financial-stability memo is above OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE, so "you're not cleared for that" would be wrong. Leak inquiries are run by the Cabinet Office's Government Security Group and departmental security officers; they interview, read access logs, and can refer to the police. Their usual result is nothing.

## 6. Texture and jargon

A **compliance officer** says: "it's a hit on the lexicon", "we've dispositioned it", "it's a Conduct Rule issue", "I'll need to notify" (Principle 11, the duty to tell the regulator), "put it to the whistleblowing champion", "hold the account". A **CISO** says: "DLP caught it", "that's exfil", "we've pulled his access", "JML's been actioned", "I'm not going to say breach until legal says breach". A **records manager** (the registry clerk's successor) says: "it's on the file", "I'll put it up", "it's been declared", "that's a transfer year", and about FOI, "it's in scope", "s.35's engaged". A **private secretary** says: "the Chancellor has seen", "the Chancellor has noted" (she has not read it), "it's gone into the box", "we'll submit on that", "No. 10 are content", "can we get a line on this", "that's one for officials", and, to end a meeting, "I think we're clear."

**Common fiction mistakes.** Calling a Treasury finance memo "classified". Having a civilian "sign the Official Secrets Act" (one acknowledges it; it applies anyway, and only to Crown servants and contractors). Treating an economic leak as an OSA offence (the real hooks are misconduct in public office and the Civil Service Code). Charging "theft of information". A minister phoning the Commissioner to order an arrest (a permanent secretary writes; the police decide). Whistleblower protection stopping a dismissal or an arrest (it only pays compensation afterwards). A journalist's phone seized like anyone else's. A company "owning" what an employee remembers.

## Plot implications

**Jonas's four-year record (chs. 1–14).** Realistic. The books are his; the member data in them is Walbrook's confidential information and keeping it at home breaches his contract; his own analysis is his. His reasoning in chapter 14 ("the books are mine... that is what they will say") is correct as law and as the firm's likely line. No change needed. A gift for act II: a lawyer or Calloway tells him in one sentence that information cannot be stolen, and that what Walbrook can get is a civil order for delivery up, which takes a judge and a hearing he can attend.

**Graham and Haldane learn of the record through a compliance alert and say nothing (ch. 2).** Realistic in substance, with two nudges. Routing: alerts normally go to a compliance surveillance reviewer, not the line manager. Smallest change: the reviewer, seeing a member code in a photograph of the head of collateral risk's private notebook, escalates to the head of compliance, who brings it to Graham "before it goes anywhere else"; Graham stays first reader and a third person now knows. Or keep the text and make the manager-review queue an oddity of Walbrook's that compliance has wanted to fix. Second, Haldane's "close the alert, no action" while acting on it is a conduct-rule breach and, with the call to Carrow, a disclosure of an employee's personal data with no lawful basis. Realistic (Staley did worse with less) and worth keeping: it is what later makes Haldane vulnerable and gives Graham his choice.

**Jonas files with the regulator and loses access within 71 minutes (ch. 7).** The filing to the Bank of England is a protected disclosure to a prescribed person. The Bank would not name him to Walbrook; Walbrook does not need to be told. Cutting his access is a detriment for which he could later claim. The speed, as the chapter itself concludes, points to the Treasury rather than the Bank. Realistic as drafted; the VERIFY at line 82 can be closed: there is no CCP-specific individual route, it is PIDA whistleblowing to the Bank as prescribed person, and the Bank's contact details are public.

**The memo, stamped "Noted. File.", retrieved three years on (ch. 10).** Realistic if the memo arrived on paper (Graham's covering note suggests it did); a typed minute is likelier today, but a handwritten "Noted. File." on a received paper is within living practice and reads better. Change the second biro note, "Retrieved from registry": retrieval is logged electronically and nobody annotates the paper. Smallest change: Carrow's copy is a fresh printout whose footer carries the file reference and the date and time it was printed, before the sinking; or a records-team slip stapled to it with the request date. Same kitchen-table discovery, less contrivance.

**The Treasury asks for the notebooks "for safekeeping" (ch. 10).** Realistic as a request with an implied threat, which is how it is written; the Treasury has no power to require it, and the VERIFY at line 72 can be closed on that basis. Two realistic additions if wanted: Carrow could ask Jonas to sign a short confidentiality undertaking "pending the review", and Haldane's "Walbrook's property" line could be backed by the standard contract clause quoted in section 2 above.

**The sovereign fund's identity hidden behind client accounts (ch. 5).** Realistic. Under EMIR Article 39 a client may clear through an omnibus segregated account, in which the CCP records the clearing member's pooled client positions and the member keeps the client-level records; only under individual segregation would the CCP record the client. Three banks, three omnibus accounts, is a clean way to hide a single fund from the CCP's screens. **Unconfirmed:** whether a UK CCP would nonetheless be able to identify a very large omnibus client on request (one source says it must be able to, for portability); the Bank of England and Treasury would in any case learn it from the banks within hours of a crisis. Jonas's never naming the fund in the record is good practice and good character.

**Priya detained on a government request, laptop and phone seized (act II, chs. 16, 20).** Realistic in kind, wrong in one detail. The request would come from a senior official (Carrow) to the Met in writing, as the Home Office permanent secretary's did in 2008, and the police would decide; Strand not asking how, and saying "theft" twice for the minutes, is how a minister keeps her distance. The custody record would name an offence (fraud by abuse of position, or a Data Protection Act s.170 or Computer Misuse Act allegation), and a careful officer would first offer a voluntary interview. The change: "released after two days" needs a superintendent's extension and then a magistrates' warrant, which no court would grant on these facts. Smallest change: arrested Sunday afternoon, held overnight, interviewed under caution with a duty solicitor, released Monday afternoon "under investigation" after about twenty hours, devices kept under s.22 with no return date and a warning of a s.49 notice if she will not give the passcodes. The devices staying with the police for months is accurate. Her paper notebook surviving because paper was not what they were looking for is plausible and a good echo.

**Calloway publishes from a leaked memo (act II, ch. 25).** Realistic, and legally cleaner than the characters may think. The memo is not an official secret: the OSA 1989 has no category for economic information. A civil-servant leaker risks dismissal under the Civil Service Code and a theoretical misconduct-in-public-office charge the DPP would almost certainly not bring (Green, 2009; Huawei, 2019). The paper's exposure is defamation, which truth answers, and the Editors' Code public-interest test, which "exposing serious impropriety" and "protecting the public from being misled" plainly meet. The Treasury could not realistically injunct a three-year-old warning memo. A Basu-style police warning to editors is possible and would be a gift to the story. Strand's denial being true is correct and, as the outline says, irrelevant.

**Walbrook reports "theft of confidential material" (ch. 14).** Realistic as what Graham texts and a press office says; not what a lawyer writes or a charge sheet says. Smallest change, if any: keep the phrase as the firm's line, and make the first legal document Jonas sees in act II a civil one, Walbrook's application for an interim injunction and delivery up, with the police interest riding on the Treasury's allegation rather than Walbrook's. That split (firm to court, state to police) is accurate and useful: it lets Graham, who signed the court statement, learn that the arrests were not Walbrook's doing.
