# Cybersecurity, surveillance and insider risk

Background research for the novel, compiled by an agent on 2026-10-09 from web sources. It is written for a novelist: how things work, what an insider would notice, and what a scene would look like. It is not operational guidance. Anything not confirmed against a primary source is marked **unconfirmed**. Plot references are to the chapters in [outline/act-1.md](../outline/act-1.md), [outline/act-2.md](../outline/act-2.md) and [outline/act-3.md](../outline/act-3.md) and to the act I drafts in `manuscript/`.

## Summary

The real cyber history of financial market infrastructure is a history of outages and ransomware at the edges, not of hackers breaking a clearing house from the inside: exchanges have halted because a matching engine failed over badly (London, October 2023; Tokyo, October 2020), clearing has run on paper because a software vendor was encrypted (ION, January 2023), and the world's biggest bank settled US Treasuries by courier with a USB stick (ICBC, November 2023). UK regulators have responded with operational resilience rules that force firms and infrastructures to name their important business services and prove they can recover within set tolerances, and with a sector crisis machinery (CMORG, SIMEX) that a Walbrook crisis would plug into. Inside a firm, access is narrowed and passes are revoked in minutes by routine identity and building systems, and email is filtered by surveillance "lexicons" that will catch a member code in a photograph; but a flagged message normally goes to compliance, not to the recipient's boss. Under UK law, information cannot be stolen, so "theft of confidential material" is an employer's phrase, not a charge; the realistic criminal hooks are the Computer Misuse Act and data protection law, and a whistleblower who went to the regulator first is well protected. A private investigator like Kessler can learn a great deal lawfully from open sources and physical surveillance, and the industry has a documented history of buying the rest illegally; she cannot lawfully get a phone's location from a carrier, and switching a phone off does stop live tracking by anyone but Apple. Journalists protect sources with Signal, SecureDrop and discipline, and sources are usually caught by the first unencrypted contact, by document forensics, or by police obtaining phone records, not by broken encryption.

## 1. Defending market infrastructure, and what actually goes wrong

**The rules.** The UK's operational resilience regime (Bank of England, PRA and FCA policy published March 2021) requires firms and financial market infrastructures (FMIs: CCPs, settlement systems, payment systems) to identify their "important business services", set "impact tolerances" (the maximum tolerable disruption, usually expressed in hours), map the people, systems and third parties each service depends on, and test against "severe but plausible" scenarios. Firms had to be operating within tolerances by 31 March 2025; FMIs supervised by the Bank had the same deadline. The Bank has said FMI scenarios should be extreme enough to include "a severe cyber-attack impacting multiple data centres at once". Internationally, the CPMI-IOSCO *Guidance on cyber resilience for financial market infrastructures* (June 2016) sets the famous **two-hour recovery time objective** ("2hRTO"): a CCP should be able to resume critical operations within two hours of a disruption, even a cyber-attack. A 2022 CPMI-IOSCO review of 37 FMIs found a "serious issue of concern" in that a small number could not credibly meet it. A UK CCP must notify the Bank of operational incidents promptly (a 2018 rule, in force 7 May 2018, requires written notice, in practice an email to its supervisors, with follow-up reports as the incident evolves); a new incident-reporting framework for FMIs (IOREP) was finalised in March 2026 and comes into force in March 2027.

**The testing.** CBEST is the Bank's threat-led penetration testing scheme (since 2014): accredited testers, given government threat intelligence, imitate the attackers most likely to target a specific institution. CMORG, the Cross Market Operational Resilience Group co-chaired by the Bank and UK Finance with HM Treasury and the NCSC at the table, runs the sector-wide SIMEX exercises: SIMEX 2018 was a cyber-attack scenario with 29 systemic firms and FMIs; SIMEX 22 involved 50 firms; SIMEX 24 (2 October 2024) rehearsed a total sector shutdown and restart. The sector response framework these exercise is what a real Walbrook crisis would run through: a standing bridge call between the Bank, Treasury, the FCA, the big firms and the FMIs, with a shared situation report.

**What goes wrong in practice.** Almost never a dramatic hack of the core. The real pattern:

- *Failover that does not fail over.* London Stock Exchange, 19 October 2023, 15:11: one partition of the order book halted because of a matching-engine logic fault in which "the mirror process did not take over as expected"; two more halts followed in December. Tokyo Stock Exchange, 1 October 2020: a failed disk and a mis-set switch to backup killed a whole trading day. Euronext, 19 October 2020: three hours down across five markets from a third-party middleware fault, trades after 17:30 cancelled, and LCH SA suspending buy-ins for five business days to let members catch up.
- *A vendor in the plumbing.* ION Cleared Derivatives, 31 January 2023: LockBit ransomware on a software firm most people had never heard of forced at least 42 brokers and clearing members in Europe and the US to process derivatives trades and margin by hand for days; the CFTC could not get timely position data. The closest real precedent for a clearing crisis made worse by technology.
- *Settlement by courier.* ICBC Financial Services, New York, 8 November 2023: ransomware cut the US arm of the world's largest bank off from Treasury settlement. It owed BNY Mellon about $9bn intraday, far more than its capital, and sent settlement instructions across Manhattan by messenger on a USB stick until its parent injected capital.
- *The slow, boring breach.* Capita, 31 March 2023: an outsourcer for 450 UK pension schemes detected the malicious file within ten minutes and isolated the machine 58 hours later; data on 6.6 million people, a £14m ICO fine. Travelex, New Year's Eve 2019: ransomware took the foreign-exchange provider offline for weeks and left the travel-money desks of Barclays, Lloyds and RBS dark.
- *The heist.* Bangladesh Bank, 4–5 February 2016: attackers (attributed to North Korea) sat inside the network for months, learned the SWIFT terminal, and on a Thursday night sent 35 payment orders worth $951m from the bank's account at the New York Fed. The printer that logged SWIFT confirmations had been disabled, so the duty manager found a jammed printer and "assumed it was a common problem". A misspelt beneficiary and a weekend saved most of the money; $81m went through Manila casinos and was never recovered.
- *Not an attack at all.* CrowdStrike, 19 July 2024: a faulty security update crashed Windows machines worldwide; banks and payment networks were down for a morning, and for several hours nobody on the crisis calls could say whether it was an attack.

**How a CCP handles a cyber incident on a crisis day.** The decision that matters is whether to disconnect: isolate systems, and stop margin calls, settlement and default management in the middle of a panic, or stay up and perhaps let an attacker move. The expected sequence, from NCSC guidance and the FMI rules: declare an incident, stand up a command structure separate from the technical responders, notify the Bank by email and phone, bring in the retained incident-response firm, fall back to manual procedures for the important business services, and keep the two-hour clock in view. Members are told by bulletin, as LCH SA told its members in October 2020. A scene set here is a room with two clocks: the market's and the regulator's.

## 2. Insider risk and leavers

**Narrowing access.** Firms run joiners-movers-leavers processes through an identity system (Active Directory and whatever governs application entitlements). Narrowing someone's access to "what a graduate gets" is a matter of removing him from groups; disabling sign-in and killing live sessions takes minutes once someone with authority asks. The decision is normally made by HR, legal and the line manager together with the CISO's team, and in a regulated firm it would be logged. An hour and eleven minutes from an escalation to a cut (ch. 7) is fast for a bureaucracy but trivial for the system; the speed is a plot signal, not a technical implausibility.

**Revoking a pass.** Building access runs on a separate physical access control system (Lenel, HID and the like). A revoked card reads red at the turnstile; the guard's screen shows a name and a "disabled" or "alarm" status and the standing instruction is to call security or the police. The scene in ch. 14 is exactly right.

**Monitoring email and devices.** Two different machines are in play and fiction often confuses them.

- *Data loss prevention (DLP)* is about outbound data: it inspects messages and attachments for patterns (card numbers, client identifiers, "confidential" labels, fingerprints of known documents) and can block, quarantine, encrypt or hold a message for approval. Vendor documentation describes "flag for a manager to approve before delivery" as an available action, so manager-in-the-loop review exists; it is normally for the sender's manager on outbound mail.
- *Communications surveillance* is a regulatory obligation in wholesale finance (FCA SYSC 10A: record, monitor and keep auditable all communications relating to in-scope business). Surveillance tools (Smarsh, Global Relay, NICE and others) run "lexicons", lists of words and patterns, over email, chat and recorded calls and raise alerts in a compliance queue. The FCA's August 2025 review of eleven wholesale banks describes firms updating lexicons, detecting "channel hopping" to WhatsApp, and scanning emojis and voice notes. Alerts go to compliance surveillance analysts, who escalate to the business when they think there is a case.
- *Inbound personal mail* is typically filtered for malware and phishing, and inbound attachments can be OCR-scanned (Google Workspace documents OCR of image attachments for compliance rules; Microsoft Purview has equivalents). So a photograph of a notebook page containing a member code could genuinely trip a rule. What is unusual is the routing in the draft: an inbound personal message held for the *recipient's line manager* to review. It is a configurable choice, and a firm could defend it ("the manager knows what is business"), but a compliance officer reading the chapter would raise an eyebrow. See plot implications.
- *Devices.* Corporate laptops and phones carry endpoint agents that log USB use, printing and uploads; firms can see what a user copied where. Personal phones are the blind spot, which is why the FCA cares so much about WhatsApp.

**"Theft of confidential material" in UK law.** Information is not property. *Oxford v Moss* (1979): a student who read a proof exam paper and put it back committed no theft, because confidential information is not "intangible property" under the Theft Act 1968 and the paper itself was returned. Practitioners still cite it: you can take information without stealing anything. An employer can only build a criminal complaint from:

- *Computer Misuse Act 1990*, s.1 (unauthorised access) and s.3 (unauthorised acts impairing a computer). Since *R v Bow Street Magistrates, ex parte Allison* (House of Lords, 1999), an employee who knowingly goes beyond the access the employer has defined for him commits s.1. Knowledge is required; recklessness is not enough. The offence is triable either way, so it is arrestable and premises can be searched and devices seized. Prosecutions are rare: 55,576 CMA offences were recorded in 2024/25 and 55 led to a charge; in 2020 there were 45 prosecutions in total.
- *Data Protection Act 2018*, s.170 (knowingly or recklessly obtaining or disclosing personal data without the controller's consent), the successor to the "section 55" blagging offence. Applies if the material contains personal data; a ledger of bonds and member codes mostly does not.
- *Theft Act* for the physical medium only: a company USB stick, a company notebook.
- *Breach of confidence* and contract are civil. The usual fast weapon is a High Court injunction for delivery-up of documents, obtainable within a day or two, plus a search order in extreme cases.

So "we have reported the theft of confidential material" is realistic as a phrase and a tactic; a police officer hearing it will ask whether any computer was accessed without authority or any personal data taken, and otherwise call it a civil matter. If the police act, the realistic form is a voluntary interview, or an arrest under CMA s.1, search, seizure of phone and laptop, and release under investigation. Two days' detention then release without charge (Priya, act II) needs a superintendent's extension beyond 24 hours to 36 and a magistrate's warrant beyond that: possible but heavy for this offence. Firms report through Report Fraud (which replaced Action Fraud in December 2025) or directly to the force; the City of London Police is both the national fraud lead and the local force for Walbrook. **Unconfirmed:** that a Treasury official could cause the police to detain someone on a firm's complaint; it is a favour, not a procedure, and the book should treat it as one.

**Whistleblowing.** Under the Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998 (Part IVA Employment Rights Act 1996) a worker is protected from dismissal or detriment for a "qualifying disclosure" made to the employer or to a "prescribed person". The FCA and the Bank of England/PRA are prescribed persons; the Bank reported 166 PIDA disclosures in 2021–22. Disclosure to a journalist is protected only under the harder tests: s.43G (reasonable belief the information is substantially true, not for personal gain, having first gone internal or to a regulator, or reasonably fearing concealment or reprisal) or s.43H (an "exceptionally serious" failure). Protect, the whistleblowing charity, calls media disclosure "a last resort". Jonas filed with the regulator through its own portal on Friday (ch. 7 and 14); that is the strongest possible position. Priya's threat to go to a journalist is the risky one, legally.

## 3. Tracking and surveillance by a private investigator

**The industry.** London's corporate intelligence sector is concentrated in Mayfair, Belgravia and St James's; firms are founded by former MI6, MI5, police and military intelligence officers and ex-journalists (Hakluyt, 1995; Orbis, 2009; K2, Control Risks, Kroll). Politico called it in July 2025 "lucrative, freewheeling, and largely unregulated"; UK private investigators are not licensed. The documented dark side is the trade in illegally obtained data: Operation Motorman (ICO, 2002–06) found investigator Steve Whittamore selling thousands of "blags" (DVLA records, ex-directory numbers, criminal records) to 305 journalists; SOCA's Operation Millipede (2012) produced a list of 98 corporate and legal clients of rogue investigators; the ICO prosecuted Woodgate & Clark in 2017 for impersonating bank staff to obtain mortgage details and balances. Black Cube's work for Harvey Weinstein (2016–17), using operatives with false identities to approach accusers and reporters, is the best-documented modern case of a private intelligence firm run against a private individual. Kessler's firm sits in this world; "two or three former colleagues" is the realistic size of a surveillance team.

**Lawful open sources.** In a few hours at a desk, with Jonas's name and employer:

- Companies House (directorships, service address, month and year of birth, and on older filings often the home address; suppression became available in 2024).
- HM Land Registry title register for any address, £7 online: owner, price paid, lender. A garage on its own registered title in a dead father's name is findable by searching the father's name, or through a probate search on gov.uk (grants and wills are public, £16 a copy, online about two weeks after the grant), which names the executor, Jonas, and often the asset. A council lock-up on a licence would not appear; it would be found by following him, which the outline already has them do on Friday (ch. 8).
- The open electoral register, sold to anyone; the full register, held by credit reference agencies and sold back as licensed "tracing" data; 192.com and similar aggregators.
- Social media: Laura's and the nursery's accounts, the surgery's website with staff days, running-app heat maps. The nursery name and the Monday-Tuesday-Thursday routine in ch. 12 would come from a day's physical surveillance plus Laura's digital footprint, not a database.
- Data brokers: investigations in 2024–25 (Netzpolitik, BR, Wired, Le Monde) bought samples of billions of advertising-ID location points from brokers such as Datastream Group by posing as a marketing firm and re-identified EU officials from them; German state police have bought the same data. Legally grey, cheap, and a Mayfair firm would know a reseller. **Unconfirmed** for the UK specifically.

**What a phone reveals, and to whom.** The network operator always knows roughly where a switched-on phone is (which cell, with timing information), and keeps the record. Under the Investigatory Powers Act 2016 that communications data is available to police and other public authorities through an authorisation process (since 2015, with a judge's approval where the aim is to identify a journalist's source); it is an offence for an official to obtain it without authority, and a telecoms operator will not hand it to a private firm. Private investigators have no lawful intercept or location powers at all. They can, in the real world: follow a person; place a GPS tracker on a car (no specific UK statute, but a probable data-protection and harassment offence, and the Association of British Investigators tells its members not to without consent); exploit the target's own accounts if they have a password; buy ad-tech location data; or, at the criminal end reported in the press, pay for an SS7 network lookup from a grey-market "location service" (**unconfirmed** as to UK availability; reported by Citizen Lab and others in the US and elsewhere). Apple's and Google's unknown-tracker alerts mean an AirTag planted on a person is found within hours; the trackers of choice are cheap GSM units on vehicles.

**Does switching the phone off help?** Yes, against anyone except the manufacturer. A powered-off phone deregisters from the network and goes dark to the carrier, the police and any ad-tech data; its last known location remains on record. The caveat is Apple's Find My network: an iPhone 11 or later with Find My enabled remains locatable through other people's iPhones for up to about 24 hours after a manual power-off, and only by the owner's own Apple ID. For a thriller: switching off works; taking the battery out is 2005; a Faraday pouch is what a professional would carry; and the realistic risk is not the phone being tracked while off but the pattern of where it was last on and where it comes back on (ch. 14: Jonas turns it on for three minutes to look at New York, and that is a dot on a map).

**ANPR.** About 12,700 police ANPR cameras in the UK submit around 60 million reads a day to the National ANPR Data Centre at Hendon, kept for a year; access is for police and approved law-enforcement bodies only, and Kessler cannot query it. London's congestion charge and ULEZ cameras feed Transport for London, not her. What she has is the private equivalent: car-park operators' and petrol stations' ANPR, and a tail car. If Laura drives to Norfolk, a private firm finds her by knowing the mother's address, not by cameras.

**What a scene looks like.** Two cars and a motorbike; a "trigger" sitting on the house and a "follow" that stays back; handovers at junctions; a man who is not looking at his phone (ch. 8's detail is good, and a professional would be looking at his phone precisely because everyone else is). Losing a professional team on the Underground is possible because they cannot follow on foot in numbers without being seen; it is also how they confirm he has noticed them.

## 4. Secure communication between a whistleblower and a journalist

**What good practice looks like.** The Perugia Principles (2019) and SecureDrop's own documentation describe the toolkit: Signal with disappearing messages for conversation (end-to-end encrypted, minimal metadata, but the phone number is the identity and the phone itself can be seized); SecureDrop, a Tor-based drop box run on the newspaper's own servers (The Guardian, FT, New York Times and others), so there is no third party to subpoena and little metadata to keep; air-gapped machines for reading documents, which the Guardian used for the Snowden files in 2013 (MacAskill: no phones in the room, email systems changed constantly); meeting in person, without phones, as the Snowden team did in Hong Kong after Snowden flinched at an iPhone being taken out; and physical delivery of paper, which has no metadata.

**Where it fails.** Almost never on the encryption.

- *The first contact.* Snowden's warning, relayed by Rusbridger: the first unencrypted email or call to the switchboard is enough. Jonas ringing Calloway through the paper's switchboard (ch. 17) leaves a record at the paper and on his phone bill.
- *Phone records.* "Plebgate" (2012–14): the Metropolitan Police used RIPA to obtain the call and location records of three Sun journalists and found the source without asking the reporter a question; forces obtained 82 journalists' records in three years. Since 2015 a judge must approve, but the records still exist and a judge can still say yes. In Jonas's story the state is a hunter, which makes this the live threat.
- *The document.* Reality Winner (2017) was identified within days because the leaked document had been printed and folded, carried the near-invisible yellow tracking dots many printers add, and only six people had printed it. Word files carry author names, PDFs revision histories, photographs EXIF data: a photo of a handwritten page has the phone's make and the time in it.
- *Habits.* Reporters keep contacts under real names and meet sources in cafés with the source's phone in his pocket; sources email from work addresses (ch. 2 is exactly this mistake) and reuse usernames.

**What a scene looks like.** The journalist suggests Signal and the source does not have it; the journalist asks him to leave the phone at home next time; a page shown but not handed over (ch. 17); the paper's lawyer asking where the documents came from and not being told. Handwritten notebooks are, for these purposes, the most secure medium in the book: they cannot be remotely read, searched or wiped, and their only vulnerability is a person.

## 5. Texture: a bad day in a SOC, an IT security team and the NCSC

**The security operations centre.** A SOC is a room, or increasingly a Teams channel, where analysts triage alerts from the SIEM (the system that collects logs from every server, laptop and firewall and correlates them into alerts). Tiers: tier one triages, tier two investigates, tier three hunts. A bad day begins with an alert that does not close: "we've got lateral movement", "there's a beacon from a box in finance", "who owns this host?". The language is of containment: isolate the endpoint, pull the network cable, "burn it down", reset credentials, "assume breach". The incident gets a name and a ticket; a bridge call opens and never closes; someone starts a timeline in a shared document because the regulator will ask for one. The CISO briefs the executive committee hourly; legal joins to decide whether it is a notifiable breach (72 hours to the ICO for personal data); communications drafts a holding statement; the retained incident-response firm (Mandiant, CrowdStrike, NCC, PwC) is on the call by lunchtime. The question everyone asks and nobody can answer for a day is "how long have they been in?".

**The firm's security team.** Small, overworked and used to being ignored: patching, access reviews, phishing tests, and a stack of alerts they cannot all read; Capita (detected in ten minutes, isolated after 58 hours) is their nightmare because it is ordinary. When a risk manager's access is cut they get a ticket from HR and do it in five minutes without being told why; when the pass goes red they are not involved at all. They would be the people to notice that an executive had asked for an unusual search of someone's mailbox, and they would do it, and not like it.

**The NCSC.** Part of GCHQ, at Nova South in Victoria since February 2017, with an operations floor shown to the Queen at the opening. In a national incident it runs the technical side (attribution, malware analysis, advice to firms) while the Bank runs the financial response through CMORG and the Treasury chairs the politics. NCSC people speak in understatement ("we are aware and working with partners") and distrust the word "sophisticated".

**Common mistakes in fiction.** A hacker getting in within a minute (real intrusions take weeks); "tracing" a call live with a progress bar; a private investigator "pulling phone records" (she cannot); "triangulating" a switched-off phone; encrypted messages being "cracked" (they are read on a seized phone, not cracked); an air gap crossed by magic (it is crossed by a USB stick or a person); one genius doing what is in life a team; an exchange "hacked" to move prices (real outages are failovers and vendors); everything happening on screens (the ICBC fix was a man in a taxi). The realistic version is slower, more human and more embarrassing, which is better for a novel.

| Whole book | The notebooks as the object | Handwritten paper is the most secure medium in the story and the most vulnerable to a person, which is the right irony | None; worth a line from Kessler or Calloway |
