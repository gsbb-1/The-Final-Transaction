# IDEA

## One-liner

**The Final Transaction** is a 400-page thriller set in the financial industry: action and suspense on the surface, human drama underneath.

Working logline (proposed by an agent; the author's to change): when a tanker sunk in the Strait of Hormuz sets off the collapse he has spent four years predicting, a London risk manager must decide what to do with the notebooks that map exactly where the system will break, while a hedge fund wants them to profit, a Chancellor wants them buried, and his family becomes the price.

## Premise

<!-- What happens, to whom, and why it matters. Two or three paragraphs. -->

Direction chosen on 2026-10-03; cast and act I detail proposed by an agent on 2026-10-07 and open to change. Full cast: [characters/](characters/README.md). Act I: [outline/act-1.md](outline/act-1.md).

Jonas Whitlock, turning 40, is head of collateral risk at Walbrook Clearing House in London, the institution that holds the collateral behind the bond and derivatives markets. For almost four years he has kept a private record, one handwritten entry a day, of the warning signs that the same bonds are pledged as collateral many times over, so that a sharp enough shock will force everyone to sell at once. Three years ago he put it in a memo and gave the system three years. Nobody acted. Chapter 1 is entry `#1399`.

A tanker is sunk in the Strait of Hormuz and the shock arrives on schedule. As oil spikes and bond prices fall, a Gulf sovereign fund, fearing its assets will be frozen, pulls its collateral and turns a wobble into a global panic. Jonas's record is suddenly the most valuable document in London: to Sebastian Crane, a hedge fund manager positioned for the collapse, it is a map of where to push; to Caroline Strand, a Chancellor five weeks from an election whose Treasury filed the memo, it is proof she was warned. Crane sends a private investigator for the notebooks. Strand wants Jonas's signature on a statement that all is sound.

By the time markets open on Monday, Jonas has refused to sign, his only ally has vanished, his wife and daughter are in hiding, and he is carrying four years of notebooks with nowhere to go.

What follows (outlined in [outline/act-2.md](outline/act-2.md) and [outline/act-3.md](outline/act-3.md), as proposals): Jonas and Priya turn the record into a map of the twelve institutions that will fail and the order they fail in, and discover that Crane's trades were placed before the sinking. The sinking was arranged by a faction in Al-Mansouri's own country, and Crane knew. In the same hour that Al-Mansouri becomes Jonas's ally, Crane's investigator takes the notebooks. Crane uses the map to attack; Walbrook announces it may not open; Strand refuses to act. Jonas, who has spent four years being right in private, goes on live television with everything, and forces the one transaction that can stop the collapse: the Gulf fund buying the failing collateral in the exact order the map dictates, which breaks Crane's fund in an hour. Three months later Jonas writes entry #1500 and closes the record.

## What the genre demands

- **Thriller:** a clear threat, a ticking clock, and stakes that keep rising.
- **Action:** physical danger and pursuit, not only danger on a screen or a balance sheet.
- **Suspense:** the reader knows enough to fear what is coming; reveals and reversals are paced across the whole book.
- **Drama:** characters with something personal to lose, whose relationships change because of the plot.
- **Financial setting:** the money mechanics drive the plot and feel authentic, but a reader with no finance background can always follow what is at stake.

## Audience

Proposed by an agent, 2026-10-09; the author's to change.

Adult readers of commercial thrillers who like a story built on how a system actually works and told by someone inside it: the readers of Robert Harris and the later le Carré, and the readers of Michael Lewis who would take the same material as fiction. It does not assume any knowledge of finance; everything the reader needs is explained by a character who needs it explained.

Comparable titles, for positioning rather than imitation:

| Title | Why it is comparable |
| --- | --- |
| *The Fear Index*, Robert Harris (2011) | A financial thriller for a general reader, London and Geneva, a system that gets away from the people who built it |
| *A Week in December*, Sebastian Faulks (2009) | London finance as the engine of a plot about ordinary lives, a hedge fund manager betting on a bank's failure |
| *Margin Call* (film, 2011) | One institution, one night, the people who can see the collapse coming and the ones who decide what to do about it |
| *The Big Short* and *Too Big to Fail* (non-fiction, Michael Lewis 2010 and Andrew Ross Sorkin 2009) | The appetite this book is written for: readers who want the mechanics and the people in the room, but as a novel with a protagonist and a clock |

What distinguishes it: the narrator is not a trader or a banker but the man whose job is to check the deposits; the device of the daily record; and a global panic shown through one clearing house, one family, and the one week in which being right stops being enough.
