# Chapter 14: The open

Monday. Written in sittings, at a café table and then a bench, not a desk. First time in 1,405 entries. The handwriting will show it.

I did not sign.

That is the whole of the decision and I want it on the page before anything else, because by tonight there will be other versions of it. I did not sign, I did not reply, I did not hand anything to anyone for safekeeping. The phone rang 23 times between midnight and six. Treasury switchboard, nine. Graham, six. Haldane's office, four. Four withheld. None from Norfolk.

The night bus put me down at London Bridge around midnight. I am not writing where I spent the hours until 5.41, except that I spent them awake, with the rucksack between my feet. I had the rucksack on both shoulders, the way you carry a child's bag, and I walked up through the City in the dark because the City in the dark is the one place a man with a rucksack looks like he belongs. Cleaners. Night staff going home. Nobody looked.

At 6.30 the statement went out. I read it on the phone, standing outside a shuttered sandwich shop. Walbrook Clearing House confirms that its collateral arrangements are fully sound and adequate to all foreseeable conditions. Richard Haldane's name at the bottom. Not mine. They had wanted the head of collateral risk to say it and they had settled for the chief executive, which tells you what they thought the head of collateral risk would be worth by lunchtime.

At 6.52 I tried the staff door.

I don't know why. Habit. Four years and 11 months of walking in that door. I wanted to see whether the number on the screens matched the number Priya gave me yesterday, and the only screens that would show it were on the third floor. I put the pass to the reader and the light went red. I tried it again. Red. The guard behind the glass looked at his screen, then at me, and reached for his phone, and I was back on the pavement before he had finished dialling.

So that is how I learned. Not a letter. A light.

***

The café opposite opens at seven and has a television above the counter. I took the table under it, back to the wall, rucksack between my feet, and ordered a coffee I did not drink.

7.04. The ticker along the bottom of the screen: Walbrook issues record margin call.

For anyone who finds this and does not know what that means. Every night Walbrook works out how much more each of its members must put up to cover what they owe. The result is on our screens from about seven, it reaches the members' banks by eight as a direct debit, and it is paid by nine or the member is in default. Usually it is a sum nobody outside the building hears about. This morning the Gulf fund's banks were closing it out from the open, and London had known since Saturday night that they would, so the bonds everyone else had posted to us were worth less than on Friday before a trade had printed. And if I am right about what I have been writing in these books since entry #212, those bonds were already doing more than one job each. If. I have never been able to drop that word. So the call was the largest in Walbrook's history. The ticker did not give the number. I did not need it. It is in yesterday's entry, in my handwriting, as Priya gave it to me down the phone. I am not writing it twice.

7.20. Breakfast television. The Chancellor from the Treasury courtyard, coat buttoned, no notes.

She was good. I want to record that. 'The system is sound. Walbrook confirmed this morning that its collateral arrangements are fully sound, and the Bank of England stands ready to provide liquidity as required. I would caution your viewers against giving any weight to irresponsible claims from a disaffected individual.' The interviewer asked which individual. She said she would not dignify it.

Disaffected. Four syllables. I said them under my breath and the woman at the next table moved her bag.

I have made no claims. I have made one disclosure, on Friday, to the Bank of England, through the route the Bank provides for exactly that. If that is a claim, I was irresponsible to use the right form. Unless she meant Priya. Priya said she would send the number to the Bank and to a journalist, and then she stopped answering her phone, and somewhere between those two facts there may be a newspaper that has it. I do not know. That is the point. I am sitting in a café learning what is happening to me from a ticker.

7.58. The ticker again. A clearing member tells Walbrook it will not pay, and Walbrook declares it in default an hour ahead of the deadline, on its own word.

I knew the name. Everyone in the building would know it. One of the five largest. I am not writing it down because by the time anyone reads this it will be in every paper, and because I do not want it in my hand.

Then the thing I have described a hundred times happened on a screen above a coffee machine. A member fails to pay. Walbrook declares the default, takes over the member's book and starts hedging it: selling what that book needs sold so the hole stops growing. The auction of what is left comes days later, and the market does not wait for it. It knows which way Walbrook has to sell and it sells first. The price goes down, so every other member's collateral is worth less, so Walbrook calls them again, intraday, and by nine there is a second call on top of the first, and the first is not yet paid.

The pound was down about 5% against the dollar by eight. Gilt futures kept hitting their limits: a minute's hold, a wider band, another hold, so that the screen went grey every few minutes and came back lower. By half past eight the three biggest banks' shares had been thrown into auction so many times that the ticker had stopped naming them and started counting them.

I watched it the way you watch weather you forecast. No satisfaction in it. I had wanted to be wrong. I wrote that in entry #1 and I have not changed my mind.

***

Eight. I had said eight. I had the phone in my hand at 7.58 when the ticker said a member had failed, and I watched that instead, for 17 minutes, because it was the thing I have waited four years to see and because a man who counts everything can still fail to count the one thing he promised. I know what Laura said she would do at eight. I am writing it down so that I cannot pretend later that I forgot.

8.15. Graham.

A text, not a call. He has never texted me in five years. "Walbrook has reported theft of confidential material to the police. They want to speak to you. Don't come in. I'm sorry. G."

Theft. The books are mine. Bought with my money, written in my hand, in my own time, kept on my father's property. There is not one line in them copied from a Walbrook system. There are numbers I knew because of my job, and that is what they will say. They will say it was the firm's confidential material and I took it off the premises, and they will say it this morning, loudly, because by lunchtime the question everyone is asking will be why Walbrook did nothing and the answer they want on the record is that a man stole the evidence.

I'm sorry. G. Graham has been sorry for three years. He was sorry when he filed the memo.

8.31. Thirty-one minutes late. I rang Laura. It rang out. I rang again and got her voice telling me to leave a message. I did not leave one, because I did not know who would hear it. I rang her mother's landline. Margaret's phone rang 14 times. Nobody picked up. It was half past eight on a Monday in a village near Norwich and there was nobody in the house.

I rang Priya. Not voicemail. The number you have dialled is not available. Priya's number was available at 9.52 yesterday morning when she called me and read me the figure and told me what she thought of me. It has been not available for 22 hours.

Then I did what I should have done at midnight, which is think about the phone. The woman outside Priya's building knew the route Laura walks to the surgery and the name of the nursery. Her people found the lock-up. I have told myself since Friday that the garage is in my father's name and on no form of mine. It is on the one form that matters: his probate, which I applied for, which names me as executor and names the garage, and which anyone with his surname and sixteen pounds can read. That is what she can do: read. I do not know what else she can do. But it is not only her now. Graham's text says the police want me, and the police do not need to guess where a phone is. They ask, and the network tells them. I turned it off, wrapped it in a paper napkin from the counter, and put it in the inside pocket of the rucksack with the notebooks. Then I paid for the coffee and left, because the police had my name and the building was 200 yards away.

***

I will not write down how I got here. I have learned something about writing things down.

Here is a bench on a hill. From it I can see the towers where the money is, and I have been watching them all day for signs, which is stupid, because a bank in default looks exactly like a bank in credit from four miles off. The sun is on the glass. Office lights on. Lifts going up and down. Somewhere inside, screens that would tell me the number, and I cannot see one of them.

People have passed the bench all day. I counted 60 before I stopped. Dog walkers, a school party, two runners, a man in a suit at 11 in the morning with his tie in his pocket, not looking at anything, who may have been the first of them or may just have been a man. Nobody has looked at the rucksack. Nobody knows that it is Monday for them yet. It will be Monday for them tomorrow, when the cash machines are slow.

Nine notebooks. I counted them twice on the bus. Black, A5, in order, the ninth with about 35 blank pages left. Three years, ten months and five days of evenings. Laura has asked four times in four years what was in them, and I said work, and I have been thinking all day about whether that was a lie. It was the truth. That was the problem with it.

Her client. I keep writing that. She never gave a name and I have not got one for her, so her client is what he is. I know three things about him. He wanted the books badly enough to pay, which means he did not have the map on Sunday. He wanted them before the open, which means he wanted to know where to push. And whatever he holds, he is short, because nothing else about his week makes sense. Every tick down on that screen in the café was a payment to him. He has been paid every minute since seven this morning, and he is being paid now, as I write, and he still does not have the map. That is the one thing I own that he does not.

Elsie. I don't know if you will read this. If you do, this is the day it came true, and I was on a bench.

2.35pm. I turned the phone on for three minutes, by my watch, to see New York.

New York opened, fell 7% and stopped for a quarter of an hour, as it is built to do. The ticker said so and then I did not read the ticker any more, because the phone had 41 missed calls, and one message, from a number I did not know, timed 11.02.

"Mr Whitlock. The offer stands until the London close. It is smaller now, for reasons you will understand better than most. After the close it is not an offer. D.K."

I turned the phone off.

She is right that I understand it. The map is worth less every hour the thing it maps is burning. By Friday it will be a history book. She knows that, and so does her client, so I have until 4.30 to be worth paying for and after that I am only worth stopping.

Nine notebooks. Four years. Ninety-four minutes.

I have started reading them from the beginning.
