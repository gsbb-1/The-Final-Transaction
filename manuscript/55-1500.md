# Chapter 55: #1500

Friday, the eighth of January. This is entry 1,500, and I have been looking at the number for ten minutes, because it is round, and I notice such things, and nobody else does.

Seventy-nine days since the six o'clock. I have not counted anything else. I have not counted my age since the autumn; it is on the form I sign at the Bank every month and I copy it without looking. I counted the years once, in a tidy block, as if a life were a quarterly report with the footnotes left off, and I was proud of the footnotes. I am not going to do that tonight. I will say only that the dots at the end of #1399 are still there, and I have decided to leave them.

I took the tenth down from the dresser, where it has lain since last night for anyone to pick up, and Laura is across the kitchen with her back to me. She has not asked what I am writing. She has never read a page and I think she will not read this one, and I find I would not mind if she did, which is new and which I leave here for the record.

***

On the last night of my thirties I wrote four lines and I had a phrase in my head for them, and I did not write it down, because it embarrassed me. The phrase was *life lesson*. In October, at Calloway's table, with a borrowed pad and a clearing house due to fail by morning, I wrote one: *Being right is worth nothing if you only write it down.* It was true, and it was half.

This is the other half.

**Nothing is true until somebody else has been able to check it.**

The first half is the one Laura gave me. She sat at that table in her coat and said, tell me where it is, the way you would tell Priya, and it was the first time anything of mine left the page. The second half is the one Priya gave me, on the morning of the twenty-first, from a hospital bed, correcting me twice in front of a deputy governor. I said *I think* in my throat for four years, and I called it caution. It was a man making sure no one could ever say *no, you're wrong*. Careful is a thing you do with someone. Alone, it is another word.

I gave an order in a room at Walbrook with a woman on a telephone correcting me, and each time I was glad of her, in a way I would have found unbearable in September. She was right and I was wrong, aloud, and the market turned. That is all the transaction was, at the end: a number said out loud, and a person who could say no to it. I have spent eleven weeks looking for something larger and there is not anything.

***

I have never signed an entry. I used to say a signature was a claim, and that a record was only a record if nobody had claimed it. For 1,499 entries it was a good rule. I was wrong about that as well. A record that nobody has claimed is a diary; a claim is what you hand to someone so that they can check it.

This one is written to be checked. Every figure in it is somewhere else as well: in a statement in Holborn, in the Bank's log of that morning, in a red notebook on a sofa in Bethnal Green. Anyone may look, and if I am wrong they may say so, and I will be glad. That is what a name at the foot of a page is for. So.

***

Elsie.

Your first word was on the twenty-third of December, at twenty to seven in the morning. It was not the one I had put money on, though I have not put money on anything since the autumn. It was not Dad. It was not Da; we agreed in the autumn not to argue about that for a year, and there are nine months of the year left, and I am not going to spend them on it.

You were on my hip. The kitchen window had gone grey and the glass was white at the corners. You took your whole arm out of my coat sleeve, like a signpost, and you pointed it at the garden. Not at me. At the shed roof, where nothing had happened, an ordinary frost, the kind I have seen for forty winters and never once been asked to look at.

Then you turned your head and checked that I had.

I had. I looked. You were right. It was exactly as you said.

Look.

Jonas Whitlock
