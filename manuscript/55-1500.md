# Chapter 55: #1500

Friday, the last day of the year. The last night of it, nearly. This is entry 1,500, and I have been looking at the number for ten minutes, because it is round, and I notice such things, and nobody else does.

Seventy-nine days since the six o'clock. I have not counted anything else. I have not counted my age since October; it is on the form I sign at the Bank every month and I copy it without looking. I counted the years once, in a tidy block, as if a life were a quarterly report with the footnotes left off, and I was proud of the footnotes. I am not going to do that tonight. I will say only that the dots at the end of the first of these are still there, and I have decided to leave them.

Laura is across the kitchen with her back to me. She has not asked what I am writing. She has never read a page and I think she will not read this one, and I find I would not mind if she did, which is new and which I leave here for the record.

***

On the last night of my thirties I wrote four lines and I had a phrase in my head for them, and I did not write it down, because it embarrassed me. The phrase was *life lesson*. I have one now. I have not written it in any of the nine books, because I did not have it, and I did not have it because I was not in a position to be told.

It is this.

**Nothing is true until somebody else has been able to check it.**

That is all of it. Not *being right*. I have been right, I was right for four years, and it was the most private thing about me. I said *I think* in my throat for four years, and it was not caution. It was a man making sure no one could ever say *no, you're wrong*. Careful is a thing you do with someone. Alone, it is another word.

I gave an order in a room in St James's with a woman I trust correcting me twice, and each time I was glad of her, in a way I would have found unbearable in September. She was right and I was wrong, aloud, in front of a deputy governor, and the market turned. That is all the transaction was, at the end: a number said out loud, and a person who could say no to it. I have spent eleven weeks looking for something larger and there is not anything.

***

The rest, quickly. It is the last page, and I have never been able to leave a ledger without totting it up.

The nine are in the toolbox in Lewisham, or eight of them are; the ninth I finished in the hall in December, standing up, and it is with the others. I was offered a sum I will not write. I said that the books were full of people who had not agreed. I go in to the Bank three days a week, to a room with Priya in it, who tells me when I am wrong. I count that as the best pay I have had.

I have signed my name fifty-odd times since October: a statement, a bail form, a contract, thirty-one pages in a room in Holborn. I have never signed one of these. I used to say a signature was a claim, and that a record was only a record if nobody had claimed it. I was wrong about that as well. A record that nobody has claimed is a diary. So.

The tenth is on the table. It has forty pages left and I do not think I shall use them. There are five more in the desk, in their cellophane, and Laura has counted them.

***

Elsie.

Your first word was on the twenty-third, at twenty to seven in the morning. It was not the one I had put money on, though I have not put money on anything since October. It was not Dad. It was not Da; we agreed in October not to argue about that for a year, and there are nine months of the year left, and I am not going to spend them on it.

You were on my hip. The kitchen window had gone grey and the glass was white at the corners. You took your whole arm out of my coat sleeve, like a signpost, and you pointed it at the garden. Not at me. At the shed roof, where nothing had happened, an ordinary frost, the kind I have seen for forty winters and never once been asked to look at.

Then you turned your head and checked that I had.

I had. I looked. You were right. It was exactly as you said.

Look.

Jonas Whitlock
