# Chapter 30: In his head

Wednesday. #1414. A reporter's pad of Calloway's, spiral at the top, lined, the first eleven pages torn out by him and the twelfth by me because it had a phone number on it. I have never written an entry on anything but a black A5 book with an elastic band, and I am going to write that once and not again: from here on I write on what there is.

This is the worst entry I have written. I have been sitting with that sentence for some time, in the hope of a better one, and there is not one. I am writing it so that it is at the top.

***

The four hotel sheets are on the table beside the pad. I wrote them last night in the lobby and on the bus and at this table, and they are #1413, and they are the whole of the record that exists in my hand. Four sheets. Nine books.

I did not sleep. I sat here from a quarter to eleven, when I got back, with the sheets squared in front of me, and at some point Calloway came in and looked at me and at the empty floor by the chair where the bag goes, and did not ask, and went to bed, which is the kindest thing a journalist has ever done to me. Priya was asleep on the sofa. The flat has one bedroom and a sofa and a kitchen, and I have been given the kitchen.

She found me at ten past six. She came in for water and stopped in the doorway and looked where Calloway had looked, and said, 'Where is it.'

'Gone.'

'Gone where.'

'The cloakroom at the hotel. They wouldn't let it upstairs. I had a ticket. Somebody else had the same ticket at twelve minutes past eight.'

She sat down across from me. She did not say anything for a while. Then she said, 'All nine.'

'All nine. And the page.'

'The page was in the ninth.'

'Yes.'

She put her hands flat on the table the way Laura does, and I understood that I have been living for a week among women who do that before they say something.

'Jonas. I am going to say one thing about this and then I am never going to say it again, because we haven't got time. You carried them on both shoulders for nine days and the first time somebody made you put them down you put them down.'

'Yes.'

'Right.' She got up and put the kettle on. 'That's said.'

***

Nine o'clock. The box on the Kentish Town Road. Laura on the first ring. I told her. Not all of it: that I had met a man who could help, that the meeting had gone well, and that the books had been taken while I was in it, all nine, by the people who had been to her mother's door.

She was quiet for a count of three.

'Are you hurt?'

'No.'

'Then it's a bag, Jonas.' And then, because she is Laura: 'You've got five days.'

Four minutes. I have begun to think of the four minutes as the most honest part of the day.

***

Who has them. I have had the night to do that sum and it comes out the same each time.

Not the police. The police do not forge cloakroom tickets; they stand at the counter and show a card. Not Al-Mansouri's people; they would not let the bag up, and that is the opposite of wanting it. A ticket with a 63 on it, made in the hours between my handing in the bag and a man in a good coat collecting it, is a piece of work, and I have met exactly one person this fortnight who does pieces of work. She was in that lobby. I did not see her. I looked at every face in it and I did not see her, and that is what she is for.

So by now, nine on a Wednesday, a man in Mayfair whose name I first wrote down six days ago has nine books on his desk. He has the twelve. He has the order. He has what each one is holding, in my hand, under the word *think*, which he will not read as a caveat because men like him do not have caveats. He has the date in Priya's box, which he already knew, because it is his date. And he has four years of me.

I have been trying all night to decide which of those is worst and I keep arriving at the same one, and it is not the map. It is that he can now read what I did about it. Nothing. He will see the entry where I was relieved. I know he will, because it is the one I would go to.

What he does next I can also do as a sum, and I would rather not, and I will. He does not need twelve. He needs the next three. He knows which lines they hold. He knows what days they pay late. If I were short the pound and the gilt and wanted the clearing house to break rather than bend, I would go for two, three and four, together, on a morning when they are already stretched, and I would make their exact collateral fall faster than everyone else's. I know how I would do that. So does he. The difference is that he will.

***

Priya came back from the kettle with two mugs and the red notebook, and opened it at the page.

Her copy. Twelve codes down the left, in her hand, and a number beside each one. Nothing else. No column. No lines of gilts, no amounts, no "think". On Friday afternoon, at ten to five, with the hire car waiting, she had copied the codes and the order and said the rest stays in the books.

'That's what I've got,' she said.

'That's a list.'

'Yes.'

'A list of twelve four-letter codes that mean nothing outside the building, in the order I think they fall over.' I said it before she could, which was a mistake, because she had been going to say it more precisely.

'Jonas. If I walk into the Bank of England with this page, what is it?'

'Nothing.'

'It's a page with twelve words on it. The holdings were the evidence. The dates were the evidence. Four years of a code standing next to a fingerprint was the evidence. A central bank needs to be told what each of these twelve is holding and how much of it is promised twice and on what day you saw it, with the entry number, and then it needs to be able to check, and we can't give it one of those things. We can give it twelve words and your face.'

'Yes.'

'Good. Now the other thing.'

She turned the red book round so that the list faced me, and put her finger on the first code, and said: 'What's it holding?'

I looked at her.

'You wrote it. Four years. You read them every night for a week on a bench and in a hotel and at this table, from the first page. You read that column on the train on Monday for the ninth or tenth time; you said so. It's not in Mayfair, Jonas. It's in your head. It was always in your head. The books were where you put it so that you wouldn't have to carry it. Well.' She tapped the code. 'Carry it.'

***

So we started. 6.40 by the cooker clock.

The first one is easy, because it has failed and because I have written about it more than any other: three lines of long gilts, and the amounts, and the pattern over four years, and the Friday it paid us with cash it raised that morning by lending out the very bonds Priya had seen failing the day before. I gave it to her in a minute and a half and she wrote it down and checked it against her own figures for Friday's seven of forty, and it held.

The second is the one that is standing because the Bank is lending to it. I know its lines. I know roughly what it posts, because I have looked at it every morning for four years and it is large, and large is easy to remember. What I do not know, to the number, is how much of it I believe is working another job at the same time, because that is not a number I ever carried in my head; it is a number I built, every quarter, from a stack of entries, and wrote in the column, and then let go of, because it was written down.

I got to a figure. Priya looked at it and looked at her Friday reconstruction and said, 'Within ten per cent,' and wrote it with a question mark, and I had to let her.

The third took forty minutes. The fourth took an hour and I changed my mind twice and the second time she put the pen down and said, 'Stop guessing. Tell me what you saw. Not the number. What you saw.' So I told her what I saw: a Tuesday in the fifth book, a substitution at a time of day when nobody substitutes anything, and the same line coming home through two members in the same week in the eighth, and she built the number herself out of what I saw, which is what she does, and it was better than mine.

By two in the afternoon we had four of twelve that I would put my name to, two with question marks, and six that were a code and a rank and my sense of them, which is not evidence.

It is slow. I want that written down, because for four years I have thought of the record as a thing I had, and it turns out I had the pages. What I have is the shape. I can tell you that the seventh name was always late on the same mornings as the second, and that there is a line it posts that I have seen going out in the morning and coming back at three more times than I can count. I cannot tell you how many times, or the dates. A man in a room in Mayfair can. He has the dates.

Priya said, at about three, with her head on her hand: 'We can get to twelve like this. By Friday, maybe. And it will be a page I'd argue for and you'd argue for and nobody at the Bank would move a pound on, because it's two people's memory of a notebook they haven't got.'

'What would move them?'

'Our own data. Walbrook's. The member collateral reports, the fails lists, the concentration numbers, with the firm's name at the top, matching what you remember. Then it stops being your memory and starts being their records.' She rubbed her eyes. 'Which are in a building I'm bailed not to go near, on systems you've been cut off from, under a man who's spent the week saying the memo went to a committee that never met.'

'Yes.'

'So we need somebody inside. Or somebody who was.'

I did not say the name. Nor did she. I have written his name in a different hand in nine books and he signed my birthday card *many of them*, and I am not writing it tonight.

***

Calloway came back at half past five with the evening's paper under his arm and his face already arranged. The second member, the one everyone has on their debit cards, had met the morning call at 8.55. The pound had done something he described with a word I will not write. The Chancellor had been "in discussions with the Bank". The auction of the first member's book was into its second day and the prices, he said, were the kind that get described as "in line with expectations" because nobody wants to say what the expectations were.

He asked, carefully, what we had.

'Four,' Priya said. 'Two more with a question mark. By Friday, twelve, if he doesn't fall asleep.'

'And the Bank?'

'Twelve words and his face.'

He did not laugh. He sat on the arm of the sofa, where he sits, and said: 'The thing in the lobby. The ticket. Is that a story?'

'Not yet,' I said. He looked at Priya. She said, 'Not yet,' and he nodded as if he had expected to lose that twice.

***

7.52. Phone two, on the table by the pad, face up, on. The long number with the code I know.

Not him. Nasser. The voice of a man who had decided about me on a carpet and had not revised it.

'Mr Whitlock. Mr Al-Mansouri asked me to tell you himself but he is in a car. The Bank has agreed to meet. Tomorrow. Thursday, in the evening; you will be told the hour, the door and who will bring you in. He asks me to say that he does not expect to be in London many more days and that this may be the only occasion.' A pause that was not a pause; he was reading the next line. 'He asks that you bring the page.'

I said nothing for long enough that he said my name again.

'I'll be there,' I said.

'And the page, Mr Whitlock.'

'Yes.'

He rang off. 7.54.

Priya had heard it. Everyone in this kitchen heard it. She looked at the red book with its twelve words and the pad with four of them filled in, and she said, 'Well. You've got until tomorrow evening to have a head worth taking into the Bank of England.'

Elsie. On Thursday your father is going to walk into the Bank of England with a stranger's page, four names he can swear to, eight he can't, and a sentence he has written 1,414 times. *I cannot prove it.* He has never been so sure of it, and it has never been so true.
