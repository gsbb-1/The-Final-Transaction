# Chapter 44: The signature

Simon Carrow rang at 5.41, and said only that the Governor's office had rung his, and that she should have a screen in front of her at six. He did not say which. She had been awake since four, in the kitchen of the flat above Number 11, and she put the laptop beside the cold tea and turned the sound down to a level at which she could hear herself think.

At one minute to six the picture was a chair. At six it was the man.

She had last seen him across a table on Horse Guards Road, in a coat mended at the knee, with his hands flat on the wood. He was in a white shirt now that did not fit, under a lamp, with a blue cloth behind him, and he looked like a witness who has been told that the court is not sitting and has decided to give his evidence anyway. A voice below the lens asked him who he was. He told it. He said that the police were looking for him. He said it as a man says what he has rehearsed so often that the words have stopped being a performance, and she noted, as a professional matter, that this was the most persuasive form of testimony there was, and that nobody could teach it.

She did not take notes. She had never in her life listened to evidence without taking notes. The pen was in her hand, and for the first four minutes the pad stayed blank.

He explained the clearing house. He explained it badly, by an advocate's standards: too plainly, every sentence qualified. *I think. I could be wrong.* She waited for a hedge she could use, and he hedged only before she could get there. He laid the weakness of his case on the table himself, and by the third minute she had stopped reading him for the sentence to pull at. She was reading him for the sentence that would be quoted.

Then he read twelve names, off a page, slowly. She did write those.

At 6.07 the phone rang beside her hand and the screen said *PM*. She answered it with her eyes on the laptop.

'You're watching.' He was in the study at Number 10; she could hear the clock. 'I've had the Governor, the Cabinet Secretary and my press secretary, in that order, and told all three that I would speak to you first. The channels took the feed at three minutes past.'

'Prime Minister.'

'I'm going to tell you what I'll say, Caroline. If you sign it, I'll say that the Chancellor took a difficult decision in difficult circumstances. If you don't sign it, I'll say that the Chancellor took a difficult decision in difficult circumstances. It's a remarkably flexible sentence.'

'It is.'

'I am not going to tell you to. I've thought about it for twenty minutes, and I'm not going to.' A pause. 'You know why.'

'Because then it would be yours.'

'Because then it would be mine,' he agreed, mildly. 'I'd like to go on being able to say that it isn't. Seven o'clock. The Governor says the exchanges can be asked until about then.'

On the screen the man in the white shirt had turned to the lens. She saw his mouth form the sentence before the sound reached her, and it was one she had heard on Sunday from the other side of a table.

'*Chancellor. You asked me for a signature once. I am not going to ask you for yours. It is a page, and it is in your building, and it is one page long.*'

'Did you hear that?' the prime minister said.

'I heard it.'

'Good.' He rang off.

She watched to the end. He said that he had never been sure, that he had been right twice in public and thought he was right a third time, and that this was all he had ever had. In twenty-six years she had not once heard a witness volunteer the limit of his own case and gain by it. Then the red light went out and the chair was a chair, and a woman at a news desk put a hand to her ear.

Eleven minutes. She looked at the pad. It said *twelve*, underlined, and nothing else.

***

She walked to the Treasury, because three hundred yards by car would be photographed. It was not raining. She carried the umbrella anyway.

Carrow was in her room with the lights on and the blinds half up on the park, and a folder on the desk with a single sheet in it. At 6.43, he said, the Metropolitan Police had told the press office, as a courtesy, that a man had been arrested in the lobby of the newspaper's offices on suspicion of offences connected with the complaint of Walbrook, and that an envelope in his possession had been taken as evidence. He did not look up from the folder.

'That is the page.'

'It was drafted on Sunday night, Chancellor, after the meeting. I thought it prudent to have it.' The word sat between them. 'I have not changed it.'

One page. The crest, the Governor's name, and four paragraphs she could have written in her sleep, having read versions of them for eight days: that the Treasury, in exercise of its powers, <!-- VERIFY: the legal basis for a Treasury indemnity to the Bank of England; the 2022 gilt operation was indemnified by letter from the Chancellor, and the Financial Services Act 2012 s.61 route (public funds notification, then a direction) is the alternative; left generic here --> would indemnify the Bank against loss on any lending or purchase made in connection with the stabilisation of the sterling collateral markets, as notified in writing by the Governor before noon that day. There was a space for a name.

She sat down. She did what she had done for twenty years with a document she did not wish to sign, which was to draw the two defences up side by side, as if for a client who had asked what he could say.

If she signed. A Chancellor, in extremis, on the advice of the Governor, with no other buyer, had indemnified a lending operation to stop a clearing house failing on the morning the failure was forecast on air, by name, and had done it within the hour. The buyer was a sovereign fund, supervised, transparent; the Bank would hold the bonds against cash. The Opposition would call it a bailout with a foreign flag on it, which would be true. On Sunday she had not been able to write the second answer to the first question. She tried now, and she could, and it was not a good answer, and it would stand.

If she did not. She had the answer to that already, because she had given it on Sunday: *prudence*, in the minutes, in Simon's hand, at her own request. A man had named twelve institutions in order on every channel in the country, and two had fallen in the order he named, and on Sunday she had said across a table that the order could not be verified. The question that would come, from a committee or from a barrister in an inquiry in a room with a tape running, was the one she put to witnesses: *Chancellor, on the morning you watched this man on television, what was your reason for not signing?* She sat with it, in those words, and went looking. The election was not a reason she could give. The Gulf state was not, because he had answered that on air by not naming it. What was left was the sentence on page four of the memo, with the date and her initials beside it, which said what she had told the country she had not seen.

She had no answer. She had spent a quarter of a century learning to tell a weak case from a case that cannot be put, and this was the second. Signing was dangerous, and she would be punished for it. Not signing was indefensible, and a lawyer does not choose the indefensible, not for a client she likes, not when the client is herself.

She took the pen from the holder, not the Chancellor's pen with the crest that she used for photographs. She wrote *C. Strand*, and the date, and the time, 7.02, and found that her hand was perfectly steady, which in a witness she would have thought interesting.

'The Governor,' she said, and Carrow, who had already dialled, held out the telephone.

'Chancellor.'

'It is signed. You will have it in four minutes. I would be obliged if the Bank would let the exchanges know that the Treasury stands behind the Bank.'

The Governor had a gift for knowing when not to speak. He said, 'Thank you,' and nothing else.

***

Carrow had reached the door with the page in a clear folder.

'Simon. The first letter.'

'I thought you might raise it.' He turned back. 'There is a draft in the folder, under the indemnity.'

One paragraph on Treasury paper, to the commander who had received the first, in the same plain language. That the Treasury had reviewed its letter of the date in question. That it no longer wished to draw the police's attention to Mr Whitlock's conduct. That the Bank had asked that he be available to assist it that morning and the Treasury had no objection. That all questions of investigation and charge were for the Commissioner and the Crown Prosecution Service. It did not apologise and it did not say that anyone had been wrong. It said that the Treasury's concern was withdrawn.

'It is correct,' she said. 'The Treasury withdraws. The police decide.'

'Yes, Chancellor.'

'And the official writes it.'

He stood very still. 'I wrote the first,' he said. 'I think it should be I who withdraws it.' He did not make it a plea. She had hired him for the way he did not.

She understood what he was offering: the whole of the exposure, in his own name, as a good official offers it. She had accepted it once already, behind a shut door, by saying that he should do whatever he judged appropriate. She took a sheet from the drawer and wrote on it, in the small hand that had filled a hundred blue notebooks, *Chancellor directed. CS. 7.14.* and put it on top of the draft.

'That goes on the file. Attached. In my hand.'

'It will be requested, Chancellor.'

'I should hope so.'

He looked at the sheet for longer than he needed to, and then at her, and for the first time in three years she saw something in his face that was not care. He took the folder and went.

***

She wrote the other letter at once, before she could improve it.

*Dear Prime Minister.* Then the reason, drafted as she would have drafted a withdrawal from a case. A lawyer who finds she is a witness in the matter she is conducting may not go on acting. She had read a document she had told the country she had not seen. She had declined to sign a thing and had signed it three days later. There was a note in her own hand in the margin of a Treasury file that anyone with a reference could ask for, and when it was asked for she would not be able to answer as Chancellor, because the answer was the margin. It would be better, for the country and the party and the department, that the person who answered was someone else.

Four paragraphs. She asked for nothing. She did not mention the election, except the date. She would not make the country change its Chancellor in the middle of choosing one, and she saw that this too was a lawyer's reason, and that she was glad of it, and that it was not the only one.

She read it back once. It was good. It was what she would have advised. She folded it in three and put it in the red box, under the cover note on the memo, and shut the lid, and sat for a moment with her hand on it.

Then she took the pen, opened the lid again, took the letter out and wrote the date at the top. The Friday after polling day, in full, with the year. A week ago she would have thought she could not do it.

The hand was perfectly steady. She noticed that, and it did not seem interesting any more.
