# Chapter 38: Tuesday

Tuesday. Week four. Entry #1420, on the back of Calloway's printer paper, in sittings, because the day did not allow for anything else. First sitting 10.20am, his kitchen table, the television on in the next room with the sound off.

Monday first, since Monday did not get an entry. It is the first day in 1,419 that did not, and the reason is that I could not lift the pen. I number days, not pages, so today is 1,420 and Monday stays empty, and the gap is the entry. Four lines. The week Laura gave me ran out at nine on Monday morning; I know the time because I was standing in a phone box on the Kentish Town Road when it did. This morning at nine, from the same box, I got her mother again, and a message read off paper, and rang off. The auction of the first member's book closed, the loss was a number the papers printed and I will not, and Walbrook asked the 38 survivors to put the default fund back to where it had been, by this morning's call. The Bank lent again, to the second name on the page and to anyone else who asked, and the second name met Monday's call at 8.51 with the Bank's money, and the market went up a little, the way a man feels better on the second day of a fever. Nothing moved. The woman who said no on Sunday was still saying it. The man who said yes was on an aeroplane home.

I had the page in my coat before I was awake. I have been sleeping in the coat.

***

What I could do this morning, counted at six. Nothing. The working: no notebooks; they are in a room in Mayfair, read by a man who has already acted on them. A telephone four people can ring, none of them the one man who could act, because he has been recalled. No signature; the Chancellor gave her answer in person on Sunday. The map, in my head and on a page in my coat, rebuilt from memory and Graham's papers and Priya's red book, and as far as I can check it, right. A colleague in a hospital bed under a name that is not hers. That is the inventory. A man who can see exactly what is going to happen and cannot touch any of it. Priya would say I have had four years of practice.

7.04. Walbrook's call went out and the ticker said record, as it has said every morning for a fortnight, so that the word has stopped meaning anything, like a bell that will not stop.

8.00. The open.

It came in the order on the page. I want that down plainly while I can still write plainly. The second name, then the third, then the fourth. Not the whole list, and not the market; the market had been going down for two weeks, and this was something else inside it, with edges. The second member's parent, the bank whose name is on more plastic in more pockets than any other in the country, was thrown into auction on the exchange at 8.02, came out lower, and was thrown in again. Nobody does that to a bank on the first morning of the week's hope unless they have decided to. <!-- VERIFY: LSE volatility auction halts on a FTSE 100 bank in the first minutes after the 8am open, repeated; mechanism as in ch. 14 --> Then, on the prices page Calloway had left open on his laptop, the three long gilt lines I had written against its code in the holdings column fell faster than the gilts beside them. I know which lines they were because I was watching for them, which is the only reason anyone would have seen it. Three lines out of a few dozen, a little ahead of the rest, as if they had been pushed.

I have written since entry #212 about bonds lent out in the morning to raise the cash for the nine o'clock. If you want to break a man who pays that way, you do not attack him. You get to the repo market before him, borrow the exact bonds he will need, sell them, and make them dear to get back. Then Walbrook marks his collateral down and calls him for cash at the moment the cash has gone. I wrote that on a Tuesday when I was 36 as a thing that might happen to somebody one day. Somebody had read it. <!-- VERIFY: targeted repo squeeze on specific gilt lines; as marked in ch. 29 -->

8.30. The news had the phrase it uses when it has heard something it cannot source. *Reports that a major clearing member is experiencing difficulty rolling its overnight funding.* Experiencing difficulty. Four syllables again.

9.00. The deadline. The register in a room I have not stood in for two weeks going from grey to green, 38 lines, or 37. I could not see it. I have never in four years not been able to see it at nine. I sat in a stranger's kitchen with my hands flat on the table and tried to feel the room from four miles off, and could not.

9.40. Calloway, on phone two, from his desk: 'They're saying it hasn't confirmed.' I said I knew. He said how. I said it was the second name on the page.

10.02. The ticker. *Walbrook declares second clearing member in default.*

Mine. My second. I had it second at this table a fortnight ago and Priya argued and conceded; Greywether's list had it second; this morning the market had it second; now Walbrook has it second. Everyone's second. It is the name on Laura's debit card and on mine. There is a cash machine with its name on it 200 yards from this door, and by this afternoon there will be a queue at it, and tomorrow the queue will be the news.

I am stopping because I am going to the hospital. A wanted man in a hospital corridor at eleven on the worst day of the century is the safest man in London, because nobody in a hospital looks at anyone, and she will have found a screen by now and I would rather she did not watch it alone.

***

Second sitting. 1.40pm. A plastic chair in the corridor outside her side room, this paper on my knee.

The name on her chart is not hers and I am not writing the one it is. A wrist in plaster to the elbow, a bruise down the right side of her face that has gone from purple to a colour I have no word for, and a nurse every two hours with a torch who asks her the date, and she tells them, and then she tells them the pound.

She had a screen; a nurse had lent her a tablet. When I came round the curtain she said, 'Ten o'clock two minutes. You said by ten. You're slipping.'

We watched it the way we used to watch the register, without talking. What happened to Walbrook between ten and noon, as I would have written it in the room: Walbrook took the second book and had to hedge it, which means selling what it was long of into a market that knew exactly what was coming. The second member's own margin went first. Then its contribution to the default fund. Then what was left of the survivors' contributions, spent all last week on the first default and not yet put back, because this morning's refill was the call that half of them had just struggled to pay. The fund is sized for the two largest members failing at once. We have had two, a fortnight apart, in a market that fell every day between. Priya did the arithmetic on the back of her cast, left-handed, and got there before the screen did.

11.50. The third name. An intraday call, triggered by the price crossing a line. An hour to confirm. It confirmed at 12.44, 54 minutes in, with money I would bet came from Threadneedle Street at 12.30, because that is what the lending has been for. Four years of payment times in my head, and I have never seen that name go past twenty minutes. <!-- VERIFY: intraday call confirmation window of one hour, per research/swaps-and-clearing.md §2 -->

12.00. Richard Haldane, live, in front of the building I walked into every morning for four years and eleven months. He had shaved. He read a statement, and I am putting it down as near as I can because I want his words and not mine. Walbrook Clearing House had today declared a second clearing member in default. The firm's pre-funded resources had, in the course of the morning, been fully utilised. Walbrook had called upon its clearing members for further contributions under its rules and was in continuous contact with the Bank of England. Unless those contributions were received in full by the start of business tomorrow, Walbrook might not be in a position to meet its obligations. A further statement would be made at the close. <!-- VERIFY: default fund exhausted, then unfunded assessment calls on surviving members payable next morning, then a CCP's statement that it may be unable to meet its obligations, following the waterfall in research/swaps-and-clearing.md §2 -->

May not be in a position to meet its obligations.

Priya said, 'He said it. He actually said it on television,' and then nothing for some time, and neither did I. In the corridor a daughter asked her father what it meant and he said he did not know, love, something about banks.

I will put down what it means, once. Walbrook stands in the middle; every promise between two members is a promise to Walbrook and from Walbrook, and that is the only reason they trust each other. If Walbrook cannot meet its obligations, then tomorrow morning nobody knows what they are owed. Not a loss; a loss is a number. A blank. Every pension fund that hedged the pensions it owes has a hedge with no one on the other end. Every rate in the country is priced off a market that has stopped, and so is every rate everywhere that has ever dealt in pounds, and the first thing people do when they do not know what they are owed is not pay anyone. That is the freeze. Nine books describing it, and I had to write the sentence in a hospital.

12.20. The pound fell through a number last seen before I was born. <!-- VERIFY: sterling below any level since the 1970s or the 1985 low; the exact figure is deliberately not given -->

1.05. The fourth name. Not a default; a mention. A broker's note the news had got hold of, naming the fourth parent as "the next focus of market attention". I had it fourth. Greywether's list had it near enough fourth. The market has got there, two weeks after I did and a fortnight after he did, with the whole page still in my coat.

Priya watched my face while I read that and said, 'Don't.'

'Don't what.'

'Don't do the sum. I can see you doing it. Two of twelve, third in trouble, fourth named, and you're working out the day of the week for the twelfth. Stop.' She shut her eyes. 'You were right. We know. Everybody in the country knows now, Jonas, and it's doing them precisely as much good as it did you.'

They came with the torch at 1.30 and asked her the date and she told them, and the pound. I came out here because she had gone very quiet and I know what her quiet is for.

***

Third sitting. Calloway's kitchen. 7.05pm. The afternoon in order, since order is the one thing I have that nobody else does.

2.30. New York opened and did not halt. It simply fell, steadily, which is worse to watch than a halt, because a halt is at least somebody doing something.

3.10. I rang the office in St James's on phone two. The woman answered. The managing director was not in London. I said I knew that. If I had a matter to raise I was welcome to put it in writing. The same sentence as three weeks ago, when it was a door that opened eleven minutes later. Today it was a door.

4.00. The second member's parent was suspended from trading at its own request. The cash machine 200 yards from this door had a queue of 30 when I passed at half past four with my collar up, and a man at the front hitting the screen with the flat of his hand. Nobody looked at me.

4.30. The close. Walbrook's further statement did not come. Its site said "in due course", which is what institutions say when the statement is being written by someone else.

6.40. The Bank of England. Not the Governor; a statement over no name, read out by a correspondent in Threadneedle Street in the rain. The Bank was aware of the position of Walbrook Clearing House. It had provided and would continue to provide liquidity to the clearing house and its members. It was in close contact with the Treasury and was considering all options, including the use of its resolution powers, in order to protect financial stability. <!-- VERIFY: a Bank of England statement that it is considering CCP resolution under FSMA 2023 Schedule 11; the Governor's description of the regime is in ch. 24 -->

All options including resolution. Nobody has ever done it. Resolution is a fire door and not a sprinkler; I wrote that in the fourth book, about a different building. A fire door is a thing you go through when the building is already lost.

***

Twelve names. I have checked the order against today and it is the order. Second at 10.02. Third at 12.44, alive on borrowed money. Fourth named by one. I have a page and a memory and the fact that every single thing on the page has been right, in sequence, at the hour, and that the man who stole it has made more money today than most countries, and that the man who drew it is in a borrowed kitchen with the lights off, correct.

What the man who drew it could have done at 36 is for another night. Tonight only the fact: I was right about the names and the order and the day, and the clearing house I spent five years inside may not exist tomorrow, and the first did nothing whatever to the second.

I know roughly where my wife is tonight, and I cannot go there. I have written round that sentence twice today and I am putting it down straight. Nor my daughter. I have the number of a landline in Norfolk and a rule about not ringing it from a phone, and the rule is still the right rule, and it means the only two people I could not bear to lose are the two I cannot reach.

7.15. Someone is knocking at the street door. Two knocks, with a knuckle, and then nothing: no bell, no second go. Calloway rings the bell. Priya is in a bed. There is nobody left who knocks like that, and I know, from a kitchen in Norfolk I was not in, exactly who does.
