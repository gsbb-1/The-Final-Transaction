# Chapter 13: The lock-up

Top deck of a 47, going north. 11.48pm. The bag is between my feet with a foot through each strap. My left trouser leg is wet to the knee, the right knee is torn, and the back of my hand is bleeding. I am writing the rest of the day because it is the only part that matters, and because if I stop writing I will have to think. <!-- VERIFY: route 47 (Catford–Shoreditch via Deptford, London Bridge) is 24-hour -->

Laura came back at 5.10. The bag was on the bed. I had put it away once and taken it out again.

She looked at it and then at me and said, 'Sit down. You're grey.'

I sat on the bed and told her what I had decided to tell her, which I had rehearsed and which came out in the wrong order anyway.

That there are people who want my notebooks, and that they had been in the house on Friday while she and Elsie were out. That today a woman in a park had told me which road Laura walks down and the name of Elsie's nursery. That Priya had rung and been angry and then stopped answering, and her door had been open and she had not been behind it. That I wanted Laura to take Elsie to her mother's tonight, tell nobody, and not come back until I rang.

She asked the questions a doctor asks. Short ones, in order.

'What's in the notebooks?'

'A record. Of what I've seen at work. Numbers.'

'Four years of numbers.'

'Yes.'

'Who are the people?'

'I don't know. She works for someone. She wouldn't say who.'

'Are we in danger, or are you?'

'I don't know.'

'That's not an answer, Jonas. That's a result pending.'

'It's the one I've got.'

'Why not the police?'

I gave her the answer I had, which is that I did not know what I would be reporting, and that the people who had wanted the notebooks on Saturday were the government. I did not say it well. She watched my hands the whole time. I put them under my thighs eventually, like a boy.

She did not argue. She said, 'All right,' and started putting Elsie's things into the bag on top of her own, properly, nappies at the bottom and the thing Elsie needs first at the top, and I stood in the doorway and was no use. I had wanted the argument. I had been ready to lose it.

She rang Margaret and said they were coming up tonight, a change of plan, nothing wrong, and listened, and said, 'I'll tell you when I'm there.' She did not look at me while she said it.

7.50. Elsie in the seat, half asleep already, the rabbit in with her. Laura in the driver's door with the keys.

'You'll ring at eight.' Not a question.

'Yes.'

'If you don't ring at eight I'm ringing the police and telling them everything you've told me and everything you haven't, and I'll be guessing the second half.'

'Yes.'

She looked at me for a count of about three. Then she said, 'Four years,' and got in and shut the door. I watched the car to the junction. Brake lights on, then off. 7.53.

Dark coat, walking boots, the empty rucksack from under the stairs. 8.31, the P4 from Herne Hill. It is the long way to Lewisham and it is the way nobody would expect, because nobody takes the P4 anywhere by choice. <!-- VERIFY: P4 runs Brixton to Lewisham via Herne Hill -->

Nine o'clock came somewhere past Brockley. I waited for the phone to go, and it did not. Nobody rang to ask whether I had decided. That was when I understood that the asking was over, as she had said it would be, and that silence is what the next part sounds like.

Lewisham, 9.34. Ten minutes to the arch. Nine garages behind it, my father's the sixth, the rent still going out on the 3rd in his name.

A car at the mouth of the row, lights off, nobody in it. Nothing. There are always cars.

The padlock, the key, the door up on its springs with the noise it has made since I was fifteen. The phone for light, not the strip light. His bench. His crate. The smell, which is oil and cold brick and him. The steel toolbox on the shelf he put up. Eight black books in it, in order, dates on the spines. Into the rucksack, one by one, counting. The ninth was in my coat. Nine. I had never had them all in one place before, and I stood there and thought about it, which I should not have done. A train went over.

I pulled the door down and that was when the headlights came on.

At the end of the row, pointing at me, the car not moving. And at the same moment, from the direction I had come in by, a shape at the mouth of the row that was a person standing still, which is the one thing a person does not do at a quarter to ten on a Sunday behind a railway arch.

I would like to write that I did something. What I did was drop the padlock, and bend to pick it up because it was my father's, and then understand what I was doing, and leave it. It is still there.

The row is a dead end. Between the last garage and the arch wall there is a gap I know about because I was once fifteen, and I am no longer the width I was at fifteen. I went in sideways with the bag held in front of me. The coat caught and I heard it go. Something on the brick took the back of my hand. The person at the mouth of the row was walking now, not running, which was worse, and the car door had opened.

On the far side there is a chain-link fence and then the footpath down to the river, and I went over it the way a 40-year-old man who sits at a desk goes over a fence: I got to the top, and then I was on the ground on the other side with no clear memory of the middle. The knee went then. I got up. I checked the bag before I checked anything else, and I am not proud of the order.

The Ravensbourne is not much of a river and the path along it is not lit. I ran along it in the dark with the water on my left and my breathing so loud I could not hear whether anyone was behind me. <!-- VERIFY: Waterlink Way along the Ravensbourne, Lewisham to Deptford; lighting and night access --> I went down a concrete slope without meaning to and in to the knee, and the cold was the only thing that stopped me.

Footsteps on the road above. One set. Not hurrying. Then a car, slow, on the road beside the path, its lights going through the trees, stopping, going on.

There is a road bridge over the water a little further down. I got under it and sat on the ledge with the bag in my lap and my back against the brick, and I counted. To 300, four times. The car came back once. The footsteps did not come at all.

10.21. I came out and walked. Not ran. Back streets to Deptford. I did not look behind me, because looking behind is what a man does who is being followed, and I wanted to be a man walking to a bus.

The 47 came at 11.17. I am on it.

I cannot go home. They have been in the house, and the house is where a man goes who has notebooks and nowhere else. I am going to try to be a different man for a night.

I did not sign. I did not ring either number. Nine went past and I let it, and six is coming, and I will let that go too. I have nine books in a bag that smells of my father's garage, a wife driving north who gave me until eight and meant it, and a colleague who has not answered for fourteen hours.

11.52. Wellington has been open for two hours. Sydney opens in eight minutes. Tokyo an hour after that. I do not know the number yet. Priya knew it this morning. I hope she is somewhere she can watch it come true.

***

She stopped at the services before Newmarket because Elsie woke and would not settle, and sat in the car park for the best part of an hour with the engine off, feeding her, watching a lorry driver eat a sandwich, and not ringing anyone. Past Thetford the road went into the forest and there were no lights except her own.

Elsie had gone under before the M11 and had not stirred. Laura had tilted the mirror so she could see her: mouth open, head to the left, the rabbit's ear across her face. Breathing fine. She checked anyway, every few minutes.

She took a history, because that is what you do when you do not know what is wrong. Presenting complaint. Onset. Course.

Wednesday: his birthday. After Tom and Aisha had gone he had sat down to write at the kitchen table before she was up the stairs, and in the morning he had the face he has after writing, which is the face of a man who has put something down. Friday: she had come home at twenty past nine and he was at the kitchen table in his coat, writing, and he had said long day and put his hands in his pockets. She had not thought: someone has been upstairs. Saturday: a meeting, on a Saturday, in a suit, and back at half past six, grey, in a different shirt, which means he had sweated through the first. He had said 'work' on Wednesday and 'work' on Saturday and 'work' every night for four years, and she had stopped asking because the answer did not change, and she saw now that she had taken a stable reading for a healthy one.

Sunday: the call at breakfast and his hands. Then a woman in a park, a road, a nursery. Then a bag on the bed and his hands under his thighs.

What he had told her she could count on one hand. What he had not: what was in the notebooks; who the people were; why the government had wanted them on Saturday, which he had let slip and not explained; why not the police. She had asked that one straight and watched him not answer it.

She ran it as a differential. He had done something, and these were the consequences. Or he had found something, and someone wanted it kept quiet. Or he was ill, and none of it was real. She held the third one honestly for a mile and set it aside: he had been grey, not flushed; slow, not fast; and a man inventing a threat does not pack his daughter's nappies at the bottom of the bag.

Which left the first two, and from here she could not tell them apart. That was the finding. Everything she had, she had on his word. She had seen her husband sit on a bed and tell her to leave, and she had gone, with a child, into the dark, on four years of 'work'. The shape of what she had not been told sat in her like a mass under the skin: you can feel the size of it long before you know what it is.

Eight o'clock. He had said eight. She would give him until eight and not a minute past, and then she would stop being the kind of person who could be sent to Norfolk.

Midnight. She put the radio on, low, for the news, because it was something to do with her hands.

The pound had fallen sharply in early Asian trading. The market in Sydney had just opened, lower, and Tokyo would follow within the hour. The Chancellor would make a statement before London opened. There had been, the newsreader said, in the voice they use for the weather, unconfirmed reports concerning a London clearing house.

Laura had never once heard the words of his job on the news. She turned the radio off and drove.
