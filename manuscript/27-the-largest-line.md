# Chapter 27: The largest line

Monday. Week three. London again.

I am not writing how I came down from Norfolk. I have learned that lesson and I am keeping it. I will write that it took five hours and 20 minutes, that I did not sit facing anyone, and that at one point a man across the aisle opened a newspaper with my face on the front of it, folded it to the crossword, and did it in biro for 40 minutes without once looking up. That is what being famous is. It is a man doing a crossword on your face.

Laura said, at the door, 'You have one week.' Not a question. She has stopped asking me questions, which is worse.

***

Twelve names. I have had them since Thursday night, on one page, folded inside the ninth book, in Priya's handwriting and mine. Twelve members, in the order they fail, and what, I think, each is holding that it has promised to more than one person. I cannot prove a line of it. I have written that sentence in nine books and I am writing it again so that nobody who finds this page thinks I have forgotten.

What I can do is read it. And on the train, reading it for the ninth or tenth time, I did the sum I should have done a week ago. A map of where the selling will come from is only worth anything to two sorts of people: the people who want to sell first, and the people who could buy. Crane is the first sort. He has been paid every minute since last Monday and he still wants the books, and the reason he wants them is to know which three doors to kick next. I have spent a week running from the first sort. I have not once gone looking for the second.

There is only one buyer. I have known his line for four years without knowing his name. The largest line in the collateral report, every morning, through three clearing banks, segregated, in an account that says who it is to anyone in our building who cares to look. The fund that pulled its collateral last week and started this. The fund that has spent five working days turning British government bonds into cash and moving the cash somewhere it cannot be frozen. Everybody else in London is holding bonds that fall every hour. He is the one man holding the money.

He broke it. He is the only one who can afford to buy the pieces.

Priya said, at Calloway's table last night, that it was the stupidest idea I had had in four years and also the first one that involved leaving the room. Calloway gave me the address. He had rung it, twice, last week, for a comment, and got the same woman both times saying the fund did not comment.

***

St James's. A street of the kind where the brass plates do not say what the building is for. The fund's London office has a plate with four letters on it and a door that is opened for you by a man who has already decided whether to open it.

I had thought about this for five hours on a train and I still had no better plan than the truth. I gave my name. I said I would like to speak to the managing director's office. The man at the door looked at me for the length of time it takes to compare a face with a photograph, and then he said, 'One moment, sir,' and went behind a desk and picked up a phone and said my name into it, quite quietly, as if it were a password.

The lobby. Marble, a rug, two chairs nobody has sat in, a bowl of white flowers that were real. A screen on the wall with the sound off, which this week means a red line falling. 11.04 by the clock above the desk. I stood with the rucksack on both shoulders and counted the flowers. Fourteen.

A woman came down. Not the managing director. Not anyone, as far as I could tell: a good suit, a lanyard turned round so the name faced in, a card already in her hand. The office's card. The four letters, the street, a switchboard number. She said the managing director was not in London. She said that if I had a matter to raise I was welcome to put it in writing to the address on the card. She said it very politely and she had said it before, to Calloway, twice.

I said I did not have anything in writing that I could put in the post, and that I thought Mr Al-Mansouri would want to know what else was in the notebooks. I said his name. I had never said it aloud before. Four years of a line in a report.

She did not blink. She had read the paper too. 'Is there a number,' she said, 'where you can be reached?'

There is one. It is a telephone I bought for cash from a shop on the Caledonian Road a week ago, and I can count on one hand the people who have the number. I have been careful with it the way I was careful with the books, which is to say that I have kept it and done nothing with it. I wrote the number on the visitor's slip she gave me, in the box marked Company. I did not have a company. I wrote Walbrook and then crossed it out, and she watched me do it.

'Thank you, Mr Whitlock.' And the door, opened for me, by the same man.

Eleven minutes. A lobby and a card. I stood on the pavement in St James's with a sovereign wealth fund's switchboard number in my hand and understood that I had just done the one thing I have never done in 1,412 entries, which is walk in somewhere and ask.

***

I went to the park. Not the one by Priya's. The one with the lake, the one you reach from the bottom of that street in four minutes, because I could not think where else to stand still. Halfway along the water I looked up and understood where I was. Across the lake, the backs of the buildings on Horse Guards Road. Third floor. A room overlooking the park, where nine days ago a woman had put six pages in front of me and asked me to say they were wrong. I had walked to within 400 yards of the Treasury with the books on my back. I sat down on a bench with my back to it. I have sat on a lot of benches this week.

I gave it an hour, because that is what you give. If nothing came I would go back to Kentish Town and tell Priya she was right about the idea and wrong about the leaving.

11.52. The phone, which has rung four times since I bought it, rang.

A number I did not know, long, beginning with a code I did know. I let it ring twice because a man who answers at once is a man who has been waiting, and then I thought: I have been waiting for four years, and answered.

'Mr Whitlock.' A man's voice. Not the switchboard. Unhurried, exact, English learned early and kept carefully. 'My name is Khalid Al-Mansouri. You came to my office this morning. I am told you were polite about it.'

I said that I had tried to be.

'I have read Mr Calloway's article. I read it on Sunday, and again this morning, which I do not do with newspapers.' A pause that was not hesitation. 'It says that three years ago you wrote six pages to the British Treasury, and that they were filed. It says that the remainder of what you know is in a set of notebooks which the police would like to discuss with you. It does not say what is in them. I would like to know what is in them.'

'A list,' I said.

'Of what?'

'Of who is next. In order.'

Nothing on the line for a moment. Somewhere behind him a door closed. When he spoke again his voice had not changed at all, and I noticed that it had not.

'How long is the list, Mr Whitlock?'

'Twelve.'

'And how long have you had it?'

'Since Thursday, on one page. Since I was 36, in pieces.'

'Yes,' he said. 'The article gave that impression.' And then, as if it followed: 'I am not in London. I will be, tomorrow. There is a hotel on Park Lane that my office uses. Seven o'clock, tomorrow evening. Give your name at the desk and ask for Nasser; he is my chief of staff and he will bring you up. You will be searched, and I apologise for that in advance. You will come alone.'

'How do I know who you are?'

'You do not,' he said. 'You have my switchboard number on a card in your pocket. Ring it and ask for me, and you will be told that I am not in London, which is true. I am afraid that is the best I can do by telephone. Mr Whitlock. You came to me. I am assuming you had a reason, and that it was not money.'

'I had a reason,' I said.

'Then you may tell me tomorrow. I will listen for as long as it takes. Good morning.'

The line went. 11.57. Five minutes. I have had longer conversations with the man who sells me a travelcard.

***

I sat on the bench for some time afterwards and did what I do, which is count what had changed.

One hand's worth of people had this number this morning. The newest is a state. I do not know what a state does with a telephone number. I know what the police can do with one. I have decided, sitting here, that I will keep the phone on, because a man who has just asked a sovereign wealth fund to ring him back cannot then hide from the call, and because I am tired of being a person who cannot be reached. Laura would say that is the first sensible thing I have written in a fortnight. She would also ask why it took a stranger.

Crane's people, Walbrook, the Treasury, the police, a newspaper, and now a Gulf fund: every one of them knew more about my week than my wife did until Friday night. That is the record I actually kept. Not the one in the bag.

The bag. I have it on my knees as I write this, in a room in Kentish Town that is not mine, at a table that is not mine, with Priya across from me reading what I have written over my shoulder and not commenting, which for her is an act of love. Nine books. The twelve names folded inside the ninth. The pay-as-you-go on the table, face up, on. Everywhere I have gone since Sunday week I have carried it all, on both shoulders, and I have told myself that this was the safe way, because the books are only safe where I am.

Tomorrow I go into a hotel on Park Lane and I am searched before I am let into a room. Nobody has yet said what happens to a rucksack.
