# Chapter 40: Entry 1420

Tuesday. Late. Fourth sitting, same number, because it is the same day and I am not sure the day is going to let go of me in the ordinary way. The back of the last of Calloway's printer paper. My writing has got smaller through the day; I can see it happen down the sheets.

I wrote the morning at ten, the middle of the day in a hospital corridor and the afternoon at seven, and I have read those pages back once and I am not going to again. The second member at two minutes past ten. The default fund gone by noon. Haldane at twelve, shaved, in front of the building, saying that the clearing house "may not be in a position to meet its obligations", and saying it well, because he has always said things well. At 6.40, the Bank: "all options, including the use of its resolution powers". A word that has never been used anywhere in the world about a clearing house, used about mine, read out by a man in the rain in Threadneedle Street from a statement with no name on it.

The order was right. All of it. Twelve codes. The first fell on the Monday before last and the second this morning, the third is alive tonight on money it borrowed at half past twelve, and the fourth was named by a broker at one. I have had the order since Thursday week on one page and since I was 36 in pieces, and I was right, and I am going to count what being right has bought, because counting is what I have.

No notebooks. Nine of them, in a room in Mayfair with no screens, for six days now. No clearing house, or not after tomorrow. No signature: a woman on Horse Guards Road said no on Sunday in a sentence with the word "prudence" in it, and I have met the word before, in a covering note. Priya in a bed under a name that is not hers, her right wrist in plaster to the elbow, a nurse with a torch every two hours asking her the date. Al-Mansouri on an aeroplane home since Monday, as far as his office will say. And in the inside pocket of my coat, where the cloakroom ticket was, a brown envelope that a woman gave me at a quarter past seven, standing in this doorway, having knocked twice with a knuckle. I have not written her yet. I will, when I can. Not tonight. Tonight only this: what is in the envelope would finish Sebastian Crane, if I had anywhere to take it.

I have been through everywhere twice. The police: a man with a High Court order served on his house walks into a station with an envelope, and the envelope goes into a clear bag with a label, and the man goes into a room, and in the morning the clearing house fails whether or not he says no comment. The paper: Calloway can have it in print on Thursday, with lawyers, by which time there is no Walbrook to save. The Bank: wants the Treasury. The Treasury: no. The one man with money: a telephone call on Friday telling him to come home, and a woman in St James's telling me this afternoon that he is not in London, in the sentence she uses whether he is or not.

That is the joke, and I want it written down in case I am not the one who reads this. I have spent four years saying I could not prove it. This afternoon somebody handed me the proof, and it is paper. At the end of all of it, I have been given more paper.

***

Laura came at ten past nine.

I did not know she was coming, and that needs to be exact, because of what I wrote at seven. She drove down from Norfolk on Saturday, through the photographers, against what I asked, and left word with her mother that I would know where she had gone and was not to go there. I knew. Tom and Aisha's, twenty minutes from a house she cannot go back to. I did not go there and I did not ring it, because of the thing I understand about telephones, which she has learned from me and used on me, and for three days the only words between us have been read to me by her mother off a piece of paper at nine in the morning. She said she would find me. Tonight she rang the bell.

Calloway was at the paper. I looked through the spyhole and saw my wife on a landing in Kentish Town with our daughter asleep on her shoulder in a coat over pyjamas, the rabbit's ear against her cheek, a bag at her feet, and I opened the door and did not ask how, because she was there.

'You told me his name at Mum's table, and his flat was in the phone book,' she said, before I could. 'I watched the six o'clock. I've watched you every night for a week in other people's living rooms. The patient stops answering the phone, you go to the house. Where can I put her?'

The sofa. She built a wall of cushions along the edge of it, one-handed, before she put Elsie down, and then she put her down and stood with a hand flat on her chest for a count I knew was ten, and then she took her hand away and turned round and looked at me, in that order. Hands. Face. Weight. She has lost the habit of hiding what she is doing when she does it.

'You've lost about half a stone. You haven't slept. You're holding your left arm against your ribs, so something happened to them on Friday that you didn't tell me about. Sit down.'

I sat down.

She did not ask me how I was. She has stopped asking me questions whose answers she can read. She sat at Calloway's table, where the page had been made, with her coat still on, and said, 'Tell me where it is. All of it. The way you'd tell Priya.'

So I counted it for her, the way I have counted it above. No books. No clearing house. No signature. Priya. The envelope. The man recalled. She listened the way she listened at her mother's table, except that she did not watch my hands this time, because she already knew what they were doing.

When I had finished she said, 'And you were right.'

'About all of it. The order. The names. The days.'

'You were right,' she said again, as if she were reading a result back to me and wanted me to hear it in someone else's voice. 'And what has that done?'

I have been asked that question, in one form or another, by Priya, by Calloway, by a woman in a park, by my own wife at one in the morning in Norfolk. I have never answered it, because the honest answer has been in the seventh book since the week Elsie was born and I have never read it aloud.

I said: the record was the problem.

She waited.

I said: the memo was the warning. Six pages. I wrote it and it was filed and I was relieved, I told you that at your mother's. The record was not a warning. Nobody read it. That was the point of it. I kept score against the world for four years in a book the world could not see, and if I was wrong, nobody would ever know, and if I was right, I had it in writing. Either way I lost nothing. I spent four years being right in private because it was safer than being wrong in public. That is not what a careful man does. That is a hedge. I have been hedged since I was 36.

She did not say anything for a while. The flat made its noises. Elsie breathed behind the cushions.

Then she said, 'Then be wrong in public.'

I said I did not follow.

'You do. You've spent four years making sure that if you were wrong, nobody would see it. Fine. Go and be wrong where everyone can. Say the whole thing, out loud, in your own name, before you know how it ends, to people who can do something about it and people who can do something to you. If you're wrong, you're wrong in front of the country, and I'll be here, and so will she. If you're right, it's the first time in four years it'll have counted for anything.'

'I don't know how,' I said.

'Jonas. You told me the whole of it in two hours at my mother's table with a child on your knee. You've been rehearsing for four years. You just said it to me in four minutes and it was the clearest thing you've said since I met you.' She stood up. 'You don't need to know how. You need to know where.'

She picked Elsie up, cushions and all, and the rabbit, and asked which room was Calloway's. I told her. In the doorway she stopped, with our daughter's head under her chin, and said, more quietly, 'I'm not telling you to be brave. I'm telling you to be the thing you've written down. There's a difference and you know it,' and went in and shut the door.

Before she went in she asked whether the flat was safe, and I told her the truth: that a woman who is paid to know stood on this doorstep tonight and told me Crane has asked for Elsie by name, that the men who watched this door for a fortnight are his now, and that the same woman thinks they are hunting a bag and a broadcast, not a child, for one more night. In the morning Calloway takes the two of them out through the loading bay before I say a word into a camera. Laura listened to all of it and said, 'Then we're where you are,' and shut the door.

***

Elsie.

I have written to you before. The first time is in the seventh book, and you will know the entry if you ever read in order, because it is the one where the handwriting changes. Until then I was writing to be right. After that night I was writing to someone, and I told myself that was the change your mother asks about, the one I would not write down on the last night of my thirties. Different meaning. It was half the change. A letter you never send is a drawer. I had found someone to write to and gone on putting the pages in a garage.

Here is the other half, and I am going to write it once, plainly, and then not again, because a thing like this said twice is a thing a man is hiding behind.

Being right is worth nothing if you only write it down.

I was right at 36. I was right at 37, on letterhead. I was right 94 times by the night before my birthday and I am right 1,420 nights later, and a clearing house is going to fail in the morning, and a woman I love is asleep in a stranger's bed with her hand on your chest, and I have nothing in my hands but a borrowed pad and an envelope. That is what right is worth on its own. I have the exact figure now. I have always wanted the exact figure.

You changed the meaning because before you there was nobody the figure was for. Being right was the point; it did not need to be for anything. After you it was supposed to be for something, and I kept doing it the old way for a year and told myself the feeling was enough. The feeling is not enough. You will be told, by people who like you, that it is good to be right and that the world will come round. Some of them will mean well. Your father has conducted the experiment over four years at a cost of one clearing house, and the result is in.

What it is for is this. You say it where it costs you, to the people it is about, before you know how it ends. Your mother put it better. She said be wrong in public. She has been better at this than me for six years and I have only just written it down, which is, I suppose, the whole of what I am.

It is 11.20. Calloway has a key and will be back by midnight. He also has a lawyer's number and a studio the size of a cupboard, two floors below his desk, which he has mentioned twice this week in the voice of a man mentioning the weather. Your mother is asleep. Your father has an envelope, twelve names in an order, the only head in London that knows how fast each one goes, and nothing left to lose that he has not lost already.

He is going to be wrong in public. He has worked out where. He is sitting in a kitchen that is not his, listening for a key.
