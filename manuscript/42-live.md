# Chapter 42: Live

Past midnight. Still #1420. The day ends when I sleep, and I am not going to sleep, so this is the same entry, fifth sitting, on the last clean side of paper in the flat, and the last thing I write in it will be what I said to a journalist at his own kitchen table at ten to twelve. I want it in my hand before it is in anyone else's.

The key at 11.48. He came in the way he has come in every night this week, quietly, coat on, looking at the table first to see what was on it, and he saw me, and the sheets, and the bag by the sofa that was not mine, and the shut door of his own bedroom, and worked all of it out in the time it took to put his keys down.

'Your wife.'

'And Elsie. Since nine.'

'Good,' he said, which I had not expected, and put the kettle on, and stood with his back to me while it boiled, the way Laura had at her mother's. He had a copy of the first edition folded under his arm. He put it on the table face down. I did not turn it over. I know what is on it. I was there.

'The desk wants a piece from you for Thursday,' he said. 'In your own words. The whole thing. Two thousand words, the lawyers all over it, Crane's people given forty-eight hours to respond. It would be the biggest thing the paper has run in ten years.' He turned round. 'And it would run on Thursday, Jonas. I told them that. I told them there won't be a Thursday worth printing on.'

'No.'

'So I'm asking what you want instead, because you've got the face on.'

I said: six o'clock. This morning. Live.

He did not say anything, so I said the rest, in the order I had put it in while I waited for the key.

The paper has a studio. He has mentioned it twice this week as a man mentions the weather: two floors down from his desk, the size of a cupboard, a camera and a light and a chair, which they use to put reporters on the rolling channels when the channels want a face and will not send a car. At six it is empty. At six the people who read the news have been on air for an hour and have nothing to say that they did not say at ten last night. At six I sit in the chair and say all of it. What Walbrook is, in the words I used to Laura. Why it will not be able to pay tomorrow. The twelve, by name, not code, in the order they fail; two have failed and the order has been right twice in public, the third paid at 12.44 with the Bank's money, and the fourth was named by a broker at one for anyone who knows how to read a paragraph about "the next focus of attention". Then the dates. Greywether's positions, nine days before the ship. Faisal's office on the other side, which I will not name because I do not need to. Sebastian Crane. The envelope, held up. And the transaction: that there is a buyer with the money in London at seven, that the Bank can lend against what he buys the moment the Treasury puts its name under the Bank's, and that the one signature standing between the clearing house and the fire door belongs to a woman whose department wrote "Noted. File." on six pages three years ago.

'So that she has no choice,' he said.

'So that she can't file it. You can't stamp a broadcast.'

He sat down.

'I'm going to tell you what happens,' he said, 'and I'm going to do it the way I did the phone in Clerkenwell, because you listened then. Our lawyers will try to stop it. Not at six; they'll be asleep at six, and that's the only reason it goes out at all. But the moment the desk tells them, which the desk will have to, because I am not putting a wanted man in front of a live camera without the editor knowing, they will say three things. One: you are about to name twelve banks, on air, as about to fail, which is the one thing nobody in this country is permitted to say about a bank, true or not. Two: you are about to call a man a party to the sinking of a ship, with a brown envelope you got in a stairwell from a woman who has been following you for a fortnight. Three: Priya's on bail, you're subject to an order, the paper is already in it up to the neck, and if the editor says yes it's his licence and his pension. They will say all that and they will be right.'

'Yes.'

'And then the other thing. Jonas, look at me. You will be arrested within the hour. Not might. The paper's address is on the paper. A live broadcast is a man saying "I am in this building now." They've been polite with me because I'm the one in the phone book and you weren't; the moment your face is on a screen with a clock in the corner they don't need a form, they need a car. They'll let you finish, probably, because the pictures of them not letting you finish would be worse. Then they'll have you in the lobby. The envelope goes in a bag. You go in a room. Laura watches it on television.'

'Yes.'

'You keep saying yes.'

'I've said no for four years. I know what it's bought.'

The kettle had boiled and clicked and neither of us had moved. Through the bedroom door Elsie made the sound she makes, once, and stopped.

'Why six,' he said, more quietly. 'Why not eight, with a lawyer in the room and your wife somewhere else.'

'Because the Bank and the exchanges can hold London's open if they're given a reason before it, and not after. Because the banks' calls go out at eight and the direct debits land at nine and a clearing house that cannot pay has to say so to the Bank before then, and nobody gets to be told "later" this morning. Because a man with the money has to know where to put it before seven. And because she needs to be told in a way she can't have handled at official level in the normal way. At six she'll be awake. At six there's no one between her and the screen.'

'Tokyo's been open five hours.'

'Tokyo isn't where the clearing house is.'

He looked at me for a while. Then he reached across and turned the first edition over so I could see the front page, which I had not wanted to, and I looked at it, because he was right that I should. Haldane's face. The word in the headline that the Bank had read off a card. Below the fold, smaller, the picture of me in a tie that Elsie had tried to eat.

'One more,' he said. 'You said a buyer with the money in London at seven. On Sunday he had no authority, on Friday he was recalled, and on Monday you told me he was on a plane. Who says there's money?'

The phone said it. The cheap phone, face up on the table, which I have kept on since St James's, rang at 12.14, and I knew the number by its length before I knew it by anything else. I let it ring twice. Habit.

Nasser. He did not say good evening. He said, 'Mr Whitlock. Mr Al-Mansouri asks me to tell you three things. He is in London. He has been in London since Tuesday last; the office has told you otherwise twice, on his instruction, and he asks me to say that he knows you will have counted. Second, the fund will be a buyer at the open, with the Bank of England or without it. The money will be in London by seven. He has signed, in his own hand. Third, he should like to know where to put it.' A pause, in which I heard a hotel room and a man who had been awake as long as I had.

Twice. I had. I said that he would know where to put it at six, and from where, and that he should have a television on. Nasser said, 'A television,' in the voice of a man writing it down, and then, 'Thank you, Mr Whitlock,' and the line went. 12.16. Two minutes. The longest conversation I have had with a state, and the first in which it has told me where it was.

Calloway had heard enough of it.

'Right,' he said. He stood up and picked up his coat, which he had not taken off, and put his keys in his pocket, and stood there like a man who has just remembered he has to wake an editor, which he had. 'I'm going to go and ruin a man's night. You're going to stay here and not sleep, and at half past four I'll come back with a shirt that isn't that one, and we'll walk in through the loading bay, because the front hall has a camera and a man who reads the papers. The lawyers get told at five. The editor gets told now. If he says no, I'll tell you at half four, and you can decide what to do with the envelope and a day.'

'He won't say no.'

'He might.' He was at the door. 'You'll be arrested within the hour, Jonas. I want to hear you say it once more so that I know I said it to you.'

'Within the hour. Yes.'

'And the lawyers will try to stop it.'

'Yes.'

He stood with his hand on the latch. 'Four years you've been saying "not yet" to me, to her, to everyone. I want to know what changed. For the record. Yours, not mine.'

I told him what Laura had said. Four words. He heard them, and nodded once, the way you nod at a figure that has come out, and went.

12.20. The key in the door, from the outside. The flat quiet; the kettle cooling; a shut door with my wife and daughter behind it; a pad with my writing across the lines. In five hours and forty minutes I am going to sit in a cupboard two floors below a newsroom and say my own name into a camera, and everything after it, in order, and not one word of it will be new. I have had it written down for four years.

Yes. I said yes. It is 12.20 on Wednesday, and I have written that down, and in the morning I am going to say it.
