# Chapter 28: The hotel

Tuesday. Written on hotel paper, four sheets of it, taken from the writing desk in a lobby on Park Lane while a man in a waistcoat looked for a bag that was not there. The pen is mine. It was in my coat. That is why I still have it.

I am going to write the meeting first, in order, and try to write it the way I would have at seven o'clock, when I knew less. I will not manage it. You will see where I do not.

Nasser met me in the lobby at 6.58. Grey suit, no lanyard, a face that had decided about me on the way across the carpet. He shook my hand. Then he said, 'The bag, Mr Whitlock,' and I said the bag came with me, and he said, quite kindly, that it did not: nothing went up to the floor that had not been through his people, and his people did not open other men's bags, so the hotel would hold it. A girl in a waistcoat at the cloakroom gave me a numbered ticket, thin card with a crest on it, and hung the rucksack on a rail between a camel coat and an umbrella, and I watched it go onto the hook the way you watch a child go through a school gate. Ticket 63. I buttoned it into the inside pocket of my coat and went upstairs with the man who had made me do it. Two men outside the suite went through the coat and gave me back the pen, the phone and the ticket.

Khalid Al-Mansouri stood up when I came in. I do not know why I had not expected that.

A long room, a table under the window with the park going dark behind it and Park Lane's traffic making the glass hum. No screens. Papers in four piles, squared. He is 47 and looks it in the way men do who have been tired for a long time and do not mention it. He gave me his hand and then a chair, and said, 'I have not slept in your time zone yet. You will forgive me if I am direct. I owe you an account first, because you cannot judge what I ask for until you know what I know. Then you will owe me one.'

He turned the first pile round to face me. Trade confirmations, a dozen pages, the counterparty names blacked out properly. Dates down the left-hand side. I read dates for a living. I saw it before he said it.

'My cousin's name is Faisal. He runs a private office at home for a part of my family. Not a large one. Three weeks ago it became large. These are its trades, from eleven days before a ship sank in the Strait. Oil, bought. My own country's currency, sold forward. British government bonds, sold, through two intermediaries, in size. Freight. Insurers. Everything a man would do if he knew that on a certain night the Strait would close and nobody would know why.'

I asked who the counterparty was.

'On nine of the twelve tickets, the same name. Greywether Capital. You know it. Mr Calloway did not print it, but a man who has sat where you sat for four years does not need a newspaper for that.'

I said that Priya had found Greywether's largest positions dated nine days before the sinking, and that we had not known what it meant.

'It means this. Faisal is not a trader. He needed to move a great deal of money quietly, in a market that watches my country's money the way you watch a line in your report. He needed someone on the other side who would not ask why, who would take the risk onto his own book for a day and lay it off in pieces so that nothing showed. Mr Crane stood there, and was paid in the only coin that matters to him, which is the date. He did not sink the ship. He knew the night it would sink, and every pension fund in London has paid him for that knowledge since.'

I asked whether his government had done it.

'A faction in my family did it, to remove the men who run my country, by making my country the thing the world is afraid of. They have very nearly succeeded. My government has spent two weeks saying it was not us, and it was not, and it was. I cannot say that on television. I can say it to a man who has spent four years writing down what he could not prove.'

It was never an accident. I have written the word "accident" about that ship in six entries and I was wrong six times.

Then it was my turn. I am not writing it out; I have written it 1,412 times already and tonight I cannot carry it. Fingerprints, paired fails, cash at nine and bonds at three, four years of it. The memo. Twelve names and an order. That I could not prove the chains and had never claimed to. He listened without taking a note, and when I said "cannot prove" he nodded as if I had passed something.

'You did not bring the page.' Not a question.

'It is in the bag.'

'Which my people would not let up.' The smallest movement of his mouth. 'That is the first thing my security has protected me from this week that I actually wanted.'

I am writing that sentence down exactly because of what it turned out to mean.

'Mr Whitlock. You did not come to St James's to tell a stranger he was right. What do you want from me?'

I told him, in plain words. Twelve members are going to be forced to sell what they hold, in an order I know, over the next week, and each sale makes the next one certain. Nobody in London can buy; everyone in London is a seller. The one man in the world with the cash and a reason was sitting across a table from me. If a buyer with the map bought exactly what the weakest members were about to be forced to sell, at the moment they had to sell it, in the order they were going to fail, the selling would stop. Not slow. Stop. And anyone who had been short those twelve names since nine days before the ship would be squeezed until he broke.

'I want a buyer,' I said. 'You want what is in the books. It is the same thing.'

He looked at the table rather than at me. I know the look; I have worn it. A man who has seen the figure and is checking it before he says it aloud.

'You are asking me to bring my client's money back into the one jurisdiction I have spent a week removing it from. On the word of a man wanted by the police, using a list he cannot prove.'

'Yes.'

'I cannot carry it alone. I must be able to borrow against what I buy the same morning, or I am simply the next man holding bonds that fall. That means the Bank of England, and the Bank will not lend without the Treasury behind it, and the Treasury is run by a woman who denied on Sunday that she had ever seen your memo.'

'Yes.'

'And it must be done in one morning. The moment the market sees what I am buying, it will know the list.'

'One morning,' I said. 'One transaction.'

I have been in a room with the Governor, the Chancellor and a woman offering three million pounds, and never before with someone who saw the same thing at the same moment I did. He saw the transaction, whole, in the order it would have to go. I watched him see it.

'Then we need the Bank in the room,' he said, 'and before I am recalled, which will happen, because my cousin is not stupid. I will ask for the meeting tonight. You will bring the page.' He stood. 'For two weeks I have believed that I served my client best by leaving. You are the first person to suggest that I might serve it by coming back. I do not yet know whether you are right. Nobody else has suggested it.'

8.37 by the mantelpiece clock. Nasser took me down. I was already feeling for the ticket in the buttoned pocket before the doors opened, because for one hour and 39 minutes I had done the thing I have not done since Sunday week, which is let the books out of my sight.

She had taken the chair at the far end of the lobby at 6.40, with its back to the window and a sightline to the cloakroom and the lifts, and had ordered a tea she did not want so that she would be a woman with a tea. Dark trousers tonight, a pale jacket, hair down. He had looked at her face for 19 minutes in a park nine days ago and she had let him, and this was the cost: she could not be the one to go to the counter.

She had watched him come in at 6.52 with the bag on both shoulders. She had watched the chief of staff refuse it, which she had not expected, and had felt the small cold pleasure of a plan improving itself. Then she had read the number off the stub as the girl held the book to the light to tear it straight; Kessler had chosen the chair for the light. Sixty-three.

Ten minutes earlier the first of her two men had checked an umbrella, received 61, and photographed both sides of it. She had sent the pictures to the office at 6.55 with one line. The office had the card stock already: on Monday afternoon her people had watched him walk into an office in St James's, and she had decided that a man wanted by the police would meet a sovereign fund in one of four hotels, and had had a coat checked at each. The third member of her firm, the one she did not use on the street, had a very expensive printer and no curiosity. The cab took 38 minutes; the ticket in it had taken 14 to make. She timed things.

At 8.12 her second man, in a good coat that was not his, walked to the cloakroom with 63 in his hand and said, before he was asked, that it was the black rucksack, the old one, he was afraid, and the girl laughed and said it was the only rucksack she had taken all night. He tipped her, and went out through the revolving door with four years of another man's evenings on his shoulder and did not look at the chair by the window, because he had been told not to.

She stayed. That was the only part not in the plan, and she told herself it was to be sure he came down alone.

The lift doors opened at 8.41. He was already reaching into his coat. He crossed the carpet at a walk that wanted to be a run and put the ticket on the counter, and the girl went to the rail and stood in front of it, looked at the ticket again, went to the back, and came out with the man in the waistcoat, and Kessler watched Jonas Whitlock understand.

He did not shout. He stood with both hands flat on the counter and his head down, and she saw his lips move and knew that he was counting. The man in the waistcoat went along the rail coat by coat. Whitlock did not watch him. He had turned and was looking at the lobby, every chair, every face, and his eyes went over her and did not stop, because she was a woman with a tea and her hair down and he had never once seen her sitting.

She felt what she usually felt at this point, a column adding up. Underneath it was something else, and she made herself look at it, because she did not permit herself things she had not looked at.

The subject had just spent an hour and 39 minutes with the managing director of a Gulf sovereign wealth fund. Her client had told her yesterday that the price had gone up because a Gulf fund was asking questions about Greywether's trades. He had not said which questions, or why a fund that had made more in a fortnight than in the previous nine years should mind who asked. He had said "by whatever route works", and when she had asked whether that included the child he had not said no.

Twelve names and an order. She had assumed, because it was her job to assume, that he wanted to know where to push. She looked at the man at the counter, who had spent four years writing down what he could not prove and had just lost it to a girl with a book of tickets, and understood that she did not know what Sebastian Crane would do with twelve names, and that in fifteen years of government work and six on her own account she had never handed something over without knowing that.

The man in the waistcoat reached the end of the rail. Whitlock asked for something, and the girl, red now, gave him paper from the writing desk, four sheets, and he folded them once and put them in the pocket where the ticket had been, and buttoned it.

Kessler put a note under the saucer and left by the side door onto the park, where the car was.

'Mayfair?' the driver said.

'The office. The safe. Mr Crane can have them at nine.' She looked at the rucksack on the seat beside her and did not open it. 'I want to know what I'm carrying first.'
