# Chapter 12: The offer

#1404

She crossed the road when I reached the pavement. Not quickly. She finished the coffee and put the cup in the bin on the corner first.

'Mr Whitlock. My name is Dana Kessler.' She did not offer a hand and did not offer a card, not then. 'I'd like ten minutes. There's a café on the corner, or we can walk. I think you'd rather walk.'

I would rather have walked. I did not like that she knew it.

Mid-forties. Dark coat, flat shoes, no bag, hair tied back. Nothing you would describe to anyone. She spoke the way a good anaesthetist speaks, which is Laura's phrase: quietly, in full sentences, so that you do not notice you are being told what will happen to you.

'Have you been up there?' I said.

'No.'

'Have your people?'

'I'm going to talk about you, Mr Whitlock. Not about anyone else. Shall we?'

We walked towards the park. It was 12.51. Families, dogs, a man selling balloons on the corner by the gates. I counted eleven children between the flat and the first path, because I was already counting them before I knew why.

'I act for a client,' she said. 'He would like to acquire your notebooks. All of them. Today.'

'Which notebooks?'

'Black. A5. Hard covers, the kind with the elastic. There's a box of six in the bottom of the right-hand drawer of your desk at home, still in the plastic, same make. I'm assuming the used ones match. You strike me as consistent.'

So that was Friday. I had known it was Friday. It is different to be told.

'Nine, I'd estimate,' she said. 'One every five months or so, if you write a page a day. I may be out by one.'

She was not out by one.

'My client would pay three million pounds. In any form you find convenient. Sterling, another currency, an account in a name of your choosing, or you can have it in a holdall if you'd like to feel it, though I'd advise against. Today. Before nine this evening.' <!-- VERIFY: the amount of Kessler's offer is undecided in the ledger; £3m is a placeholder for the author to confirm or change -->

She said three million the way Laura says a dosage. No weight on it. A number that was correct for the patient.

'For notebooks.'

'For time. My client has people who could work out what you've worked out. It would take them a month. He doesn't have a month. From what I gather, nobody does.'

I did not mean to ask who the client was. I asked anyway. 'Who is he?'

'Someone who'd like to know what you know, and is prepared to pay for it rather than take it. I'd dwell on the second half of that.'

A woman went past us with a pushchair and Kessler stepped off the path to let her by, and watched the child go for half a second longer than she needed to. I noticed that because I was noticing everything. I do not know what it meant.

'Where is Priya Raman?' I said.

She did not stop walking. 'I told you I'd talk about you.'

'She's not at her flat. The door was open.'

'If I told you I didn't know where she was, you wouldn't believe me. If I told you I did, you'd believe that, and I'd rather not be responsible for what you did next. So I'm not going to say either. That's not me being clever, Mr Whitlock. It's me being careful with you.'

I have been in a room with the Chancellor of the Exchequer and the Bank of England this week and nobody has frightened me as much as a woman declining to answer a question in a park.

'The money is the easy version,' she said. 'I want to be plain about that, because you're going to go home and think I was threatening you, and I'd like you to remember that I wasn't. I'm describing. Your wife walks to the surgery on Mondays, Tuesdays and Thursdays. Dulwich Road, along the park, Brixton Water Lane, in by quarter past eight. She drops your daughter first. Brambles, on Half Moon Lane. They open at half past seven and your wife is usually there at ten to eight, because she likes to be early and she likes to be the one who takes the coat off.'

She said all of it in the same voice as the three million. A woman reading a timetable aloud. No emphasis anywhere, which is where the emphasis was.

'I'm telling you so you know how much I know,' she said. 'Not for any other reason.'

'You've said my daughter's name.'

'I've said your daughter's nursery. I haven't said her name and I'm not going to.' A pause. The first one she had taken. 'I would very much prefer you took the money. I'd like you to understand that as a preference and not as a tactic. It is the outcome I want. It's the outcome you want, if you think about it with anything except the part of you that's been right for four years.'

We had come round to the gates again. She stopped and I stopped.

'This is how it goes,' she said. 'Today I ask. You say no, because you're going to, I can see it. You go home. You think about your family, which you should. At some point this evening you make a decision. If it's the one I'd make, you ring the number on this card before nine, you tell me where, and somebody comes and collects nine notebooks and you never see or hear from me again. You'll be surprised how completely.'

'And if it isn't.'

'Then I stop asking.' She took a card out of her coat pocket. Plain white, a mobile number, nothing else. 'I don't enjoy the next part, Mr Whitlock. That isn't a moral position. It's untidy and it's expensive and people remember it. But I'm paid to get to the end, and I do.'

I took the card because not taking it would have been a gesture, and she was not a person you made gestures at.

'You should know,' I said, 'that the Treasury asked for them yesterday.'

She did not look surprised. I do not think she can. 'And what did they offer?'

'Safekeeping.'

'Then you've had two offers in two days for the same nine books, and one of them was free. I'd think about what that tells you about their value.' She looked at her watch, a small plain one on the inside of her wrist. 'Ten past one. You've got until nine. Theirs is six tomorrow morning, I believe. Mine comes first. Go home.'

She walked away towards the station and did not look back, which I know because I watched her the whole length of the road. At the corner a man in a grey jacket who had been reading his phone outside the newsagent put it in his pocket and went the same way, thirty yards behind her. I had seen a grey jacket on Friday, on the far side of a street and then through the glass of a train. I cannot say it was the same one. I cannot say it was not. I had been looking at children.

I stood by the gates for some time. I do not know how long. I did not count it.

Central line, Circle line, Thameslink. The same 51 minutes in reverse. On the train I took out the card and looked at the number, and then I took out my phone and looked at Priya's number, and I understood that I was checking whether they were the same, and that I did not want to know. They were not. That proves nothing. I know what proves nothing. It is my job.

Somewhere past Elephant and Castle I did the sum she had told me to do. Three million is the house, cleared, four times over. It is Elsie's education and Laura never working a Saturday again and my father's garage rent paid until I am dead. It is also what a man who has been positioned for a collapse would pay without noticing, to be told which morning. I know what a position like that is worth when it comes right. I have cleared them. Three million is the change.

The house was empty. Laura had taken Elsie to Aisha's for the afternoon; there was a note on the table under the statement, which she had moved to write it. She will have read it. It is two paragraphs. *Back by 5. Eat something.*

I ate something. I do not know what it was.

Then I sat down here, at the kitchen table, with this notebook, and I began to write the day, in order, because I did not trust myself to remember it correctly by tonight and because I did not know where I would be. That was twenty past three. It is now ten to four.

I have Kessler's card on the table in front of me. I have the statement next to it, and Carrow's card on top of that. Two telephone numbers and a line for my name. Any one of the three would be the end of it. Nine books in a garage in Lewisham, three million pounds, the Chancellor's gratitude, a quiet Monday. I have added it up four times. It keeps coming out the same, and I keep not believing the total, which I have told junior analysts for ten years is the first sign that the total is correct.

Laura will be back at five. Elsie will need her tea. I have not decided what to tell her.

I have decided that she is leaving tonight. There is a bag open on our bed that is not mine.
