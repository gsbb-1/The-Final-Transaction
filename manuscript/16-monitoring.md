# Chapter 16: Monitoring

The room was under the Cabinet Office and had no windows, which was the point of it, and a clock that was a minute slow, which Caroline Strand had noticed on her first visit and never had corrected, because a minister who asks for a clock to be fixed is remembered for the clock. <!-- VERIFY: COBR briefing rooms are in the Cabinet Office at 70 Whitehall; windowless; the slow clock is invented -->

Tuesday, 7.30am. The prime minister at the head of the table, in the chair that was no different from the others and was never sat in by anyone else. The Governor of the Bank of England on his right, with a deputy. The Cabinet Secretary. Simon Carrow at her left, where he had been for three years. A note-taker at the end, a young woman with a laptop, who would produce the minutes, and the minutes were the thing. Everyone in the room would be gone within a decade. The minutes would not.

The prime minister had not slept, which he wore as a kind of tan. He said, 'Caroline. Where are we.'

She had the page in front of her and did not look at it. 'Sterling closed in New York at a level I am not going to repeat in this room, because every figure said here finds its way out of it. The gilt market opened at eight yesterday and in any meaningful sense has not opened since. One clearing member of Walbrook has defaulted. The clearing house has taken over its book and is selling what it has to sell into a market that knows what it has to sell. The Bank provided liquidity under its existing facilities, which is what the Bank is for, and the three largest banks are standing. Nobody has failed who should not have failed. I would ask us to hold on to that sentence, because it is true, and it is the one we need.' <!-- VERIFY: Bank liquidity facilities (ILTR, DWF, CTRF) available to banks in a stress; a CCP's access is narrower -->

'Governor.'

The Governor was a careful man, which she had always respected and this week found inconvenient. 'Prime Minister. I agree with the Chancellor's account of yesterday. I do not agree that it describes today. The defaulted member's book is being hedged, which means forced selling of long-dated gilts at a known time in a known direction. Every other member's collateral is marked down as that selling proceeds, so every other member is called again. We are watching three further members closely.' He did not name them. The not-naming sat on the table like a third party. 'The Bank's view is that this market will not clear on its own, and that the Bank should stand in it. Temporary, targeted purchases of long-dated gilts, announced before the open, for a fixed period. We did something very like it in 2022, and it worked within the hour.' <!-- VERIFY: Bank of England temporary gilt purchases, 28 September to 14 October 2022, indemnified by HM Treasury -->

'At what size.'

'Whatever is needed. We would announce an upper figure. The figure matters less than the announcement.'

'And the losses, if there are losses.'

'Would fall on the Bank's balance sheet, and the Bank would look to the Treasury to indemnify them, as last time. Which is why it is the Chancellor's decision as much as mine.' He turned to her and did it courteously. 'Chancellor.'

She had known since Sunday that this was coming and had drafted the answer in the car. She did not use the draft. The draft had been for the Governor; this was for the minutes.

'No.'

The prime minister waited. The Governor did not move.

'I want to be exact about what I am saying no to, because I am not saying no to the Bank. The Bank is independent, and if the Governor judges that purchases are necessary for financial stability, that is his judgement to make without me. What I am declining to do is indemnify them. The Treasury will not underwrite the Bank buying government debt, at a price nobody can defend, four weeks before polling day. If I do that, every newspaper in the country has the word by lunchtime, and it is not "stability", and it is not "orderly". It is the other word. We spent yesterday saying there would not be one. I will not provide one by Tuesday.'

'Chancellor,' the Governor said, 'with respect, the word is already in use.'

'In the press. Not in this government's mouth. The distinction is the whole of my job this month.' She turned to the prime minister, which was the only move that counted. 'Twenty-four hours. The member's book is a finite quantity and it is being sold. When it is sold, the forced selling stops. Walbrook's default fund is intact. We say what we said yesterday and we do not put the taxpayer's name on a price that falls. If the Governor's three members come under real pressure, we meet again, this afternoon if necessary. But we do not pre-empt a bailout of the City on the morning after we said the City did not need one.' <!-- VERIFY: a CCP's default waterfall (defaulter's margin, defaulter's default fund contribution, CCP skin-in-the-game, survivors' contributions) and how quickly a single large default could exhaust it -->

She had said "bailout" herself, once, and placed it where she wanted it: in a sentence about not doing it.

The prime minister looked at the Governor, then at the clock, then at her, and she watched him do the arithmetic she had done in the car: that the Governor would be Governor whichever way the vote went, and she would not, and neither would he. 'Twenty-four hours,' he said. 'Governor, I would be grateful if the Bank's statement this morning said what the Chancellor's says. I do not want two governments.'

The Governor said that the Bank's statement would reflect the Bank's assessment, which was the only sentence available to him. His deputy wrote something down.

***

'Simon. The other matter.'

Carrow did not open his folder. He had read it in the car, and he spoke in the voice of a man reading aloud to a child who had asked for the story again.

'Prime Minister. Walbrook's head of collateral risk, Whitlock. He did not sign the statement on Sunday night and he has not been in contact since. His phone has been off since yesterday morning, bar three minutes in the afternoon morning. Walbrook obtained an interim injunction and an order for delivery-up of documents on Sunday evening, by telephone, from the duty judge; the order was served at his home address in Herne Hill yesterday morning. The house was empty. His wife and child left for her mother's in Norfolk on Sunday evening.' A fractional pause. 'We understand.'

'Where is he?'

'In London, probably. He used a cash machine in south London at 3.40 yesterday. The police are treating him as a person they wish to speak to, not more than that, at present.'

'And the woman.'

Carrow did not look at Strand. 'Priya Raman. Senior analyst on his team. On Friday afternoon, after Whitlock's system access had been narrowed, she logged in at his desk under her own credentials and extracted the member-level collateral report, which she was authorised to view at her own desk and not to extract, and certainly not to pass to him. Walbrook's systems logged it. The police received a complaint on that basis, under the Computer Misuse Act, and she was detained at about noon on Sunday at her flat. She was interviewed under caution overnight with a duty solicitor and released yesterday morning after about twenty hours, under investigation. Her phone and laptop were retained. A bail condition that she does not contact Walbrook staff.' <!-- VERIFY: "released under investigation" after about 20 hours within the 24-hour PACE limit; devices retained under PACE s.22; pre-charge bail conditions under the Bail Act as amended --> He turned a hand over. 'She is believed to have gone to her parents'. In the Midlands.'

'Did she pass it to him?'

'She said nothing in interview. The sent items on her laptop say it went to the Bank of England's whistleblowing address, and to a journalist. The police have not yet told us which.'

The prime minister said, 'Christ,' quietly, and looked at Strand.

Strand did not ask who had made the complaint. She did not ask who had written to the police, or on what paper, or whether a Treasury director's letter had gone in alongside Walbrook's. She did not need to. On Sunday afternoon, at 3.10, in her office with the door shut, Simon had said that it would be prudent to put the Treasury's concerns about the analyst to the police in writing, so that the matter was properly recorded, and she had looked at the papers on her desk for a count of about three and said that he should do whatever he judged appropriate. Which was yes. She had been a barrister for twenty years and knew precisely how much a silence weighed and who would weigh it. <!-- VERIFY: a senior official writing to the police to ask them to investigate, with the minister informed but not directing, follows the Home Office / Damian Green pattern (2008); ministers cannot direct operational policing -->

What she said, for the room and for the young woman at the end of the table, was: 'The position, Prime Minister, is that a clearing house has reported the theft of market-sensitive material by a senior employee, and the police are dealing with it. That is a matter for the police and for Walbrook. It is not a matter for this government, and I would be concerned if it were suggested that it was.'

She let the note-taker finish typing.

'I would add only that the material concerned is, on Walbrook's account, the firm's confidential information about named institutions, and that its theft, at this moment, is itself a threat to stability. Whoever has it has a list of who is weak. I would like that understood.'

Twice. Both times with Walbrook's name in front of it, so that if anyone ever read the minutes aloud in a room with lawyers in it, the word would be the firm's, and she would merely have repeated what she was told. She knew it was not the charge. There was no charge. Information could not be stolen in this country; she had known that since her first year at the Bar, from a case about a student and an examination paper. <!-- VERIFY: Oxford v Moss (1979): confidential information is not property under the Theft Act 1968 --> What Walbrook had was a civil order about documents. What the police had was a computer offence against a woman who had pulled a report for her boss. What the country had was a word, and she had used it twice before eight in the morning in a room where it would be written down and kept for thirty years.

The prime minister said, 'All right.' He was already on the next page, which was the Gulf. 'Caroline, you're on the broadcasts at 8.15. "Monitoring."'

'Monitoring,' she said. 'Closely.'

***

In the car, with the glass up between them and the driver, Carrow said: 'The memo.'

'What about it.'

'Whitlock took a copy out of the building on Saturday. The scan, with the footer. If he gives it to a newspaper, the footer gives them the file reference and the date we printed it, which was Wednesday.'

She looked out at the park, where a man was running in the rain in shorts, as though it were a Tuesday. 'And what does Wednesday tell them?'

'That we pulled it before the tanker.'

'We pulled it,' she said, 'because a firm reported a conduct issue with a senior employee and we were reviewing the file. That is what the records request says, I imagine, since you made it.' She did not look at him. 'Does it?'

'It will.'

'Then there is nothing in the footer that was not in the file.' She turned back to the glass. 'Four weeks, Simon. Then either I am Chancellor, and we do what the Governor wants with a mandate behind us, or I am not, and someone else can decide what to call it. In the meantime I will not have a Treasury indemnity and the word "theft" in the same bulletin, because one is a decision and the other is a report, and I intend the country to be able to tell them apart.'

Carrow said, 'Yes, Chancellor,' which was what he said when he had already done the thing.

The car turned into Whitehall. Her phone showed the Asian close and the European open, and the gap between them, and she put it face down on the seat, because at 8.15 she would be asked what the government was doing, and the honest answer was the word the prime minister had given her, and she had spent the night making sure it was the only word there was.
