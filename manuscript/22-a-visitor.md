# Chapter 22: A visitor

The grey car was at the end of the lane again on Friday morning, in the same place, nose towards the village, parked tight into the hedge where the lane widened for the field gate. Laura saw it from the landing window at ten past seven while she was carrying Elsie down, and she stood with Elsie on her hip and looked at it the way she would look at a rash that had come back.

Thursday she had noticed it and explained it. A birdwatcher. Somebody waiting for somebody. Her mother's village had one lane in and one lane out and the same eleven cars, and Laura had been coming to it long enough to name eight of the eleven, and a grey saloon she did not know had been a thing to notice and then let go. On Thursday she had let it go. She had other things. Elsie had turned one on Thursday and her father had rung at nine from a call box, as he now did, for four minutes, and had said happy birthday down a line to a child who was asleep, and Laura had made a cake with her mother because Elsie was one and that was what you did, and had photographed her with her hands in it, and had not sent the photograph to anyone because there was no one to send it to.

Friday it was in the same place, and nobody who is waiting for somebody waits two days in the same place.

She did not say anything to her mother. Margaret was 71 and had spent the week pretending the radio was not on, and Laura had let her.

***

At twenty past eight a woman walked up the lane.

Laura saw her from the kitchen. She was alone, on foot, a dark coat buttoned, flat shoes, no bag, hands in her pockets against the wind off the field, and she walked up the middle of the lane without hurrying and without looking at the house, as though she had walked up it before. She came to the gate and opened it and closed it behind her and knocked on the front door, twice, with a knuckle.

Margaret had gone down to the shop for the paper, which she did at eight and read at nine as if it were a prescription. Elsie was on the kitchen floor with the saucepan and the wooden rings, posting and taking out, posting and taking out. Laura wiped her hands and went to the door and opened it on the chain, which her mother never used and which Laura had put across on Monday night without deciding to.

'Dr Whitlock. My name is Dana Kessler. I'm sorry to come to the house. I'd like five minutes, and I'd rather not have them on the step, but I will if you prefer.'

Mid-forties. Hair tied back. A small plain watch worn on the inside of the wrist, which Laura noticed because nurses wear them that way and almost nobody else does. She stood a full pace back from the door, square, weight even, hands out of her pockets and loose at her sides where they could be seen. It was how Laura stood when she went into a room where someone was frightened. She had been taught it. She recognised it being done to her.

'My daughter's in the kitchen,' Laura said.

'Then we can talk in the kitchen. I won't touch anything and I won't stay.'

Laura took the chain off. She would think afterwards about why, and conclude that it was because the woman had said "won't" and not "wouldn't", and Laura had believed her.

***

Kessler stood where Laura put her, by the door to the hall, and did not sit, and did not look round the kitchen the way a stranger does. She looked at Laura. Once, when Elsie dropped a ring on the tiles, her eyes went down and came back, and stayed back.

'Your husband has something that doesn't belong to him,' she said. 'I act for a client who would like it returned, and who has been patient. I want to be accurate about that word. He has been patient for six days and he was offered a very reasonable alternative to patience on Sunday, and he declined it. I've come to tell you two things. The first is that the offer could, with some difficulty, be revived. I'd be prepared to try.'

'And the second.'

'The second is that there are people interested in what your husband has who are less patient than my client, and who do not come to the front door and knock.'

She said it in the same voice as the first thing. Laura knew the flat voice for what it was, which was control, and she knew that the people who could hold it while they said the worst thing were the ones who had said it before.

'Who are they?'

'If I knew their names I'd be a good deal more worried than I am, and I'd like you to notice that I'm not telling you I'm not worried.'

'Who's your client?'

'Someone who'd pay money rather than take. That distinction is going to matter to you more than his name would.'

Elsie had got herself up on the leg of the table and was standing, holding on, looking at the stranger with the open, unembarrassed stare of someone who has been alive for a year and a day. Kessler did not smile at her. She did not do any of the things people do. She looked at the child for exactly as long as it took to see where she was, and then she looked at Laura again, and Laura, who had been going to pick Elsie up, found that she did not want to cross the room to do it with this woman's attention on her and stayed where she was, by the sink, with the whole kitchen between them.

'You know where he is,' Kessler said. It was not a question.

'No.'

'You speak to him.'

Laura said nothing. The woman waited precisely as long as a doctor waits after asking how much someone drinks, and then let it go in the same way, as though the silence had been the answer and the answer had been noted.

'I'm not going to ask you where. I'd like you to give him a message, when you speak to him, and I'd like you to pass it on word for word, because the wording is the point.' She took a card from her coat pocket with two fingers and put it on the corner of the table nearest to her, not nearest to Laura, and took her hand away. Plain white. A mobile number. Nothing else. 'Tell him that I came to the house, that I knocked, and that I spoke to you in your kitchen with your daughter in the room, and that nothing happened. Tell him that is my way of doing this. Tell him it isn't everyone's.'

'Is that a threat?'

'No, Dr Whitlock. I've been very careful that it isn't.' A pause. 'That's rather the point of it.'

Then she said thank you for her time, and that she was sorry about the car, which she would move, and she let herself out, and shut the front door behind her quietly, the way you shut a door in a house where a child might be asleep.

***

Laura stood at the sink for a count of about twenty. Then she crossed the room and picked Elsie up, and Elsie put a hand flat on her face, and Laura realised that her own face was cold and her hands were not steady, and she looked at them, because that was what she did, as though they were someone else's. Tremor, fine, bilateral. Pulse up. Mouth dry. She knew the presentation. She had treated it a thousand times, from the other side of the desk, in people who had just been told something by a professional.

That was the word that came, and she made herself look at it. A professional. The woman had not raised her voice or lowered it. She had not said anything that could be repeated to a police officer and sound like anything. She had kept her hands where they could be seen, used the child's presence without once using the child, and walked out leaving a card on the corner of the table and a kitchen that felt different. Laura did it for a living, the controlled delivery of a thing nobody wants to hear, and she had just had it done to her by someone better at it, and the part that frightened her most was that she had let her in because she was good.

Through the window she watched the dark coat go down the lane. The grey car's lights came on when the woman was still thirty yards from it, which meant there was someone in it, and had been on Thursday too, while Elsie had her hands in the cake.

Margaret came back from the shop at a quarter to nine and said the lane was clear for once, and Laura said yes, and looked at the clock.

***

He rang at nine. He had rung at nine every morning since Tuesday, from a call box, for as long as his coins lasted. He would not give her a number; he had explained why on Monday, and she had understood it and not forgiven it. It meant that she could not reach him. It meant that she had fifteen minutes to decide what to do with four.

She put Elsie in the high chair with a rusk, where she could see her, and stood by the phone with the card in her hand, and when it rang at two minutes past she picked it up before the first ring had finished.

'It's me.'

'A woman called Dana Kessler has just been in Mum's kitchen,' Laura said. 'She stood about there. Elsie was on the floor. She was very polite. She left a card.'

She heard him breathe out. She heard, behind him, a road, a lorry, the pips of a box that wanted more money. He did not say anything, and she had not left him anywhere to say it.

'She gave me a message for you and I'm going to give it to you word for word, because she said the wording was the point. She came to the house. She knocked. She spoke to me in my kitchen with my daughter in the room and nothing happened. That is her way of doing this. It isn't everyone's.' She let that sit for the length of one pip. 'I don't know what that means, Jonas. You do. I could hear you not telling me what it meant from here.'

'Laura—'

'You're going to come here. Now. Tonight. Not Sunday, not when it's safe, not when you've finished whatever you're doing. You get here tonight and you sit at this table and you tell me all of it, from the start, and I decide what we do, because I'm the one in the house. And if you're not here by the time I go to bed, I'm not waiting for eight o'clock this time. I ring the police myself, from this phone, and I give them her card and your name and I tell them everything I know, which is nothing, and that's your fault too. And this time I stay in the queue.'

'Laura—'

'Say yes.'

Three seconds. She counted those as well.

'Yes,' he said.

She put the phone down before he could say anything else, because her hands had started again, and she stood in her mother's hall with the card in her pocket and Elsie banging the tray in the kitchen and found that she was not frightened any more. She was something else. She would have to wait until he was in front of her to find out what.
