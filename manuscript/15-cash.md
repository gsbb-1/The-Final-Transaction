# Chapter 15: Cash

Second sitting. A room with a bed, a chair, a television bolted to the wall and a window onto a yard. Monday night. Same number as the bench, because it is the same day, whatever it feels like.

4.30 came while I was in the first notebook. I looked at the watch at 4.21 and then I was at entry #41 and it was 4.52. I did not decide to let the deadline go. I read through it. I want that down honestly, because later I will want it to have been a decision.

So the offer is not an offer. She said so, and she is a woman whose sentences come true.

***

Cash first. £300 from a machine on a street I am not naming, at 3.40: the daily limit, and a mark on a map for anyone who asks the bank. <!-- VERIFY: typical UK daily ATM withdrawal limit £300–500 --> I knew that when I put the card in. Then I walked for twenty minutes in the wrong direction before I turned round. It felt less like a film than like being late.

Then the phone. Not mine; mine is in the napkin in the inside pocket and it is staying there. A shop on the road up from the station sold me a phone with buttons and a SIM for £31 in notes and asked for nothing. No name. No card. I have known for twenty years that you can buy a telephone in this country without saying who you are and had never once thought about what that was for. <!-- VERIFY: UK pay-as-you-go SIMs require no identity registration -->

It is not going to ring Laura, or Margaret's house, because of the thing I understood this morning: the police do not need to find my phone. They need to find any phone that rings hers. They ask the network, and the network tells them. So this one does not ring Norfolk. That is a rule. I am writing it down so that at two in the morning it is still a rule.

The hotel is on a street behind King's Cross, in a row of them, each with a star rating in the window that nobody could have awarded. The man at the desk wanted £65 for the night and a name, and wanted the first more than the second. The name was not mine. I had never in my life given a false name to anyone, and it cost nothing. That bothered me more than the room.

The rucksack is on the bed beside me. Nine. I counted them when I came in and again when I got the television to work.

***

The news at six, then at ten, with the rolling channel in between because I could not stop. Three things I did not have this afternoon.

One. The member is named. The newsreader said the name I have not written, twice, and a man outside its building said it a third time. People were coming out of the doors with boxes at six in the evening. The man with the grey hair and the plant was the head of their collateral desk, who paid us late on every bad day for four years and rang me once, on a Friday, to say timing, and I wrote it down. I did not know he had a plant.

Two. The pound. There is a number on the screen against the dollar and I have no price for it. I have traded sterling and cleared it and I have a sense in my body of where it goes on a bad day, and this is not that. The man from the bank in the studio said nobody alive had traded it at this level, and he was not being dramatic, he was reading a chart. <!-- VERIFY: "nobody alive has traded" implies below the 1985 and 2022 lows (about $1.05, $1.03); the figure is left off the page deliberately --> The Chancellor is "monitoring the situation closely". That was the whole of the Treasury's contribution to the ten o'clock news. Monitoring. Priya's word for what I did for four years, and now the government's word for what it is doing tonight. I would like it on the record that I noticed.

Three. Me.

Not my name. Not yet. "A senior employee" of Walbrook Clearing House. Walbrook has obtained an interim injunction and an order for the delivery-up of "documents containing confidential information", from a judge, by telephone, on Sunday night. The order was served on Monday. They did not say where. I know where. Some time today a man in a suit stood on a step in Herne Hill with an envelope and nobody came to the door.

Sunday night. While I was under a bridge counting to 300, a judge was on the telephone to Walbrook's lawyers, and Graham, or Haldane, or both, had sworn to something. Graham signs things.

And the word. Theft. The newspaper fronts at ten, held up to the camera: THEFT. CLEARING HOUSE "THEFT" PROBE. One had "£bn" and "theft" in the same line, as though I had taken the money. Nobody in a wig said it. The judge made an order about documents. Walbrook's statement said "unauthorised removal of confidential material". The police "want to speak to" a senior employee. That is the whole of the legal content, and the word on every front page is the one none of them used.

I know why. A man who took something is a story. A clearing house that filed something is not.

I turned it off at 10.31.

***

The payphone is at the end of the road, by the station. I must have walked past it a hundred times, going to trains. It takes coins. I had £4 in coins. <!-- VERIFY: BT payphones remaining near King's Cross; coin-operated; call charges -->

9.15. Margaret's landline. Four rings and Laura picked up, which I had not prepared for, because this morning it had rung 14 times into an empty house.

'It's me.'

Nothing for a count of two. Then: 'Where are you?'

'London. I'm all right. Are you—'

'Where in London?'

'I'd rather not say on this.'

'On what? What are you on?'

'A payphone.'

'A payphone.' She said it the way she says a drug a patient has bought off the internet. 'Jonas. You said eight. This morning.'

'I rang at 8.31. Nobody—'

'I was on my mobile to the police, Jonas. Your call came in on top of it and I let it ring. Mum was in the garden, and she doesn't hear the phone from the garden. In four years.'

I did not say anything. She was not finished, and I have learned this week what it sounds like when someone is not finished.

'I rang them. The police. I said I would and I did, at ten past eight. I was in a queue for 40 minutes with Elsie on my knee and the radio on in Mum's kitchen, and your firm came on the radio, and then the word theft, and I put the phone down. Not because I believed you. Because I didn't know whose side I'd be ringing.' <!-- VERIFY: 101 non-emergency queue times; plausible but unconfirmed -->

'Laura—'

'I don't know what you've done. Do you understand that? I have your word, and a bag I packed in eleven minutes, and a one-year-old in a cot that was mine, and the news. I had the news before I had you.'

'I had to switch the phone off.'

'Then tell me why. No. Not on a payphone.' A breath. I could hear the kitchen behind her. A tap. 'Is she real? The woman in the park?'

'Yes.'

'Does she know where we are?'

'I don't think so.'

'You don't think so.'

'I don't know how she'd know. I haven't rung you from anything but this.'

'From a payphone, so that nobody can find your wife.' She laughed, once, not a laugh. 'Do you hear it? Do you hear what you sound like?'

I heard it. I said, 'Yes.'

'Elsie's fine. A bad night and a good day. Mum thinks you've had a breakdown, and I haven't told her anything that would stop her thinking it, because I don't have anything.'

'I'll explain. Everything. Not on this.'

'You keep saying that.' The coins were going. 'Ring tomorrow. Make a time and be on the end of it.'

'Nine.'

'Nine. And Jonas. If you're not on the end of it at nine, I'm ringing them again, and this time I'm staying in the queue.'

She put the phone down before I did. 9.23. Eight minutes. I stood in the box with the receiver in my hand until a man outside knocked on the glass.

She is safe. She is angrier than I have ever heard her, and I had thought I had heard it. There was no heat in it. That is what I mean. A doctor giving a diagnosis she has already checked twice.

***

Then I came back here and read.

Notebook one. Entry #1 is a Tuesday. Two lines about a settlement fail at a member I will not name and the word "timing" with a question mark after it. I was 36. I had written the word down because the man on the phone had said it too easily.

About 150 entries, five months. Two hours. I did not get up.

Here is what I had not seen, because I had never read them. I have gone back to a day, a week, a month, and copied out #212, and looked things up. I have never read my own record from the first page as though someone else had written it, and tonight someone else had: a man of 36 who did not know yet what he was looking at.

He was counting the wrong thing. He thought he was logging fails. Each entry is a fail, a late payment, a cash-for-bonds substitution, a member code, a date, a stock. One at a time, with nothing joining them. One day, one entry, no cross-references. A ledger, not a map.

But read 150 of them in one sitting and the names repeat, and not randomly. By entry #90 I had stopped reading and started tallying on the back of the hotel's card, and by the end of the book there were seven members whose codes came up on more than one bad day, and three whose codes came up on every bad day, the same three, in the same order. The member on the news tonight is one of the three. It was there at #38.

If the first five months give three names in order, the other eight books give the rest, and I think I know, without having read them yet, what the number is. I have had it in my head for two years without writing it down, because writing it down would have been a claim and I do not make claims. Twelve. Twelve members whose bonds are promised the furthest beyond what they have. The one that failed this morning was not the weakest. It was first in the queue. The order has been in the books the whole time, one day at a time, so that nobody, including me, ever had to look at it whole.

That is what her client wanted. Not the fails. The order.

I cannot prove any of this. I want that on the same page as the number. I have fingerprints, four years of them, and a tally on the back of a hotel card. I do not have one chain. I have never seen one bond doing two jobs; I have seen what it leaves behind. A good lawyer would take this apart in an afternoon and be right to. It is a map drawn by a man who has never been to the country.

But the first name on it failed at 7.58 this morning, and I knew it would, and I knew it would be first.

Eight books to go. The light in this room is not good. I am going to read until I can't.

Elsie. Your mother is right. I sound like a man in a box.
