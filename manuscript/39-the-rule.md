# Chapter 39: The rule

The page had a date on it and a word under the date, and on Tuesday afternoon, eight days after she had written them, Dana Kessler put a sheet of paper beneath the word and the job was done.

It was one sheet. A confirmation: the document two parties send each other after a trade so that each has, in writing, what the other thinks it agreed. A swap, on this one; she did not need to understand the swap, and did not try. She needed the four things a confirmation always carried, and it carried them. The counterparty: Greywether Capital LLP, Mayfair. The other party: a company with a name that said nothing and a correspondence address in a Gulf capital. The broker who had stood between them, a firm whose name she had first heard on the Monday before last, in a room with no screens, in a sentence beginning *two of my brokers rang me*. And the trade date. Nine days before the ship. <!-- VERIFY: a swap confirmation would show counterparties, trade date, effective date and the intermediating broker; whether a broker's back-office copy would carry both principals' names depends on whether the broker acted as agent; invented here and left vague -->

She had not stolen it. She wanted to be exact about that, even to herself. A woman she had trained, eleven years ago, in a building that had no name on the door either, now ran a middle office at the broker and had never once in eleven years been asked for anything. Kessler had asked on Thursday. The envelope had come by hand on Tuesday at ten past two, with nothing written on it, and would never be discussed again by either of them, and that was the sum of what Kessler owed anyone.

The company with the empty name had taken four days. It sat in a jurisdiction whose register gave a registered agent and nothing else, and the agent's other companies, pulled one at a time, had given a director's name on a dormant one, and the director's name had given, on the third page of a newspaper's society column in that capital, a wedding, and the wedding had given the family. A private office. A cousin of the managing director of the fund whose brass plate the subject had walked up to on Monday week. The two men whose names she had, one from a brass plate in St James's and one from four days of registers, were related. <!-- VERIFY: offshore company registers (e.g. BVI, Cayman) show registered agent only; tracing through the agent's other vehicles to a named director is plausible but slow; invented -->

She did not write the cousin's name in the file. She did not need it. The question on the page was *what did he know, and when*, and the sheet of paper answered it in the one way a client could not argue with, which was in his own counterparty's hand, dated.

Sebastian Crane had taken the other side of a trade on the one date that only mattered if you knew why it mattered. He had known. Eight days ago, in the room, he had called it a coincidence in the voice he used for sentences he had rehearsed, and she had written the word *before* and left the page blank beneath it, and now the page was not blank.

She sat with it for a quarter of an hour. Below her the dry cleaner's steam press thumped through the floor. The one of her three who did not do surveillance was in the next room photocopying, which was the only sound in the firm, because the other two had not been in since Friday.

That was the other thing she knew, and had known since Saturday morning, when the older of them had rung her from a car and told her, in the voice of a man reading his own custody sheet, what Mr Crane had asked them to do on Friday night and what they had done. A stairwell at a newspaper. A girl on the stairs. A wrist, and the sound a head makes. They had not got the page. He had rung her, she thought, because he wanted to have told someone before it was in a newspaper, and she had let him finish and said, 'Thank you. Don't come in,' and he had not. Crane had paid them directly, out of a drawer, and they had taken it, and the thing she had told herself for six years, that her people were hers, had turned out to be a thing she had told herself. <!-- VERIFY: ch. 34 is not yet drafted; the stairwell, the wrist and the head injury follow the brief; the detail that the older of her two men rang her on Saturday morning is new -->

She had not reported any of it to anyone, because there was nobody to report it to. That was private work. The pay was better and nobody asked for a report, and nobody, it turned out, read one either.

The radio on the windowsill had been on all day with the sound low. At noon she had turned it up for a man called Haldane saying that his clearing house might not be in a position to meet its obligations, and had turned it down again, because she did not care about bonds and never had. She cared about the sentence a man used when he had decided something. Haldane had sounded like a man reading a sentence somebody else had decided.

***

The phone in her bag rang at 3.40. The number was the one in his drawer.

'Dana.'

'Mr Crane.'

'You've been quiet.' He sounded well. He sounded like a man who had been given a day he had designed. 'I'd assumed you were sulking about Friday and I've been prepared to allow it, because you've earned it. You'll have seen the news.'

'I've heard it.'

'Then you know we are nearly finished. Nearly. I don't like nearly.' A pause in which she could hear him choose not to look at a screen. 'Whitlock went to the Treasury on Sunday with the Gulf fund and a plan, and was refused. I know that because the woman who refused him has been telling people since, which is her way of making it true. A man refused on Sunday does not go home. He has until tomorrow morning, and he has a buyer who has been recalled and may not stay recalled, and I have spent two years and a very great deal of money not having to care what one clerk does with his evenings. I would like him to stop. Tonight. There is exactly one thing he will stop for, and you know where it is.'

She waited. He did not fill the silence; he never had. So she filled it, because she had decided on the stairs eight days ago that when he said it, she would make him have said it.

'Say what you want, Mr Crane.'

'Bring me the child.'

Four words. He said them in the tone he had used for the figure, and for *by whatever route works*. No weight anywhere; that was where the weight was. She found that she had known for eight days exactly what the words would be and had still wanted to hear whether he would say them, and that he had, and that it had cost him nothing.

'No.'

'Dana—'

'I said it to you on a motorway and I said it in your room and you did not say no on either occasion, and I noticed, and I kept the job anyway, which is mine to answer for. This one is yours. I don't work that way with children. It isn't a price. You've listened for the price twice. There isn't one.'

'Then I'll find someone who will.'

'You already have. On Friday. Out of your drawer.' She kept her voice where she kept it. 'They were good men for watching doors, and you've made them something else, and when it goes wrong, which it will, because they are not built for it, you will say you never gave an instruction, and there will be no paper that says you did. I know how that goes. I used to work for people who did it to me with a pension attached.'

A silence of a different kind.

'You understand,' he said, quite gently, 'that you are no longer retained.'

'I understood that at twenty to four.'

'And that anything you may believe you know—'

'Goodbye, Mr Crane.'

She ended the call, and took the back off the phone, and took the SIM card out, and sat with the two pieces on the desk in front of her next to the confirmation. It was 3.46. She noticed the time because it was the time, two and a half weeks ago, that an analyst had logged in at a desk that was not hers, and because she noticed things. <!-- VERIFY: ch. 20 gives 3.46pm Friday for Priya's login; Kessler would know the time from Crane's account of Pell's lunch, ch. 18, which gave the afternoon, not the minute; keep or cut -->

***

The question was where to put it.

She went through the list the way she went through registers: in order, without hope, so that hope could not choose for her.

The police would take the sheet, thank her, and file it in a case about a stolen rucksack, which she would be in. The Bank of England would read it on Thursday. The newspaper would want a second source and a lawyer, and Crane's lawyers had been writing to that newspaper since Sunday week. The Gulf fund already knew; it had asked the brokers for the dates itself, on a Friday, which was how she had known where to look. Any of them would be rid of it. None of them would do what she wanted done, which she had not until that moment allowed herself to name, and which was to hurt him.

She had not liked him. She had thought that did not matter, and in fifteen years of government work it had not, because the thing she served had not been a man. It mattered now. He had read four years of a stranger's evenings and told her, when she brought them, that he would *read* them, as if that were an answer; and she had stood at the door and watched him enjoy saying it. He had found the man in the books contemptible. She had read enough of the books, in a safe, overnight, to know what the man was, and it was not that.

There was one person Crane had never once, in all of it, considered as an actor. The man had been a bag to be taken and a clerk to be despised and now a nuisance to be stopped by Wednesday. He had never been to Crane a man who might be handed something and use it.

That was where it would hurt.

She photocopied the sheet herself, twice, and put one copy in the file and the original in her coat, and she said to the one of her three who was still hers that the firm was closed for the day and would possibly be closed for longer, and that there would be money for the month, and she watched the woman decide not to ask. Then she went down the stairs past the dry cleaner's and into the street, and did not look for the grey car, because the grey car had been Crane's since Friday too.

***

Kentish Town. She knew the door; she had paid two men to watch it for a fortnight. A street door with three bells and a flat on the second floor with the curtains drawn in daylight, which was the only flat in the street with its curtains drawn. She did not ring. She knocked, twice, with a knuckle, and stood back a full pace with her hands out of her pockets, and waited for the time it takes a frightened man to decide, which she had measured often and which was longer than people thought.

The door opened on a chain. He had a kitchen knife in his left hand, held low, as a man holds a thing he has never used and does not intend to, and she looked at it and then at him and said, 'Mr Whitlock. I'm alone. I have nothing in my hands and I'm going to keep them where you can see them. I'd like five minutes. I'd rather not have them on the step, but I will if you prefer.'

'My wife said you said that.'

'I expect I did.'

He looked at her for a while longer. He was thinner than in the park and his coat had been slept in, and she saw him do the thing she had seen him do at a cloakroom counter, which was count, and arrive at a number, and decide.

He took the chain off.

The flat was one room and a kitchen. A table with paper on it, face down. A television in the corner with the sound off showing a correspondent in rain. She stood where he put her, by the door, and did not look around, and did not sit.

'I'm not here to apologise,' she said. 'I want that said first, so that you don't wait for it. I took your notebooks. It was the job, and I did it well, and I'd do the same job again for a different client. I'm here because I've found something that belongs with you more than with anyone else I can think of, and because this afternoon I ran out of other people to give it to.'

She took the envelope from her coat with two fingers and put it on the corner of the table nearest to her, and took her hand away.

'A trade confirmation. Greywether Capital on one side; a company on the other that belongs to a private office in a Gulf capital, run by a cousin of a man you met at a hotel. The broker's name is on it. So is the date. Nine days before the ship.' She watched his face and saw that none of it was new to him except the paper. 'You knew. The fund knew. Nobody could put it on a table. That can go on a table.'

'Why.'

'Because you're the one he isn't afraid of,' she said. 'He has read every page you ever wrote and he thinks you are a man who writes things down. I think so too, as it happens. I'd like to see what happens when he's wrong.'

He did not touch the envelope. He said, 'Where are they?'

'His office. Mayfair, the quiet street; you know it from the paper, I expect. The room on the first floor with no screens. A steel cabinet behind the desk, locked, and he carries the key. I would look there first. They are in it, nine of them, in order, with your page folded in the ninth, thirty pages from the end, where you left it. He has read all of them.' She let that stand. 'I cannot get them back. Cannot, Mr Whitlock, not will not. I don't do safes. The two men I would have sent are his now, and have been since Friday, and you know what they did on Friday better than I do.'

He had gone very still at *Friday*. She had expected it and did not look away from it.

'He rang me at twenty to four,' she said, 'and asked me to bring him your daughter. Those words. I said no. He said he would find someone who would, and he already has. I don't know where your wife is, and I want you to notice that I'm telling you I don't, because the last time I declined to answer a question of yours you spent four days hating me for the wrong reason.' A pause. 'Whoever does know should move her by tomorrow morning.'

'Why are you telling me.'

'Because I have a rule,' Kessler said, 'and I've just found out what it costs, and I would like it to have been worth something.'

She buttoned her coat. He had not moved from the table and the knife was still in his hand, forgotten, pointed at the floor.

'Your wife let me in because I said "won't" and not "wouldn't",' she said. 'She was right to notice. You've a good deal in common, the two of you. Goodnight, Mr Whitlock.'

She let herself out, and shut the door the way you shut a door in a house where a child might be asleep, and went down two flights of a stranger's stairs into a street where, for the first time in six years, nobody was waiting to be told what she had seen.
