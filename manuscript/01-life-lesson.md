# Chapter 1: Life lesson

Turning 40th. 25-30 were good, but pointless years. 30-35 had some growth and still floating around. 35-40... When 37 it was three years, then by 39 life has different meaning.

That is the sum. I have been looking at it for ten minutes and I am not going to improve it. A life in five-year blocks, like a quarterly report with the footnotes left off. I know where the footnotes are.

Thirty-nine years and 364 days. Fourteen thousand six hundred and nine of them, if you count days, and I count days. Tomorrow the number has a nought on the end and people will send cards about it. Tonight it is just a number one short of another number, which is the kind of thing I notice and the kind of thing nobody else does, and that, if I am honest, is the whole of my career.

This is entry 1,399. I put the number at the top of the page the way I have put it at the top of the 1,398 before it, and then I sat and did not write, which I never do, because I had the idea that a man should put down something worth keeping on the last night of his thirties. Then I wrote what I wrote. Four lines. Good but pointless. Floating. Dots. Different meaning. I am not a writer. I am a man who writes things down, which is not the same thing, and I have never pretended it was. I do not sign these. A signature is a claim, and these are not claims.

Laura is downstairs. The oven door has gone twice, which means she is doing something for tomorrow that she believes I do not know about. I know about it. There has been a leg of lamb in the fridge since Saturday, under a tea towel, like a body. Tom and Aisha are coming at half past seven and Tom will bring a bottle that costs more than it should and a card that makes a noise. I will be surprised at the right moment. It is one of the things six years of marriage teaches you: when to be surprised, and how.

Elsie went down at 7.50 and has not stirred. Fifty weeks and five days. Nine point six kilos on Thursday, on the scales at the surgery, Laura holding her and the nurse reading the number and me writing it down on the back of my hand because I did not have a book with me, which I regretted. Six teeth. She stood in the cot this morning holding the rail with both hands and said something that Laura swears was Dad and I swear was Da, and we have agreed not to argue about it for a year.

I will come back to Elsie. I always come back to Elsie, and if anyone ever reads these books in order they will see the exact entry where that started, and they will not need me to explain it.

***

Twenty-five to thirty, then, since I have raised it.

A desk on a trading floor, the third from the window, and a screen with eight colours on it, and a man next to me called Tom who is coming to dinner tomorrow and still thinks of me as the one who read the footnotes. Good money. Better money than I have had since, if you count it the way the people on that floor counted it, which is by the year, and I stopped counting it that way at about twenty-eight when I noticed I did not own anything I would miss. A flat I did not like. A car I did not drive. Nights that ended at four and started at six, and I was good at them, and I can remember perhaps nine of them. Good years. Pointless years. The two words sit together perfectly well and I have never understood why people think they should not.

Thirty to thirty-five. I moved from the desk to the room behind the desk, where the people sit who tell the desk what it may not do, and the money went down and the hours went down and I was, for the first time, interested. Three employers in five years. Risk at a bank, risk at a broker, risk at a bank again. Each time I was hired for being careful and each time I left because being careful was wanted on the brochure and not in the room. Growth, I wrote. Some. Still floating around. That is accurate. I knew what I was looking at by thirty-four and I did not yet know what to do about it, and the honest word for that condition is floating.

Thirty-five. Walbrook.

I will say what Walbrook is, once, because I have never said it in these books and if the books are ever read by anyone but me they will need it. When two banks make a promise to each other about money, and the promise lasts for years, they do not trust each other to keep it. They put Walbrook in the middle. Walbrook promises each of them that the other will pay, and to make that promise good, Walbrook takes a deposit from both: cash, or government bonds, held in Walbrook's own account where nobody can touch them. Every morning before seven an engine runs and works out how much each promise has moved overnight, and by nine every member has either paid the difference or is in default. In five years I have never seen a default. My job is to look at the deposits. Not the promises; the deposits. Whether they are what they say they are, whether they are where they say they are, whether they would still be there on a bad morning. I decide if the deposits are real. That is the short answer, and tomorrow night at dinner Tom will ask me what I do and I will give it to him and he will say it mostly is, isn't it, Jonas, and I will say not yet, and he will laugh, because he has heard me say it for fifteen years.

Thirty-six. A Tuesday, as it happens, like tonight. A member's bonds, promised to us, did not arrive, and the member said timing, and paid the nine o'clock in cash it had not had at seven, and at ten past three the bonds turned up as if nothing had happened. I wrote it down that night on the first page of a black notebook I bought at lunchtime for the purpose. One page. A question mark. I did not know I was starting anything. I thought I was clearing my head.

One thousand three hundred and ninety-eight pages later I can tell you what I write. I write the date and the day. I write the member codes, four letters each, Walbrook's own, which mean nothing outside the building and which I have never once translated in these pages. I write the time each one's bank confirmed and what each one had in its deposit at seven and what it had at three and which lines were on the fails list and whether anybody said timing. I do not write names. I do not write the numbers from the systems, only the shape the numbers make. And the shape, on bad mornings, is always the same shape: bonds that fail to arrive in pairs, cash that was not there at seven and is there at nine, bonds that arrive in the afternoon and are gone again by the next run. One bond doing two jobs. I have never seen it. Nobody can see it; a government bond is a government bond, there are thirty billion pounds of some of those lines in issue and ours is not marked. I see what it leaves behind. Ninety-four times, by tonight's count. Ninety-four entries where the fingerprints say that something standing behind a promise to Walbrook is standing behind a promise to somebody else at the same time, and that on a sharp enough morning both promises will be called in and one of them will not be kept.

I think. I cannot prove it. I have written that sentence, or one like it, most weeks for three years, and I write it again now so that nobody reading this later can say I did not know the difference.

***

Thirty-seven. Three years.

I wrote it up. Six pages, Walbrook letterhead, a title I was proud of for about a day. I estimated, in a sentence I can still recite, that the system had approximately three years before a moderate shock became unmanageable. I gave it to Graham, who read it with the door shut and said he would take it forward, and took it forward with a covering note that called it one analyst's view, and it went to the Treasury, who stamped it and filed it, and I know that because Graham told me, kindly, over a coffee he paid for, as if it were good news. He was relieved. I will put down something I have never put down: so was I. A man who has been proved right has to do something about it. A man who has been filed can go home and write it in a book.

Three years ago this month. The three years are up. I have spent the last few weeks waiting for that to mean something and it has meant nothing. Nothing happens. The call goes out at seven and is met by nine. Two members pay late on bad days and say timing. The register goes from grey to green, forty lines, every morning, and I go home. I have begun to wonder whether being early and being wrong look the same from the inside, and I have not found a way to tell them apart that does not involve waiting, and I am very good at waiting. It is the thing about me that nobody would put on a card.

Thirty-five to forty, I wrote, and then three dots.

The dots are deliberate. There are things that do not go in here, and I know what every one of them is, and the people they concern have not agreed to be written down. The dots are the only honest way to leave a gap.

Thirty-nine. Different meaning. I will not write what I mean by it tonight, because I know exactly what I mean and it will not fit on the last page of a decade, and because she is asleep eleven feet from where I am sitting and I would rather go and look at her than describe her. The entry where it started is in the seventh book. Anyone who reads in order will find it without my help.

***

Nine books. Eight of them are full and live in a steel toolbox on a shelf in a garage in Lewisham that my father rented for twenty-two years to keep tools in, and which I have kept on since he died because I could not think how to stop, and because by the time I thought of it the box was already there. Laura believes I go to the garage to sit with his things. I do go to sit. The ninth book comes home every night in the bag she calls the briefcase, and every morning before it goes back in the bag I photograph the night's page and send the picture to myself, so that if the book is ever lost there is a copy somewhere that is not in a garage in Lewisham. I have done that 1,398 times without thinking about it. Tomorrow will be 1,399. I will do it in the kitchen with a child on my hip and I will not check the address, because in 1,398 mornings I have never once had to.

Today was a Tuesday. Nothing failed. I wrote that down and it took one line and I was pleased with it in a way I would not try to explain to anyone.

Tomorrow I will be forty, and the call will go out at seven, and the register will go green, and nothing will happen, which is the thing I have spent three years saying would not last. Then I will go home and be surprised by a leg of lamb.

Elsie. Your father is thirty-nine for another eight hours, if you let him sleep. He was right about something once, for three years, and it made no difference to anyone. He is writing that down so that it is written down. That is what he does.
