# Chapter 41: Without authority

The council would sit at ten in the morning, home time, which was eight o'clock in London, and the first item would be the decree. Khalid Al-Mansouri had been told so on Monday by a man who had rung to be kind, and he had thanked him, and had then asked Nasser to confirm it by another route, and Nasser had. From that minute his signature would be the signature of a private man. The flight was at noon. Nasser had booked it on Friday, within an hour of the call that had asked him home "for consultations", because Nasser booked things.

He had not gone on Friday. He had said that there was business in London that could not be left, which was true, and the men at home had let him say it, because it suited them that he should be in London when the decree was read and not in the room. Since Monday the office in St James's had told everyone who asked, including Mr Whitlock, that the managing director was not in London, which was the sentence it had always used and which had the advantage of being believed; a man who has been recalled and is seen to stay is a man defying, and a man on an aeroplane is nobody's business. He had let the aeroplane stand. He had used the four days as he had intended to use them. On Sunday he had sat in a room on Horse Guards Road with a man wanted by the police and put the transaction to the Chancellor of the Exchequer in plain words, and she had said no in a sentence he could have written for her, and he had carried that sentence back to Park Lane in his head and found that it was the same sentence the four men used. It is not politics. It is prudence. He had heard it in two capitals in one week, in two languages, and in both it had meant: not on my signature.

Now it was Tuesday night, and the sound was on.

He did not usually have the sound on. He had the channels on the far wall of the bridge for the maps and the faces. Tonight, in the suite on Park Lane, he had sat in the chair where Whitlock had sat a week ago and listened to the chief executive of Walbrook Clearing House say at noon, in front of his own building, that the clearing house might not be in a position to meet its obligations, and at twenty to seven he had listened to a correspondent standing in the rain in Threadneedle Street read the words "resolution powers" from a statement with no name on it, and he had let the sound stay on afterwards because it was the only voice in the room that would say the figures aloud.

Whitlock's second member had failed at two minutes past ten. Whitlock's order. He had watched it happen in the sequence a man had recited to him from memory in this room, and had found that he no longer had any interest in whether the order could be proved.

***

Nasser came at half past nine with the evening sheet and the look he had worn since Friday, which was the look of a man carrying something he had not been asked to put down.

'Sit,' Khalid said. 'Leave the sheet. I know what is on it.'

Nasser sat. He did not leave the sheet; he put it face down on the table between them, squared to the edge, which was his way of saying that it was there.

'I am going to tell you what I intend to do,' Khalid said, 'and then I am going to tell you what it will cost you, and then you may say no. I would like you to hear the second part before you answer the first.'

'Sir.'

'The transaction we built on Sunday. The fund buys what the twelve are forced to sell, in Mr Whitlock's order, at the open, through our three banks. The Bank of England lends against what we buy, the same morning, so that we are not simply the next men holding bonds. The Treasury stands behind the Bank. On Sunday the Chancellor declined to stand behind anything. Without her there is no Bank; without the Bank there is no borrowing; without the borrowing the fund carries, alone, for as long as it takes, everything it buys.' He laid it out as he had laid out the instruction on a Saturday, in order, for the desks. 'I intend to do it anyway.'

Nasser's face did not move, which was how Khalid knew that he had already thought of it.

'The cash from the sales is where I put it a fortnight ago, which is anywhere that is not London or New York. It can be in London by seven. Three transfers, tonight, to the three banks, into the accounts they already hold for us. The banks will accept the money; banks accept money. At the open they buy. Our desk has the list from Sunday, the names and the sizes. What it does not have is the pace: which line goes next, and how fast, and that is in one man, and he gives it on the morning, in the room, as he said he would. Not before.'

'And when the banks ask whether the instruction is authorised.'

'It is authorised. Until eight o'clock it is authorised by the man who runs the fund. The charter says that an instruction of that size goes to the board, and the board has not met since Friday, and will not meet before the council, because the council is the point. Nobody at home will countermand it before eight, because nobody at home will know. 'If it works.'

'If it works, Nasser, the fund will have bought the bottom of the British government bond market with the nation's money and the market will have turned on the fact, and the men who read the decree at ten will spend the afternoon explaining that it was their idea. I shall be forgiven. I may even be thanked. That is the easy case.'

'And if it does not.'

'Then at eight o'clock a private citizen will be found to have moved a great deal of his country's money into the one jurisdiction his country has spent a fortnight removing it from, into a market that is failing, on the word of a man wanted by the police, the night before he was replaced. There is a word for that at home. It is not "prudence".' He let the sentence finish. 'I will not go home. That is the cost to me, and I have looked at it, and it is mine.'

He had looked at it in the small hours of Monday, after the kind man's call, and had found it smaller than he had expected. He had been educated in two countries that were not his and had spent his life being trusted fully by neither; he had learned to live in rooms like this one. His mother was dead. His father's house would go to the family. He had one client, and it had been taken from him by a decree drafted by his cousin's friends, and he found, examining himself with the care he brought to a column of figures, that the client was still the client whether or not it wanted him.

***

'The second thing,' he said. 'The family's money.'

Nasser did look up at that.

'My father's branch. Not Faisal's.' A small dry movement of the mouth. 'The accounts you know, and two you do not, which my father kept in London for forty years for exactly this kind of night without ever saying what kind of night he meant. It goes in first. Before the fund's money. Into the same accounts, tonight, documented, so that whatever is bought in the morning is bought with my father's money until my father's money is gone, and only then with the nation's. If it fails, the first loss is mine. When the four men ask whose money was risked, the answer is in the bank's records before they ask.'

'It will not be enough to matter, sir. Not against the size of the morning.'

'It is not meant to matter. It is meant to be first.' He turned the evening sheet over at last and did not read it. 'A man who asks a country to risk its money should have risked his own, and been seen to. I learned that in London. I learned it from the other side.'

Nasser was silent for a moment. Then he said, 'And the cost to me.'

'Yes.' Khalid folded his hands. 'You will make the calls. Three banks, tonight, on recorded lines, to men who will be woken to take them. You will tell them the transfers are coming and that the fund will be a buyer at the open and that the instruction is authorised, and they will ask you, because they are careful men, whether anything has changed in the fund's governance that they should know of, and you will say no. It will be your voice. Not mine. I will sign the paper; you will be the man on the tape saying nothing has changed, twelve hours before the decree says otherwise. If it fails, I stay here. You cannot. Your wife and your sons are at home. You will go home on Thursday and be asked, by men who already know the answer, why you said what you said.' <!-- Nasser's family (a wife and sons at home) is new; nothing in the ledger about his family. 'Three,' Nasser said. 'The boys. You have never asked.'

'I have never asked. I am telling you now that I know, so that you cannot say afterwards that I did not.' He unfolded his hands. 'You may refuse. I will make the calls myself, in my own voice, and the banks will do it more slowly because a managing director ringing at eleven at night is a thing they notice, and perhaps it will cost us the first hour of the morning. That is a price I can pay. I can also write, now, tonight, that you objected and were overruled. It would be true.'

Nasser looked at the sheet, and at the window with the park gone black behind it, and at the carpet, and Khalid let him, because he had been with him seven years ago when a younger man had signed another paper against a man in Mayfair, and had carried that one out of the room as well.

'A paper saying I objected,' Nasser said eventually, 'is a paper saying I knew what it was and made the calls anyway. That is worse than the calls. No.' He stood, and picked up the evening sheet, and squared it against his palm. 'I will make the calls. I will say nothing has changed. It will be true for nine hours, and I have said truer things for less.'

'Nasser.'

'Sir.'

'Thank you. I will not say it again tonight. There will not be time.'

***

The instruction was two pages. Khalid wrote it himself, by hand, as he had not done since he was a young man at a desk in a bank that no longer existed, because he wanted the hand on it to be unarguable. The three banks. The accounts. The sums, which he wrote in figures and did not say aloud, as he never said them. The order of the family's money and the fund's. The sentence authorising the purchases "as directed on the morning by the fund's adviser, Mr J. Whitlock, or on his behalf by the chief of staff". He read it through twice and changed one word, as he had on Saturday, and signed it at 10.52 and dated it, with the year and the day, because dates were everything this month and he wanted this one on paper in his own hand.

'The flight,' Nasser said at the door, with the pages in his hand. 'Noon. Shall I cancel it?'

Khalid considered the question honestly, as it deserved.

'No. Leave it. Either I shall be on it as the man who brought the money home, or I shall not be on it at all. Those are the only two seats.' He turned back to the screen, where the man in the rain was being replayed for the fourth time. 'Ring Mr Whitlock's number when the banks are done. Tonight, not in the morning. Tell him the fund will be a buyer at the open, with the Bank or without it. Tell him I should like to know where to put it.' A pause. 'And tell him I am in London. The office has said otherwise to him twice, on my instruction, and he is a man who keeps count.'

Nasser went. Khalid listened to the door close and sat for a moment with his hands still on the table, and then, because there was nothing else to do until seven, he turned the sound off at last, and watched the smoke on the far wall, which had not moved in nineteen days.
