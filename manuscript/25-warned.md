# Chapter 25: Warned

Sunday. Margaret's kitchen. Two sittings, I expect. The first is before anyone is up.

The cheap phone buzzed at 5.58 on the windowsill where I had left it to get a signal. One line, from the only person I have given the number to. *Up.*

I did not go and look. I sat with it for a while, because it was the last while in which I was a man in a kitchen and not a man in a newspaper. It felt like a kitchen.

Laura came down at 6.20 with Elsie on her hip and her phone in her other hand, already lit. She had heard the buzz through the floor. She put Elsie in the high chair, put the phone face up on the table between us and said, 'Well. Read it.'

So I read it. Then I read it again, with my finger on the screen, because the first time my eyes had gone to the wrong things.

The headline is five words. *Treasury was warned three years ago.* Below it, a picture of the first page of my memo, at an angle, so the reader can see the letterhead and the stamp: RECEIVED, HM TREASURY, the date, and in biro the two words. *Noted. File.* The footer is not in the picture. On Friday afternoon, at his table, waiting for five o'clock, I let him photograph the first page with my thumb across the bottom of it. He saw me do it and did not ask. One story a week, Jonas, he said. The footer is still the only thing I own that nobody else knows about, and a journalist in Kentish Town now knows that there is something under my thumb.

The story has 1,014 words. I counted them because I count. It says that a six-page memorandum written by Walbrook's head of collateral risk, three years ago this month, and received by the Treasury a week later, estimated that the system had approximately three years before a moderate shock became unmanageable. It quotes the sentence. It says the memo was sent to the Treasury by Walbrook with a covering note describing it as one analyst's view. It says that the author of the memo is understood to be the person sought by police for questioning in connection with a complaint by Walbrook, and that Walbrook has obtained a High Court order against him for the return of documents. It does not name him. It says the paper is not naming him for legal reasons. I rang Calloway from the box by the war memorial at five to eight yesterday, before the shop had opened, and told him to run it, and to run my name with it. He said the lawyers would have a view on the name. They had a view. He said it would last until somebody else said it.

Then, two-thirds down, a Treasury spokesperson. *The Chancellor did not see the document. Correspondence of this kind is handled at official level in the normal way and is not routinely put to ministers. The Treasury does not comment on matters that may be the subject of police inquiries.*

I believe her. I want that on the page. I think she had not read it when Carrow slid it across the table, and has not since. She had the phrase. Nobody who had read the tables would have needed the phrase. And it does not matter. The stamp is the Treasury's. The biro is the Treasury's. The person who wrote *Noted* was paid by the Treasury to note things, and he noted, and the Chancellor did not see it, and all three of those are true at once. That is what a department is. I have worked inside institutions for fifteen years. The Chancellor did not see it the way Walbrook did not see it: on purpose, in the normal way.

Laura read it standing, with Elsie's spoon in one hand. She read it faster than I did.

'Three years ago this month,' she said.

'Yes.'

'You'd have been thirty-seven.' She did not look up from the screen. 'I remember thirty-seven. You came home one night in the autumn with a face like a man who'd been to a funeral, and I asked, and you said work. I thought it was a bad week. It was the memo, wasn't it. That week.'

I had not put those two things together. Fourteen hundred entries, and I had never once written down what my face did when I got home. She had. She has been keeping a record too. Hers is just not on paper.

She put the spoon in the bowl. 'You're not named.'

'Not yet.'

'How long?'

'Walbrook will do it. They'll want it to be about me by lunchtime.'

She looked at me the way she looks at a reading she has taken herself and does not like. 'You've got a lot better at predicting them,' she said, 'for a man who was wrong about them for four years.'

***

Second sitting. Four in the afternoon. Elsie asleep upstairs and Margaret in the garden, not gardening, where she has been most of the day, I think so that we would have the kitchen.

I was right about lunchtime. I was out by twenty minutes.

8.30. Radio. Margaret's kitchen radio is the kind with a dial, and it was on the station she listens to, and the story was second, after the pound. The presenter read the headline and the Treasury line and then said that a former government lawyer was on the line. I did not catch the name and I am not going to pretend to. He said three things. One: no offence under the Official Secrets Act has been committed by anyone, because the Act does not cover economic information and a note about collateral is not a secret in law, whatever it is in Whitehall. Two: whoever passed the paper to the press, if it was a civil servant, is at risk of their job and not their liberty, and if it was the author, is at risk of nothing at all, because a private citizen cannot leak his own memo. Three, which the presenter had not asked for: that it was worth saying, since the word had been used all week, that information cannot be stolen in this country, there being a case about it from the seventies involving an exam paper, and that what Walbrook had was a civil order, not a crime. <!-- VERIFY: Oxford v Moss (1979), confidential information not property for the Theft Act; the lawyer's summary is as research/information-security.md §2 and §4 describe -->

'Who's he?' Laura said.

'I don't know.'

'He's the first person all week who's said you haven't done anything.'

'He said I haven't committed anything. It's not the same.'

'Jonas.' She turned the radio down. 'Take it.'

9.40. The paper's site updated. *Walbrook: memo "considered and not accepted" at the time.* A statement over Haldane's name. Mr Whitlock's note was considered by the risk committee in the normal way and its conclusions were not accepted by the firm; the firm has since been in dialogue with the Treasury and the Bank on an ongoing basis; the firm will not comment further on an individual who is the subject of legal proceedings. Mr Whitlock. Twice. They had put my name in the second line to make a point about proceedings and the paper had the name it had been waiting for.

Risk committee. The memo went to Graham and Graham sent it to the Treasury with a note calling it one analyst's view. I have never been told it went to a committee. I have noted every risk committee for four years in the books on the floor beside me and I checked, at 9.50, with Laura watching: nothing. Either there was a committee meeting I was not told about, which I would believe, or Haldane has invented one on a Sunday morning, which I would also believe. Both are now on the record and one of them is a lie, and the only man who can show which is the man the record is about.

11.15. Named. The paper, then everyone. By noon it was everywhere Laura looked. By 12.40 there was a photograph. I had not known it existed. A conference, four years ago, the week I started the record, as it happens; somebody's phone; me at a lectern in a tie, mouth open, saying something about haircuts. <!-- VERIFY: the source of the photograph is left vague; the author may prefer a different provenance -->

Laura looked at the photograph for a long time. 'They've chosen one where you look guilty,' she said. 'All men in ties look guilty. It's the collar.'

Elsie reached for the phone. She likes faces. She got it, and I let her, and she held my face in both hands on the kitchen table and put it in her mouth, and Laura took it off her before she could do any damage, and that was the moment I became a headline: watching my daughter try to eat it.

***

Counting. Six pages, three years ago. Five words. One thousand and fourteen. Twenty minutes out. One kitchen.

What has changed since six this morning, in order.

One. Everyone who knows Laura knows that her mother lives in a village near Norwich. Tom and Aisha. The surgery, all of it. Brambles. By this evening all of them will have seen my face in a tie, and some of them will put the two things together, and one of them will say so to somebody. The police do not even need that. They have the car's number, from Monday, if they want it, and the car is outside this house. Norfolk was safe when I was a name nobody knew. I have been named for five hours. The grey car has not been at the end of the lane since Friday morning, and I have stopped reading that as good news. People who stop watching have usually learned what they wanted to know.

Two. The man who wants the books now knows what the man who has them looks like. So does everyone who works for him.

Three. So does the other side. I have spent four years not writing the name of the largest line in my own reports, in my own notebook, that nobody else read. I was proud of that. Several million people have now read that I wrote a memo, and one of them, I would bet the house four times over, works for the fund that pulled its collateral a week ago and knows exactly whose job it was to count it. The one institution in London that is long everything my hunter is short. The one that has lost more this week than anybody, and knows it, and does not know why, and has a London office with a door.

I have never walked through a door and said my name to a stranger and expected it to mean anything. It means something today. It is the only thing I own that went up this morning.

Laura came in while I was writing that. She read the last line over my shoulder, which she has never done in six years, and I let her, which I have never done either.

'You're going back,' she said.

'Tonight. Not the way I came.'

'And do what?'

'Walk into a building in St James's and say who I am.'

She stood there for a bit. Then she went to the drawer where Margaret keeps the envelopes and took out one with cash in it that I had not known about, and put it on the table next to this book.

'Mum's,' she said. 'For the roof. I'll tell her.' And then, because she is Laura, and because she has had a week to think about what she would say when I left again: 'If you're going to be in the paper anyway, be in it for something you said. Not for something they found.'

I have to pack. It is twenty to five. There is a bag open on the bed, and this time it is mine.
