# Chapter 35: The press outside

There were three cars at the end of the lane on Saturday morning and a van with a dish on its roof, and a man standing on the second rung of a stepladder in the field gateway with a lens the length of his forearm, and Laura Whitlock stood at the landing window with Elsie on her hip and counted them the way she had counted the grey car eight days before. She found that counting them did not help. The grey car had been one thing that did not belong. These belonged to the week. They had come on Friday at about one, the first two cars within ten minutes of each other, and by the time the light went there had been seven people at the gate and a woman doing her face in a wing mirror, and this morning there were fewer, because Saturday, and because a lane in Norfolk gives you one picture and they had it.

The picture was on page five. Margaret's neighbour had brought the paper up at eight, because Margaret had not gone down for it, which she had done at eight every morning for the eleven years since Laura's father died. The house, from the lane, with the kitchen light on. Underneath, a sentence about the wife of the Walbrook risk manager being "understood to be staying with relatives in the area". Understood to be. Laura had read it standing at the sink and thought: that is a sentence written by someone who has been told not to say the village, and has said it.

Margaret was at the table with her hands round a mug she had not drunk from. She had been 71 for most of a year and had looked it for about a day and a half. On Friday evening one of them had come up the lane and knocked, a young man with a nice voice, and Margaret had opened the door before Laura could get to the hall, and had said, 'He's a good man, you know. He always rings,' and the young man had thanked her and written it down. Laura had got the door shut. Margaret had sat down in the hall chair and not got up for twenty minutes, and had said, when she did, that she did not know why she had said that, and Laura had said that it was because it was true, and that was what they were for.

Now she said, 'They'll go when there's nothing to see.'

'There's nothing to see now,' her mother said. 'There's me.'

***

He rang at nine. He had rung at nine every morning for twelve days, and she had stopped standing by the phone for it and started being in the room when it happened, which she knew was not the same and had decided not to think about.

She picked up on the first ring. 'Yes.'

'It's me.'

Different, behind him. Not a road. A hard floor, a trolley with a wheel that needed looking at, a voice on a tannoy asking somebody to go somewhere, the particular hum of a corridor that was lit all night. She had spent six years of her life in that sound and she knew it before he had finished the second word.

'You're in a hospital.'

'Yes.'

'Are you hurt?'

'No. Priya is.'

He told it the way he told things, in order, with the times. Friday night, the paper's building, south of the river, a stairwell. Two men. Not Kessler; Kessler was not there, and he had thought about that and did not yet know what it meant. They had wanted what was in his coat and had gone for the person nearer the top of the stairs. Priya had gone down a flight. She had been out, he thought, for under a minute. Her right wrist. Her head, above the ear, which had bled a great deal and had been stitched. They had scanned it. They were keeping her. She was in a bed under a name that was not hers, and he had been in the chair beside it since two, and the payphone was at the end of the ward by the lifts, and he had come to it with a pound.

Laura asked the questions in the order she would have asked them of a registrar. Loss of consciousness, how long. Vomiting. Did she know where she was when she came round. Did she know what day it was. Was she talking sense. Was the scan clear. He answered every one and the answers were right, in the sense that they were the answers of a man who had sat through a night in a chair listening to doctors and had written down what they said, and she heard, under them, that he was not answering the one she had asked first.

'And you.'

'I'm fine.'

'Jonas.'

'My hands work. I can write. That's all I need them for.' A trolley went past him. 'The page is in my coat. That's what they came for and they didn't get it. Everything else is in a bag in Mayfair.'

'That's not what I asked either.'

He said nothing for a count of two, which was his way of agreeing.

'Laura. The cars at the end of the lane.'

'You've seen the paper.'

'Priya's got one. They're photographers. They'll stand there and they'll go. They can't hurt you.'

'Neither could she. She stood in the kitchen.'

'That's—' He stopped. She heard him start again, lower, with the care he used when he had decided what he was going to say and was going to say it whatever she did. 'Listen to me. Whatever you see at the end of that lane, you stay inside it. The men on Friday knew which of us to go for and they will know where you are, because everyone does now, and the one thing that lane has is that you can see who's coming up it. Don't come to London. I mean it. Don't put Elsie in a car. Say it.'

She did not say it. She stood in her mother's hall with the receiver against her ear and watched her mother through the kitchen door, not drinking her tea, and she did not say it, and the pips went, and he said 'Laura' once more in the voice of a man who has one coin, and the line went.

She put the phone down and looked at her hands. Steady. That interested her.

***

She took the history, because that was what she did when she did not know yet what she thought.

Presenting complaint: a man on a stepladder. Onset: Friday, one o'clock. Course: fewer today; would be more on Monday, when the paper that had the house had a quote from a neighbour, and the ones that did not have the house came for it. Then the relevant history, and she made herself go through it in order and not skip, as she would have for a patient who wanted to tell her only about the knee.

She had been sent here on a Sunday night because nobody knew about it. A week later a woman had stood in the kitchen and known Elsie's name without saying it. The police had the number of the car on the drive. The Treasury had sent two officers to Priya's door on a letter. Last night two men had broken Priya's wrist in a stairwell because she was nearer the top of the stairs than Jonas was, and the people who sent them had lost the thing they wanted, and there was a photograph on page five that told anyone who had not been paying attention where the wife was. The lane had one thing: you could see who was coming up it. You could see, and then they were at the door, and you opened it on the chain, and they were courteous.

Safe was a reading. She had taken it herself on the first night, with the engine off at the services and Elsie feeding, and she had not checked it since, because it was the reading she wanted. She knew that failure. She had seen it in better doctors than herself. A stable number taken for a healthy one.

The other thing. She had said, on Monday, at the door, *you have one week*, and had not said what happened at the end of it, because she had not known. The week ran out on Monday. She stood in the hall and found that she did know now, and that she had known since the hospital corridor came down the line behind his voice, and that it had nothing to do with photographers. He was in a chair beside a bed in a hospital she could not name, and the one thing he had asked her for in twelve days was to stay somewhere he could not reach her, and she had spent six years of marriage and twelve days of this being the one who was kept. There was no safe. There was where he was and where he was not, and she had found that she minded the second more than she minded the first.

***

'You can't take her into that,' Margaret said.

'She's in it. She's in it here. She's in it on page five.'

Margaret had got up when Laura came into the kitchen and said it, and had sat down again, and her hands had gone to the mug. Laura watched the hands. Fine tremor, both sides, worse than Friday. Her mother had spent the week pretending the radio was not on and had let a young man with a nice voice write down the only thing she knew.

'Then stay and we'll be in it together. There's two of us.'

'Mum. There isn't a together. They're not here for you, and they're not here for Elsie, they're here for a picture of the house, and when I'm gone they'll have the picture and they'll go, and you can go down for the paper.' She crouched, so that she was below her mother's eyeline, which was a thing you did, and took one of the hands off the mug and held it. 'He rings at nine. He'll ring tomorrow. You answer. You tell him I've gone to London with her, and that I'm all right, and that he's to keep ringing here, every day, because I'll ring you, and that's how it works now.'

'He'll ask where.'

'Tell him he knows where I'd go and he's not to go there.' She had thought about that in the hall. He would know. Aisha would open the door and not ask, and Jonas would not ring Aisha's number from a box because of the thing he understood about telephones, and she had decided, standing in the hall, that she was going to use what he had taught her against him and not feel bad about it. 'Tell him I'll find him. He walked into a building in St James's and said his name and a man rang him back in an hour. Tell him I can walk into a building too.'

Margaret looked at her for a while. Then she said, 'You've got his face on. The one he had at the table.'

'Good,' Laura said, and stood up.

***

She packed properly this time. The Sunday-night bag had taken eleven minutes; this took forty, and she did it the way she had done it then, nappies at the bottom, the thing Elsie would need first at the top, and she took the time because she had it and because packing was a thing her hands knew how to do while the rest of her did something else. The rabbit went in last, on top, where Elsie could see it from the seat.

In the kitchen she opened the drawer where she had put the card. Plain white, a mobile number, nothing else. She stood with it for a moment and could not have said what she meant by it, except that it was the one telephone number in the country that led to the people who knew where her husband was before she did, and she was done with being the person in the house who had nothing. Elsie went into the seat at twenty past ten and did not argue, because the car was a thing that happened to her and she liked the trees going past. Margaret stood in the door with her arms folded over her cardigan and did not come to the gate, which Laura understood: a woman at a gate is a photograph, and a woman in a doorway is not.

'Ring me,' Margaret said.

'From a phone that isn't mine.' She saw her mother not understand it, and let it go. 'Tonight. I promise.'

The lane was 300 yards to the field gate. She drove it at the speed she drove it to the shop. The man came down off the stepladder when he saw the car move and the others came out of the cars, and by the time she was fifty yards off there were five of them in the lane with the van behind, and the one in front had his hand up, flat, the way a policeman stops you, as though he were anyone.

She did not slow for the hand. She slowed for the lane. At walking pace she went through them, and they went round her the way water goes round a stone, and the lenses came up to the glass on both sides, and a woman said her name through the window twice, 'Laura, Laura,' as if they were friends. She looked straight ahead. She had treated people who looked at the floor and people who looked at the camera and she had never, until that moment, understood why anyone would do either, and she found that there was a third thing, which was to look at the road, because the road was where you were going.

In the mirror Elsie had the rabbit's ear in her mouth and was watching the men with the open stare of someone who had been alive for a year and nine days and had not yet been told what any of it was for.

Past the village sign one of the cars pulled out behind her. She watched it for a mile and then stopped watching it, because the police had her number plate and the woman had stood in the kitchen and a man with a lens had stood on a ladder, and all of them, every one, knew exactly where she was, and the only person in England who did not was the man she had married.

She had been sent to Norfolk on a Sunday night with a bag she had packed in eleven minutes. Nobody was sending her back.

At Thetford the road went into the forest, and she put the radio on, low, out of habit, and the news said his name, and she left it on.
