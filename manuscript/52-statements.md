# Chapter 52: Statements

#1424. Saturday. Two sittings, the first on the pad, because the book is not mine yet.

I signed my name thirty-one times today. Thirty pages and a last one, in a room in Holborn with a window that does not open, with a solicitor on my left whom the paper pays for and two officers on my right whom the Crown pays for, one older and one with a laptop. Nine hours, with a sandwich. I counted the pages because I count the pages. The older officer read the statement back to me at the end, aloud, in a flat voice, as though somebody else had said it, and I listened to it the way you listen to a stranger describing your house: right in every particular and not one of them mine.

I gave them the dates. The memo and its stamp. The email that went to the wrong inbox at 6.41 on a Wednesday. The Saturday at Horse Guards Road and the two paragraphs I did not sign. The map, the twelve codes, the order, and how I came to have it in my head, which took forty minutes because they kept asking *how do you know* and the true answer is *I don't, I infer*. The younger one typed that down. *I infer.* He looked up and said, 'Is that the word you want?' and I said it was the only word I had ever been entitled to.

They no longer wish to arrest me. The older officer said it at about four, looking at the table, in the voice people use for a thing they have been told to say and half agree with. Walbrook's lawyers wrote on Friday that their client's claim was 'under review'. The Treasury's second letter is in the file; I have not seen it, but he said it was one page and that the word in it was *withdrawn*. Priya's matter, he said, is 'a question of weeks and not of outcome'. He did not say what outcome. I did not ask. When I stood up he said, 'We'd be glad of your availability, Mr Whitlock,' which is what the Governor's letter said, and I understood that I had become a thing the state wants to be able to find.

I went out into Holborn at ten to five. It was getting dark. Nobody looked at me. That is new, since Wednesday, and I did not like it as much as I thought I would.

***

Priya's flat is the second floor in Bethnal Green and the plaster goes to the elbow. She opened the door with her left hand.

'You look like a man who's signed things,' she said.

'Thirty-one.'

'I did nineteen yesterday. Don't sit on that, it's the red book.'

It was on the sofa, in a clear bag with a label and a number. The police had found it on Wednesday in a steel cabinet in Mayfair, with her purse and her bail notice, and returned the three together on Friday, with an apology printed on a form. She turned it over with her good hand. Codes and ranks, in red biro, in her fast square writing, and in the margin by the eleventh a note that said *he'll say 'I think'*, and a tick, added later. I did not ask when she had added it.

She made tea one-handed and told me, with her back to me, that the Bank had asked her to lunch on Thursday and had made her an offer over the soup. A unit. Her word was 'a unit', said with the contempt she keeps for anything with an organisation chart; a small room inside the Bank, six people, and a standing order from the Governor's office to look under the promises, at the things nobody can see, which is what we did for four years on the quiet and were called difficult for. It would be hers. A title, a salary I will not write down because she would never forgive it, and a door that shuts.

'And?'

'And I said I'd think about it over the weekend.'

I laughed. It hurt my ribs. She watched me do it with great satisfaction.

'I got it from you,' she said. 'Not yet. I heard myself say it. I thought, that's his, I'll have to give it back.' She sat down. 'They know, Jonas. The man across the table said, "You'll have been told that line before, I expect," and smiled at me. I wanted to hit him with the cast.'

'Will you take it?'

'Monday,' she said. 'I'm going to say yes on Monday, and I want you to know I decided on Thursday. The weekend's for me to see whether I mind.' She turned her tea in her hand. 'They'll ask you next.'

'I know.'

'Don't say it.'

'I wasn't going to.'

'You were going to say not yet,' said Priya, and she was right.

***

There was a note from Pryce, in the second post on Friday, which I did not open until the night, on the sofa at home: four lines in longhand, lined paper, a Suffolk postmark.

*The order against me has been dropped. I am told I may go home. I have; it is flat here and I understand that I shall like it. I have been looking for the word to put at the end of this and I find that I can only write the one I have been in the habit of using, which is "not yet", and I have crossed it out. Come and see the sea when you can. Graham.*

He crossed it out so that I could read it. That is Graham.

***

I will set Haldane and Pell down together, once, as I understand it, and the understanding is Calloway's. Haldane stood aside from Walbrook on Thursday 'to assist the Bank's review of the events of the past three weeks'. The board suspended him on Friday, and the police have asked to speak to him about the statement that went out under his name and the word in it. Sir Anthony Pell resigned from the board on Friday afternoon 'for personal reasons' and is at a house in Wiltshire where the telephone is not being answered. I have no view. I would have thought I had a view. It turns out that I have only the dates.

***

Dana Kessler was on a bench in Ruskin Park, by the bandstand, at four on the Friday. I had that from Laura, who had it from her phone: a number she did not know, a voice, two sentences, and then the thing Laura says when she wants me to understand she has taken a history. 'She says she'll be on the bench. She says you needn't. I said I'd decide for you, and then I decided you'd decide for yourself. I'll be in the café.' She was. I could see her through the glass, with Elsie on her knee, not looking.

Kessler had on a dark coat, no scarf, no bag. Her hands were empty and in her lap, which I took to be on purpose. She looked less like a woman who has done a thing than a woman who has finished one.

'I've closed the firm,' she said, before I sat. 'The third of us is on a train to Manchester. The two men from the stairs have been interviewed, and one of them has said things about me I would have preferred him not to. I've made a statement. A witness, as long as I say what I did and not what I knew; my solicitor was very clear. I thought you should hear it from me and not from a form.'

'The envelope.'

'It went in as it was. Your fingerprints are on it, and a woman's I trained, who has been very brave and will lose her job.' She said that last part without inflection and I understood it was the one thing that she minded. 'I would like not to be thanked.'

'I wasn't going to.'

'Good.' She looked at the park. A man was walking a dog round the bandstand, and she watched him all the way, in that way she has, and I thought that she will do it for the rest of her life. 'I read one of them, you know. The night I had the bag in my safe, before the man came for it. The seventh.'

I said nothing.

'The entry where the handwriting changes.' She did not look at me. 'I had read enough of them by then to know what it was before I'd read ten lines. I stopped. I put it back in the bag and I was not good company for a week.' Her voice did not alter. 'That is why I said no to him on Tuesday. I would like you to know that it was not conscience. It was a page.'

We sat. A child went by on a scooter, shrieking, and she did not turn her head.

'What will you do with them?' she said. 'They will be given back to you. The Crown will photograph them first, and then they'll be returned, and they're yours, and there are nine, and some of the pages are worth a great deal to a great many people. Newspapers. Publishers. A man who was in my old line of work rang me this week and asked if I could put him in touch.'

'I haven't decided what to say to them.'

'That isn't what I asked.'

I said: 'I'm going to finish them.'

She turned and looked at me for the first time. It took several seconds. She is a woman who tells people exactly what will happen next, and I watched her try, and fail, to say what would happen next to me.

'That isn't an answer,' she said.

'It's the one I've got.'

Dana Kessler stood up and put out her hand, and I shook it. It was cold, and dry, and held mine for the length of one count. Then she walked away down the path, towards the gate, and did not look back, and by the time I could see Laura through the café window, standing, she was gone.

***

Second sitting. 9.40pm. Home, since Thursday, with the door locked and a thing I notice myself doing, which is checking it twice. Elsie asleep.

Laura came in from the kitchen with a plate for me and set it down, and beside it, from the pocket of her cardigan, a black notebook still in its cellophane, from the box of six in the study desk.

'You saw her?' she said.

'She asked what I'd do with the others. I told her I'd finish them.'

'Good,' said Laura. She sat. She took the cellophane off herself, with her thumbnail, and laid the book open flat in front of me at the first page, the way she lays a thing flat on a couch before she examines it. 'Then you'll need a tenth. Finish them, and then we'll talk about the other five.'

I am writing this paragraph in it. The glue still smells. There are five more in the desk, and she has counted them.
