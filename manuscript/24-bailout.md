# Chapter 24: Bailout

The Governor had asked for ten minutes without officials, and Caroline Strand had spent a career making sure that no conversation she took part in was ever without officials. A conversation without a note was a conversation that had not happened. That was its use, and that was its danger, and she had never before been on the side of the table that needed it.

So she gave him the flat. Saturday, eight o'clock, the sitting room above Downing Street with the curtains half drawn, two cups of coffee that neither of them touched and a table with nothing on it. Simon Carrow was downstairs in the outer office with the Saturday papers and instructions not to come up. The Governor arrived on foot from the Treasury side, in a coat that had seen better weather, and sat where she put him, and did not take out a pen.

'No note,' he said. It was not a question.

'No note. You asked for it.'

'I did.' He looked at the coffee and left it. 'Then I'll be quicker than I would be with one.'

A week ago, at a longer table, with the risk manager at the far end explaining the end of the world in a voice like a man reading gas meters, the Governor had said very little and let her run it. He was not letting her run this.

'You know where we are with Walbrook,' he said.

'Tell me as if I didn't.'

'A member failed on Monday. Walbrook declared the default, took the book and has been hedging it all week. The auction of what's left starts Tuesday; that is the fastest it has ever been done. The defaulter's own margin is gone. Its contribution to the default fund is gone. Walbrook's own capital, the layer they are required to put in before anyone else loses a penny, went on Thursday afternoon. They are now spending the other members' money. The default fund is built to survive the two largest members failing at once. It has survived one, in a market that has fallen every day since.'

'And the second.'

'There is a second.' He gave her the name. She had asked for it, in the sense that she had asked him to speak as if she knew nothing, and she took it the way one takes a diagnosis: without moving. It was a name people had on their debit cards. 'It met Friday's call at ten to nine with money it borrowed from us on Thursday night. It will meet Monday's, probably. I do not think it meets Tuesday's. Within days, Chancellor. Not weeks.'

'And if it fails.'

'Then the fund is through, and Walbrook turns to the survivors for more, and the survivors are the thirty-eight institutions who have been selling everything they own all week to pay the calls they already have. Some of them will refuse. Some of them will not be able to. At that point Walbrook Clearing House cannot meet its obligations, and it will say so, because it is required to say so, to me.'

She let a silence go by, which was a barrister's habit: let the witness hear his own answer.

'What does that mean,' she said. 'Precisely. Not the clearing house. The country.'

'Every trade it stands behind, frozen.'

'Frozen.'

'Not paused. Frozen as in nobody knows what they are owed. Walbrook sits in the middle of every contract its members have cleared. If it stops, there is no middle. Every pension fund that has hedged the pensions it owes has a hedge with no counterparty. Every bank that has fixed its funding has unfixed it. Every mortgage rate in this country is priced off a market that no longer exists, and so is every rate in every country that has ever dealt in sterling, which is all of them. The last time anyone added up the contracts behind a clearing house, the answer had so many noughts that we stopped quoting it. In the meantime nobody pays anyone.' <!-- VERIFY: scale of cleared notional is deliberately left as "noughts"; real figures for a sterling swaps CCP are in the hundreds of trillions and should not be quoted here -->

'We have a regime for that,' she said. 'Resolution. It's yours.'

'I have the power. I do not have the money. The power lets me take over the clearing house, tear up the contracts that cannot be met and allocate the losses. The losses are still losses. Nobody has ever put a clearing house into resolution anywhere in the world, and the reason is that by the time you need to, there is nothing left to resolve with. It is a fire door. It is not a sprinkler.'

There was no record. That was the point of the flat. She found it did not help.

'Options,' she said. 'I'd like the options.'

'There are three. Two are mine. One is yours.' He held up a hand and did not count on it, which she noticed. 'One: from Monday morning the Bank lends to Walbrook, and to any member that asks, against whatever collateral they can still find, at whatever rate I choose, in whatever quantity. I need nobody's permission for that and I have already given the instruction. It buys days. Possibly a week. It does not buy the second member, because the second member's problem is not that it cannot borrow. It is that what it has borrowed against is worth less every morning.'

'Two.'

'Two: the Bank buys. Government bonds, outright, in size, from Monday, for as long as it takes to put a floor under the price of the thing everyone's collateral is made of. It worked last time. It worked because the market believed we would not stop. I may lose money doing it. It is not my money to lose. So I need your indemnity, in writing, before I do it.' <!-- VERIFY: the Treasury indemnified the Bank's September 2022 gilt purchases; the mechanism is as research/swaps-and-clearing.md §5 describes -->

'And I said no on Tuesday.'

'You said no on Tuesday. On Tuesday one member had failed and the fund was intact and you had four weeks to polling day. You still have four weeks to polling day.' It was the only thing he said all morning that could have been called unkind, and he said it in the same voice as the rest. 'Three is yours. You are entitled to do nothing. I am then obliged to write to you, formally, that in the Bank's opinion public funds may be at risk. Once that letter exists, you may direct the Bank. I would, as it happens, welcome being directed. But the letter would exist. And so would the fact that you had it.'

So that was why he had wanted no note. Not for himself. For her. He was offering her the one morning in the week in which she could decide without having been told.

'Say it,' she said. 'The word. I'd like to hear you say it so I can stop waiting for it.'

'Bailout.'

'Thank you. Now give me the version that doesn't have it in.'

'There isn't one.' He did, finally, pick up the coffee, and held it without drinking. 'There is the word and there is the thing. You can have the thing without the word for about a week, if we start on Monday and we are lucky and nobody at the Treasury writes anything down. After that you have the word without the thing, because the thing will have happened by itself, and it will be called something worse.'

She stood up, because she thought better standing, and because she wanted a moment with her back to him.

A week ago she had sat at the head of a table and told a man that the system was held up by the belief that it was held up. The difference was that a week ago she had thought belief was something she could manufacture by saying the right sentence on breakfast television, and that if the risk manager signed it as well, it would hold. He had not signed. She had said it anyway. It had held for forty minutes.

The memo was in her box. It had been in her box since the Wednesday before the sinking, in a folder Simon had marked with a yellow flag, and she had read Simon's cover note, two paragraphs, and not the six pages under it. She knew the phrase in it, because everyone did now: approximately three years. She noticed that she was thinking about the memo instead of the decision, and recognised the manoeuvre, because she had watched juries do it.

'If I give you the indemnity on Monday,' she said, 'what do you announce?'

'That the Bank is undertaking temporary purchases of government bonds to restore orderly conditions, on the instruction of the Governor, with the full backing of the Treasury. Your name is in the second sentence. There is no form of words in which it is not.'

'And the Opposition says that a government that spent a week saying "sound" has spent the taxpayer's money four weeks before an election to prove it was lying.'

'Yes.'

'And the alternative is that I say "sound" for another week and then Walbrook rings you.'

'Yes.'

She turned round. He had put the coffee down.

'I want the weekend,' she said.

'You have it.' He stood, and did up the coat. 'I would only say, Chancellor, that Walbrook's call goes to the banks at eight on Monday morning. The weekend ends at seven. Not at midnight on Sunday. People always think it is midnight on Sunday.'

She saw him down herself, and did not offer a hand, and he did not look for one. At the door he said, 'I'm sorry it was me,' which she decided, later, had been the truth.

***

Simon Carrow was on the stairs before the door had closed. He had a sheet of paper in his hand and the face he wore when he had been given something he could not make smaller.

'Chancellor. Press office. Came in at half past eight.'

She took it. A single page, an email printed out, from a journalist whose name she knew from a pensions story last autumn. Eleven questions. Numbered. The first one was the only one that mattered, and he had put it first because he knew it.

*1. Did the Chancellor see a memorandum dated [three years ago], from the head of collateral risk at Walbrook Clearing House, received and stamped by HM Treasury, estimating that the system had "approximately three years before a moderate shock becomes unmanageable"? If not, who did, and what action was taken?*

*Response by 6pm today for publication Sunday.*

The ninth question asked whether the Treasury had made any request to the police concerning any present or former Walbrook employee. She read it twice and set it aside for a worse room.

'Draft the reply,' Carrow said. 'I'll have it to you in an hour.'

'No.' She took a pen from the table by the door, the first pen anyone had held in the flat that morning, and wrote on the back of the sheet, standing, in the hand that had once filled a hundred blue notebooks. *The Chancellor did not see the document. Correspondence of this kind is handled at official level in the normal way and is not routinely put to ministers. The Treasury does not comment on matters that may be the subject of police inquiries.*

It was true. Every word. She had not seen the document; she had seen a cover note on a Wednesday, and a stapled copy slide across a table a week ago, and the phrase, and she had turned no page. A department's knowledge was the department's. She knew the doctrine by name. So, she thought, would he.

'That's the response,' she said. 'Nothing else. Not a word more.'

Carrow read it, and read it again, and his face did not change, which she had hired him for.

'Yes, Chancellor.'

She went back upstairs. The coffee had gone cold in both cups. She had asked for the weekend, and it was ten past nine on Saturday morning, and the weekend was already Sunday's front page.
