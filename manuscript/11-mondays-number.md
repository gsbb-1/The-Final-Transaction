# Chapter 11: Monday's number

#1404

I asked for the night. I was given it. This is what it was worth.

Slept 1.10 to 5.40. Lay still until Elsie started at 6.05, so that Laura could have the half hour. Made the bottle. Sat on the kitchen floor and watched Elsie post a wooden ring into a saucepan and take it out again. 23 times. She did not tire of it. I envied her that.

The statement was on the table where I had left it on Saturday night, between the memo and Carrow's card. Two paragraphs on Treasury paper. Walbrook's collateral arrangements are fully sound and adequate to all foreseeable conditions. A line for my name. They want it by 6am tomorrow, so that it can go out before the 7am call. I read it again with Elsie on my knee. It had not improved overnight. Nothing does.

I am writing this at the kitchen table at twenty past three in the afternoon, which is not when I write. I am writing early because I do not know where I will be tonight. In order, then. The record is only any use in order.

9.52. Priya.

I knew it would be bad because she did not say hello. She said a number.

'Twenty-two point four.'

'Billion.'

'Yes, Jonas. Billion. That's the morning run. Initial and variation, every member, if gilts open another four points down. Which they will, because the fund's order lands at eight and everyone in the market has read the same notice we have.' <!-- VERIFY: scale of a single morning's margin call at a large UK clearing house in a severe gilt sell-off; the figure is invented and should be checked against real crisis days (e.g. Sept 2022) -->

I said nothing. She had not finished.

'I've gone through every member's liquidity disclosure. The ones who can pay by nine can pay fourteen, maybe fifteen, between them. The gap is seven. If it's six points instead of four, the gap is eleven. And one member is short on its own by two point eight before anyone else gets into trouble. I'm not saying which one on a phone. You know which one.' <!-- VERIFY: whether a clearing-house analyst would have access to members' liquidity figures in this form -->

I did know. Everyone on the third floor knows which one, and nobody says it, and that is a sentence I could have written in any of the last 1,400 entries.

'Walk me through the fund,' I said. I wanted to hear whether she had it.

She had it. She said it in one breath. The Gulf fund has given notice. On Monday we hand its collateral back, which takes the biggest cushion out of the system before anything has fallen. Then the fund sells its bonds, and the price of every bond everyone else has posted to us goes down, so everyone has to post more, at the same hour, out of the same pile. And the pile has been counted three times by three different people who each think it is theirs.

'I counted the pile, Jonas,' she said. 'It took me all night. It should not have been possible for me to count it in one night and for nobody to have counted it in four years.'

That was when the number stopped and the rest began.

'You weren't surprised on Thursday.'

'No.'

'I went back through the exceptions reports. Four years of them. The pattern's in every quarter. Your initials on every sign-off. "Monitor." That's the note. Monitor. You've been monitoring this like a man monitoring a gas leak from the garden.'

'I wrote a memo.'

'When?'

'Three years ago.'

She was quiet for the length of a breath. 'Who to?'

'Graham. The Treasury.'

'And then?'

'Then I monitored.'

I heard her put something down. A mug, a pen. 'Graham asked me on Friday whether you kept personal notes. Diaries. I told him I didn't know what he meant. I do now, don't I. You've got it all written down somewhere and you've been reading it to yourself at night like scripture.'

I did not answer that because it was accurate.

'Everything my parents have is in a fund that clears through the member I'm not naming,' she said. 'Everything. By Tuesday it's an argument between lawyers. So this is what I'm doing. I'm sending the figures to the regulator's whistleblowing address the moment I put the phone down. And I'm sending them to Rhys Calloway.'

I knew the name. Financial correspondent at one of the nationals. The pensions piece last year. He rings Walbrook twice a year and is told that everything is fine, and prints it with the word "insists".

'Don't send it to Calloway yet.'

'Why not?'

'Because once it's out you can't—'

'Can't what? Monitor it?'

She hung up. 9.59. Seven minutes.

I rang back at 10.20. Six rings, voicemail. 10.41, the same. I texted: *Ring me. Please. Not to argue.* At 11.05 it did not ring at all. Straight to the recorded voice. A phone switched off, or run flat, or in a bag in a car, or at the bottom of the Regent's Canal. I counted the possibilities and stopped at four, because after four they are all the same possibility.

Laura was in the doorway with Elsie. 'That was Priya.'

'Yes.'

'On a Sunday.'

'I need to go to her flat.'

She looked at my hands, which is what she does instead of looking at faces. I do not know what they were doing. 'Go, then,' she said. She did not ask what was wrong. Four years ago she would have. I noted that, and noted that I was noting it instead of answering, and went for my coat.

Herne Hill to Blackfriars, 11.41. Circle line to Liverpool Street. Central line one stop. 51 minutes door to door, which on a Sunday is not bad. I counted stations because it was something to count.

Somewhere under the City, on the Circle line, with no signal, I took out Carrow's card and looked at it and put it back. Eighteen hours to six. I had not rung him. I had not decided not to. I noticed that those are the same thing and that I have spent three years pretending they are not.

I have been to the flat once before, two winters ago, to drop off a laptop when she had flu and would not stop working. Second floor of a conversion on a street of them. I remembered the door because it is the only blue one.

The street door was on the latch. A man came out with a bicycle as I reached it and held it for me. Thirty-two stairs. I know because I counted them going up and again coming down and the number was the same both times, which was the only thing that day that behaved.

Her door was shut. I knocked. I said her name twice, at a normal volume and then louder. Then I tried the handle and it turned and the door went in.

I stood on the mat and said her name a third time.

It is a small flat and she keeps it the way she keeps a spreadsheet. The radiator was on. Nothing was broken. Nothing was on the floor. In the kitchen, on the table, three stacks of printouts squared off at the edges, her handwriting down the margins in two colours. The top sheet of the middle stack had been torn across and the lower half was missing. A laptop charger plugged in at the wall with nothing on the end of it. Two mugs on the draining board, both tea, both cold, both unwashed. Priya washes up as she goes. I have watched her do it at work with a plastic cup.

The bed was made. She had not slept. The bedroom window was shut. Her coat was on the hook by the door, the long grey one she wears to work. No bag. No keys on the hook where the coat was. No phone.

I stood in the hall and rang her. Nothing rang in the flat. I listened for a long time, longer than the ringing lasted, as though a phone might start up late.

I thought about it the way I think about a collateral chain, which is the only way I know. A person who leaves with her keys and her bag locks the door behind her. A person who is taken does not get to. A person who leaves in a hurry to meet a journalist might not get to either. The two mugs fit two of those and not the third, or fit all of them if she had a visitor at seven in the morning. I could not make the facts choose.

I photographed the table. The three stacks, the torn sheet, the charger. I did not touch the papers. Then I noticed that I had been careful not to touch the papers, and I stood for a moment with that. Four days ago I was a man who photographed his diary. Today I was a man who thought about fingerprints in a colleague's kitchen.

I wrote *Ring me. J.* on the back of a receipt from my pocket and left it on the top stack where she would see it if she came back, and where anyone else would see it too. I considered that and left it anyway.

I pulled the door to behind me. It would not lock. I had nothing to lock it with. I left it as I had found it and went down the thirty-two stairs.

The street was ordinary. Sunday. A woman across the road under the plane tree was drinking a coffee and looking at me, and when I looked back she did not look away.
