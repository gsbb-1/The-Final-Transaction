# Chapter 3: Forty

Forty. 14,610 days, counting the leap years. 1,400 of them in here.

Elsie did not sleep. That is the entry. The rest is what happened round it.

Up at 5.50. She was already awake and talking to the ceiling in the language she uses for the ceiling. I brought her down so Laura could have an hour. Fed her, failed to feed her, wore most of it. She has six teeth and uses them on spoons. At 6.40 I did what I do, which is photograph last night's page before the notebook goes in the bag, so that if the bag goes under a bus there is a copy. Typed the number in the subject. Sent it. Put the phone in my pocket and the notebook in the bag and Elsie on my hip, and Laura came down and said happy birthday into my neck and took her off me.

Walked to the station with the bag. Eight notebooks full. This is the ninth, 40 pages from the end. The eight live where my father kept his tools and I have not been to see them since March. The ninth comes with me, everywhere, and comes home. Laura calls it the briefcase, which it is not.

Work. Cake at 8.20 from the team, who sang. A card with 31 signatures. Graham's on it. *Many of them. G.* Graham himself said four words to me all day, in the morning meeting, and they were 'Good' and 'Thank you, Jonas', and he said them to the table. Normally he asks after Elsie. I noted it and moved on. Men of 57 are allowed a day.

The 7am call went out on time and was met in full. Two members posted cash instead of gilts, which they do at month end when they need the bonds elsewhere. I know which two and I can guess where the bonds are. It is in here already, somewhere about #1120. Nothing new today. Nothing new most days. That is the point of writing every day: so that when a day is different, I will know.

***

Home by 6.15, which Laura had asked for and which I managed by leaving a meeting Graham did not notice me leave.

Tom and Aisha at 7.30. Tom brought a bottle that was too good for us and a card with 40 on it that made a noise when opened, and Elsie, who was meant to be asleep, heard it from upstairs and that was that for the evening's plan.

Laura had cooked. Lamb, slow, the thing she does when there is time, and there is not time, so she had made it on Tuesday night after I had gone up to write. I did not know that until Aisha asked how long it had been in and Laura said 'Since about eleven last night, in instalments' and looked at me. One look. I counted it.

Elsie came down at 7.50 in Laura's arms and sat on my knee through the first course and was passed to Aisha, who wanted her, and then to Tom, who did not, and who held her like a parcel at arm's length and asked her what she thought of the gilt curve. She put a hand in his wine.

Tom and I were at the bank together, 25 to 30. He stayed. He is something now with a title that has 'global' in it and he works four days and plays golf on the fifth, and he looks well on it. He said, at about nine, with Elsie finally upstairs and Laura standing in the doorway listening for her, 'So what is it you actually do, Jonas? Laura says you won't tell her either.'

Laura said, 'Laura says he tells her it's work.'

I gave him the short answer. People who buy and sell bonds and the things built on bonds have to put down a deposit so that if one of them goes bust the other is not left holding nothing. The deposit is called collateral. Walbrook stands in the middle, holds the deposits and decides each morning if they are big enough. I decide if the deposits are real.

'Real,' said Aisha.

'Whether the thing they've put down is theirs to put down.'

'Why wouldn't it be?'

'It mostly is.'

'And when it isn't?' Tom said. He was smiling; he had been on a trading floor for 15 years and the question was a joke.

'Then it isn't on one morning for everybody at once,' I said. 'And everybody finds out at 7am.'

'Has that happened?'

'Not yet.'

Aisha said, 'That's a strange way to say no.'

Tom laughed. 'That's him. That's the whole of him. "It mostly is." "Not yet."' He said that at the bank I had been the only man on the desk who read the footnotes, and that it had made me unbearable and probably rich, and I said it had made me unbearable. Laura did not laugh.

Aisha asked whether that was what I wrote about at night. Laura said, from the doorway, 'Work,' before I could, and went up to Elsie.

***

Elsie slept from 9.40 to 10.05. Then from 10.30 to 10.45. Laura went up each time and came down each time a little quieter.

At 10.50 I went up myself, because it was my turn and because I wanted to. Elsie standing in the cot in the dark with both hands on the rail. 51 weeks tomorrow. Everyone says one, and so do I. She did not cry when she saw me. She said the thing she says that is nearly 'Dad' and sat down hard, as if that settled it. I picked her up. She weighs 9.5 kilos, I would say. 9.6. She put her head in the place under my jaw where it goes and stopped moving, and she was asleep inside two minutes, and I stood there for 20 more because I know what happens if you put her down inside 20.

While I stood there I could hear them downstairs. Tom telling a story about the bank. Laura laughing, properly, which I had not heard this week. Aisha saying 'he was never like that' and Laura saying 'he was, actually, a bit', and then something I did not get.

I thought: this is what changed. I am not going to write down what, tonight. Not on this page. At 37 I wrote a paper that said three years, and I thought three years was a number. At 39 she was born. I am 40, and this is the year the number said, and she is a day short of 51 weeks and she weighs 9.6 kilos and she is asleep on me, and downstairs there is lamb.

Put her down at 11.12. She stayed down.

***

Tom and Aisha went at 11.40. At the door Tom held on to my hand a moment and said, 'You all right? Really?' and I said I was 40 and he said that wasn't what he'd asked.

I washed up. Laura dried, which she does not usually do; usually she goes up and I do the kitchen and come up after I have written, and she is asleep or doing a good job of it. Tonight she stood next to me with a cloth and said, when the last pan was done:

'What's in the notebooks, Jonas?'

Not angry. She asks the way she asks a patient what they drink. She has asked four times in four years and I have the dates. She said, before I could answer, 'You've had your jaw set since Tom's card went off. You do it when you're adding up.' She watches hands and jaws for a living. I unclenched it, which told her something too.

I said, 'Work.'

She said, 'You said that at 36.' She folded the cloth. 'I'm a GP. When a man tells me the same thing four times in the same words I don't think he's lying. I think he's decided what he can afford to say.' She put the cloth over the tap. 'Happy birthday. Don't be long.'

She went up.

That is the fourth time. Her: four questions. Me: 1,400 nights. That is not a balance I would sign off.

***

The entry was going to end there. It does not, because of the phone.

I went to put it on charge and saw the email was still open from this morning. I had not thought about it all day. *Subject: 1399. One attachment.* I looked at it because I look at things, and then I looked at the address line.

j.whitlock. Walbrook.

Not my own. The work one. Same first letter and the phone chose for me at 6.41 this morning while I had a child on my hip, and I did not check, because in 1,399 mornings I have never had to check.

It did not arrive. I had the work inbox open all day. Nothing from me, no bounce, no error. An attachment from an outside address does not simply fail to arrive at Walbrook. It is held. Someone is sent a note that it is held, and someone opens it.

The page is the one above this. A man turning 40. Four lines. Nothing in it.

Except that I photograph the spread, both pages, because the pages face each other and the phone cannot be told otherwise. And the page facing #1399 is the end of #1398. Three lines of gilts, the member's code, the fail and the cash that covered it, where I think the bond went, the quarterly report. And the initials of the man who signed it.

Graham said four words to me today, to the table.

1,400 days. One mistake. I would like to know what it costs, and I am the only person in this house who could tell me, and I do not know.
