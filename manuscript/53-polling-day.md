# Chapter 53: Polling day

The letter was in her handbag, in a plain envelope, where she had kept it for fifteen days. It was one paragraph long and addressed to the prime minister, and she had drafted it herself on the Wednesday morning with the television on and the sound off, and had left the date blank, because a date was a fact and she had wanted, for a while, to see what she would do with the days before it.

Thursday. Seven-ten. The polling station was a primary school in her constituency, in a hall that smelled of floor polish and radiators, and the presiding officer, a woman in a lanyard who had voted for her twice, said 'Good morning, Chancellor,' and then, in a smaller voice, 'Good luck,' and then looked as though she wished she had said the first one only.

There were two photographers at the gate and she gave them what they were owed. The ballot paper was the size of a bill. She took the pencil on its string into the booth and stood for a moment with it over the box beside her own name, which was a thing she had never done in twenty years, because she had always known what she was going to do. She marked it. Folded it. Posted it in the slot. A child in a school jumper, held up by its father to see, watched her do it with the grave attention of a person following a rule, and she thought, quite clearly, that there was nothing in that room that had been made safe by her, and that this was the first time she had been able to say so without wanting to disprove it.

She went to work. It was a day on which nothing was permitted to happen. The broadcasters were silent on politics by law until ten; the Treasury, by custom, was silent on everything. The pound stood where it had stood for a fortnight, a little above where it had been before, and she did not look at it. Walbrook had met every call since the Wednesday. The Bank had been repaid the first of what it had lent, on the Friday, with interest, out of money that belonged to a man who had, according to the first person on the telephone that morning, been thanked. She had signed a page for that, one page, on a Wednesday morning, with the television on in front of her and the prime minister breathing on the line, and it had been a decision that cost her nothing but what it cost her to make. She had thought, afterwards, that it was the only decision of the year that could have been done by a lawyer rather than a politician, and that the difference was the whole story.

At five Carrow came in and stood by the door.

'Simon.'

'The committee has written, Chancellor. Whatever the result. They will want the Treasury's file on the memo, the minutes of the room under the Cabinet Office, and your private office's diary for the week of the sinking.' He looked at the folder in his hand rather than at her. 'The usual course is that the department resists, and takes advice, and discloses what it is obliged to, in the order it is obliged to.'

'I know the usual course.'

'Yes.' He waited. 'Chancellor, I wrote the letter. I should like you to know that I do not think I was wrong to. I should also like you to know that I think I was unwise.'

It was, she saw, the most that he could say, and she saw how much it had cost, and for an instant she wanted to tell him that she was sorry. She said, 'Thank you, Simon,' which was true and nothing else, and he went out.

***

The first projection came at ten. The line the broadcasters used, in the voice they reserve for weather, was 'too close to call'. By midnight it was not: her party would be second, by a margin the commentators would argue about until Christmas. The swing in the marginals was in the places where people kept their savings in funds and had been told, a fortnight ago, on the radio, that a clearing house had come within three hours of failing, and what they remembered was not that it had been saved. She did not find this unreasonable. She would have voted the same.

The count was in a sports hall in her constituency, under strip lights, with trestle tables, and she stood on the stage at ten to two between the other candidates, a man from the Opposition she had been in the House with for nineteen years, and a man dressed as a bear, and heard that she had been returned with a majority of 2,114, which was down by nine thousand. She thanked the returning officer. She thanked the volunteers, whose faces she was able to look at. She did not say anything else. The Opposition man shook her hand with both his, which she thought was a decent thing to do, and with no pleasure.

The interview was in the school next door, in a classroom with a camera in it, and a sound man who had set up in the time it took her to walk across the road. It was the programme that ran at seven on Friday morning, with a presenter she had known for twenty years and been afraid of for eighteen. Carrow stood in the doorway. Her special adviser, who had cried in the car, was not there.

The presenter was kind first, which was how you knew. She asked about the seat, the result, the swing in the marginals, the cost of the three weeks. She asked whether the Chancellor had considered that the government had lost not on the economy but on the question of whether it had been honest about the warning. Strand said that the electorate was entitled to its view, which was true and was also a thing she had said in some form ever since she was twenty-eight, to every kind of judge.

'Chancellor.' The presenter turned a page, and Strand saw that it was one page, and knew which. 'A man gave this government a six-page note three years ago. It said that the thing which happened a fortnight ago would happen in about three years. It was stamped and filed. It was in your department's papers on the Wednesday before the tanker was hit. Do you regret ignoring it?'

She had the answer. She had had it for two weeks, in its various forms, the form for the committee and the form for a court and the form for the party's historians, and she had refined it, as she refined everything, until there was no sentence in it that anyone could pull. *The advice at the time was that the arrangements were sound. The note was one view. Officials considered it in the normal way.* She opened her mouth to give it, and heard instead:

'Yes.'

The presenter waited. She had waited for a defence, and the defence had not come, and she was too good to say anything into the gap.

'Yes,' Strand said again, because it had been said, and one did not leave a sentence of that sort unfinished. 'I should have read it in the first week, not the third. I did not, because I did not wish to, and I told myself that was prudence. It was not prudence. I regret it.' She heard herself. 'That is all. I do not think there is anything I can add which would improve it.'

'You're saying you were wrong.'

'I am saying that I read it, and that I was right about the law, and wrong about the thing the law was for.' She looked at the lens, and then, because that was what one did, at the presenter. 'He had written down every weakness in his own case before I could find one. I have never met that in a witness. I did not know what to do with it, and so I did the thing I know, and that is the thing I regret.'

She stopped. The red light stayed on for another second. Then the sound man took his headphones off, and the room was very quiet, and Carrow, in the doorway, did not move.

She had said, in twenty years, what she meant a great many times. She had never before said it by accident. It was, she understood, quite different, and it frightened her a good deal more than the count had, and she sat there for a moment with her hands on her knees and found that she had not the faintest idea who was going to be in the room when she stood up.

***

At twenty past four she was in the car, going back to London, and the prime minister's concession had been on the radio for forty minutes. He had used the word *honour*. He had thanked her by name.

'The letter,' she said.

'Chancellor?'

'It is to be dated today. It is Friday. Have the private office send it to the prime minister's, by hand, before the man's speech is repeated at eight.' She opened her bag, and took out the envelope, and handed it forward. 'And the committee.'

Carrow turned in the seat. In the dark of the car she could see the edge of his face, and the folder on his knee.

'The Treasury's copy of the memo has a stamp on it, a reference, and a margin,' she said. 'In the margin on page four there are three letters and a date. They are my initials. Whoever asks for the file will see them.'

'The department's position, Chancellor, is that—'

'The department's position is that I read it on the Thursday, which I did, in the evening, after the private office had gone.' She had not known she was going to say it until it was said. 'You will give them the file. In the order it is in. Including page four.'

He looked at her for what seemed to be a long time.

'Mark it,' said Caroline Strand. 'Before somebody else does.'
