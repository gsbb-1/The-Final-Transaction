# Chapter 34: Calloway's office

Friday. #1416. Begun at 12.40 on Saturday morning by the clock over the doors, in a hospital corridor, on the backs of a leaflet about head injuries that a nurse gave me to read. I have read it. The pen is mine.

The day before the stairs. The stairs took four minutes and the day took fourteen hours, and the day is the reason there were stairs.

***

Nine o'clock, the box on the Kentish Town Road, Margaret's landline. Laura. Four minutes. Nothing to report at either end, which I have learned to count as a good morning.

The paper's building by ten, by the back way. Fourth floor, the room with no windows. Graham was already there with the wheeled case, in his coat, because he has been a 7.35 man for thirty years and has nowhere to be at 7.35.

The work, in one paragraph. Sixteen box files: four years of what Walbrook's own risk committee was told each member had lodged with us, by code, by line, by size, with Graham's initials under "no material concentration" at the foot of every quarter. Priya read. I wrote. Graham found the pages and said the names, which he is allowed to say and I am not. By six the right-hand column of the page in my coat had, against all twelve codes, what the firm's own papers say each one holds to the end of September. Not the fingerprints; those are in Mayfair. But a column a central bank can check against the firm's records is a different thing from two people's memory of a notebook, and Priya said so at six, pen down: 'Now it's their records.' The red book was open beside her all day and did not leave her reach once. I noticed, because it is what I used to do.

12.10. Phone two. Nasser, from a car. Mr Al-Mansouri asked him to say Sunday, at the hotel, at whatever hour I could manage, with whatever I had by then. 'And he asked me to tell you, Mr Whitlock, that the Bank said *concrete*. Twice. He was looking at the table.' I said Sunday.

The second name paid its nine o'clock on the Bank's money, Graham said. Four more mornings of that, by his arithmetic, and then Tuesday. He left at twenty to nine with the case. He would not leave it in the room, and I did not ask him to.

***

10.50. Calloway's rule since Thursday: not the lifts, which open onto the night desk and a camera, but the back stairs to the loading bay, where he would meet us with the car at eleven. Priya wiped the board, as she has every night. She came out behind me with her rucksack on one shoulder and the red book inside it. I had the page where it has been since Wednesday, folded once, in the inside pocket of my coat, where the cloakroom ticket was.

The stairs are concrete. A half-landing between floors, a steel rail, a light on each landing and none between. I was six steps down from the fourth. Priya was on the landing above me, pulling the door to.

I heard feet coming down from the fifth and thought: Calloway. I have had three hours to improve that and I am not going to.

Two men. I am not going to describe them well, because I did not see them well, and I know what a description is worth when a man has written it afterwards to sound as though he looked. Dark coats. One broad, older, forty or more. One younger, quicker. Neither said a word on the way down. I have seen two of her people before, in the dark and at a distance, and I could not swear to either of these.

The older one took hold of Priya's bag.

That was the whole of it. He took the strap and pulled, and Priya did not let go. Four years I have worked with her and I have never once seen her let go of anything, and she did not let go of a £30 rucksack on a staircase in the dark, and so when he pulled she came with it. Over the top step of the half-flight and down it, nine steps, onto the landing where I was standing, and I heard her wrist go before I heard her head. I did not know until tonight that you can tell the two sounds apart. You can.

Then I did the wrong thing. I went down to her. Not up at him; not down and away with the page; down, onto my knees, which is what anyone would do and is exactly what a man who wants your coat would like you to do. The younger one was on me before I had her head in my hands. His fist, the side of my face. I went over her legs. Two kicks, in the ribs, where the coat had swung open. He said, 'Where's the bag.' Not to me, exactly. Then his hand was inside my coat.

I felt the paper move. I want that down. His hand was on the page, through the lining, and it moved, and that is what four years and nine books and a fortnight had come to: a stranger's fingers in my pocket in the dark with Priya's blood running under my knee.

A door went below us. Two floors down, the bang a fire door makes, a man's voice, the lights on the lower landings coming on in order. He looked down. Not in; down. The older one said, 'Leave it,' from above, in the voice of a man who has been given a time and run out of it, and the hand came out of my coat empty, and they went up, not down, with Priya's bag, and I heard a door on the fifth, and nothing.

If anyone reading this wants to know what saved the map, it was a door. I would like it to have been me. It was a door, and a night guard who had heard a woman fall, and Calloway behind him taking the stairs two at a time. 10.54 by my watch. I had started counting when her head hit the step.

Forty-one seconds. She was out for forty-one seconds, face against the concrete, right arm under her at an angle I am not going to describe, and then she was back, and the first thing she said was: 'What day is it.' Not asking. Checking. I said Friday. She said, 'Friday. Fourteen-sixteen. Ask me the pound.' I did not ask her the pound. Blood was coming out of her hair above the right ear faster than I would have thought a head could spare, and I had my handkerchief on it, and then Calloway's scarf. I have been told since, twice, by people in uniform, that scalps bleed and it means nothing. Nobody told me that at 10.55.

The guard rang 999. The ambulance was in the loading bay at 11.09. She was sick once on the way, Calloway says. I was not in it. A wanted man does not get into an ambulance; it goes to a building with a desk and a form and, at the end of the form, a constable. I watched the doors shut on her and walked, collar up, to the nearest hospital, which is on the river and which I am not naming. Twenty-six minutes.

***

Calloway gave her name at the desk. Not her name. He had one ready, and a date of birth, and said both without stopping, and the woman typed them, because nobody in this country has ever been asked for a passport to bleed. <!-- VERIFY: NHS emergency departments register patients on the name and date of birth given, without identification; giving a false name is not itself an offence but may be if used to obtain something --> He told me afterwards that it belonged to someone who would not mind. I did not ask who. I am not writing it. It is the one thing in this entry I am keeping off the page on purpose, because a page is what they came for, and because Priya Raman is a name that was in a newspaper on Thursday in a sentence with the word "detained" in it, on bail, with a condition about Walbrook staff that she has broken every day this week in a room with me, and a hospital is a building full of people who read.

11.50. The wrist is broken: two bones, the doctor said, at the end nearest the hand, and showed me on a screen, and I understood none of it except the shape, which was wrong. They put it back behind a curtain, under something local, and I heard her, and I did not know she had that language. A splint tonight; plaster to the elbow once the swelling goes. <!-- VERIFY: a displaced distal radius fracture is commonly reduced in A&E under local anaesthetic and put in a backslab, with a full cast once the swelling settles -->

12.20. The head. Stitched, above the ear. I did not count the stitches; she will, and she will tell me. The scan at 12.50. Clear. A clear scan is not a clear head, the doctor said, and they are keeping her: a torch in the eyes every two hours, and the date. She knew where she was and what day it was. Out for under a minute, sick once: I gave both before I was asked, and the doctor looked at me the way Laura looks at a patient who has written things down. <!-- VERIFY: overnight observation after a head injury with brief loss of consciousness and one episode of vomiting, with a normal CT, is consistent with NICE head injury guidance; two-hourly observations as in ch. 36 and 38 -->

Me. The left side of my face, the ribs on the left, and nothing that stops a hand writing. I am not adding to that.

***

Second sitting. 2.20. A chair beside a bed, in a bay with the curtain half drawn, a name on the board above her that is not hers, which she has read three times since they brought her up at two. Her right arm is on a pillow. That side of her face is going a colour.

The bag. They took it. Her purse, with not much in it. The piece of paper the police gave her on Monday, with her name on it and a date four weeks off. The red book: her copy of the twelve codes and the order, Monday to Thursday in two colours of biro, and four days of our working. Everything in that bag, a man in Mayfair already has in better handwriting. I said that to her. She said, 'It was mine,' and shut her eyes, and she was right.

What they did not get is folded once in my coat. I have taken it out twice tonight and I am not going to again. Twelve codes, an order, a column in the firm's own figures. One corner is brown. That is hers. I am not writing round it.

Now the thing I have been thinking since 10.54, in the order I thought it.

For a fortnight the people who wanted the books said please. They sat in parks, left cards, stood a pace back from a kitchen door with their hands where you could see them. Tonight nobody said please, and the woman who says please was not on the stairs. I do not know what that means. I am writing that I do not know, because in a week I will want to say I knew.

I do know what the bag means. *Where's the bag.* They went for a bag because they were told a bag, and whoever told them has not been watching us this week, because this week there has been no bag. He has read about one. Nine books, and in every one of them I am a man with a bag on both shoulders, the way you carry a child's bag. I would bet the house four times over that a man in Mayfair has read every line of it. He knows how I carry things. He does not know that I have stopped, because the book that would tell him is this leaflet.

He knows the rest. The ninth book runs to Tuesday. #1405 is in it: Margaret's landline, every morning at nine, from a payphone. #1409: the village, the war memorial, a lane with one way in. #1412: the box on the Kentish Town Road, 200 yards from a journalist's door. Everything I have done this fortnight to keep my wife out of it is written down in my hand on a desk in Mayfair, and tonight the man who owns that desk stopped asking.

It is 2.40. There is a payphone at the end of this ward, by the lifts, and I have a pound. In six hours and twenty minutes I ring a number that is in no book of mine, at a time that is in the last one.
