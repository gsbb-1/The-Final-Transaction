# Chapter 7: Not yet

Second sitting. Past eleven. Laura has gone up. The people now.

After Haldane's handshake I went back to my desk and I did what I should have done at nine. I pulled the morning apart.

Priya had already started, unasked. She had the forty calls in a sheet with three columns she had added herself: when each member's bank confirmed, to the minute; what that member had lodged into its cushion, or swapped out and back, in the five afternoons before today; and whether any of those bonds had been on a fails list this week. The third column was the one that mattered and she had filled it in by hand, from Thursday's list and from memory, because the system does not keep that column; it does not think anyone would want it.

She turned her screen towards me without a word. Seven lines in the third column said *yes*.

Seven. Out of forty. Not one.

'Thursday was one,' I said.

'Thursday was one fund and one check. This is everyone, on a bad day, in a hurry.' She said it flat, the number first, as she does. 'Seven members paid this morning after half past eight, with cash they did not have at seven. All seven have had bonds going in and out of their cushion all week: failing in the morning, arriving at three, gone again by the next run. Those bonds are financing something else by day. This morning they lent them out again to pay us. I can't see the repo. I can see what it leaves. If Monday is worse than today, and it will be, those seven have to raise the cash again on bonds that are already out, and whoever has the bonds will be calling too.'

'How much.'

She told me. I am not writing the figure here because it is hers and because it was too small; she had only counted what she could see. I wrote the figure I thought was right underneath it, on paper, and she looked at it for a while.

'You've done this before,' she said.

'Yes.'

'Not this week.'

'No.'

She did not ask the next question. I said, 'Put it in a memo. One page. Numbers, no adjectives. Address it to Graham and copy me. I'm going to take it upstairs at half one.'

'He'll say not yet.'

'Then I'll have it in writing that he said it.'

***

Graham's room at 1.30. The door closed, which he never does. He had the one page in front of him and had read it; the corner was turned down where he had gone back to the top. Graham reads everything twice. It is the best thing about him and it slows him down by exactly the amount that matters.

'Seven,' he said.

'Seven that we can see.'

'This is a bad day, Jonas. Bad days look like this. People raise cash where they can.'

'They raised it on bonds that are already standing behind something else.'

'You think.'

'I think. It is what the pattern says.'

He said, 'What are you asking for.'

'Formal notification to the Bank this afternoon. Under our own obligations, not as a favour. That we have identified a pattern consistent with collateral being re-used across seven members, that it is material, and that we expect Monday's call to be larger than today's. Our number for Monday. And a request that they look across the other clearing houses and the repo market, because we can only see our own leg of it.'

'You want me to tell the Bank, on the Friday afternoon of the worst day since—' He stopped before he named a year. 'You want me to tell them that Walbrook's collateral is not sound. On a pattern.'

'I want you to tell them what we know.'

'Simon Carrow rang me at nine this morning,' he said, and he said it to the page. 'From the Treasury. He wanted the number for the morning call, and whether it was met, and he had it in the Chancellor's mouth within two hours. If I send this upstairs at two o'clock it is in Carrow's inbox at two fifteen and in a Cabinet briefing by four and on the front of a newspaper by Sunday with a photograph of this building, five weeks before an election, and nobody will read your assumptions.'

'Then they'll read them on Monday afternoon.'

'Not yet,' Graham said.

I waited. He has said *not yet* to me perhaps thirty times in five years and it has never once meant *later*.

'Let's see Monday,' he said. 'If Monday is what you say, we go together, with the day's figures. If Monday is a bad day that passes, as they do, as they have, then we have not put Walbrook's name in a report that cannot be withdrawn.' He put his hand flat on the page. 'I'm asking you to take this offline until then.'

I said, 'Graham, you filed the last one.'

I had never said that to him. He went a colour I have not seen on him and did not answer, and I took my copy of Priya's page and went out, and did not close the door behind me, because he never does.

***

At 2.10 I filed it myself.

There is a route. It exists for exactly this and nobody uses it, because using it is the end of your career at whichever firm you use it from. The Bank of England supervises us, and the law names the Bank as a prescribed person for clearing houses: a disclosure made to it is protected, which means they cannot lawfully sack you for making it, and that is the whole of the protection. There is no special door for people like me: same page, same address as for a clerk at a building society. I had bookmarked it when I was 37, the week after the memo went in and nothing came back, and I had never opened it since.

Priya's page. My figure for Monday underneath, with the assumptions. Four paragraphs that I wrote in eleven minutes and did not reread. My name, my title, my direct line. The time stamp said 14.21.

I did not tell Priya. I should have. She would have put her name on it and I did not want her name on it, and I am aware that is the same reasoning Graham uses about Walbrook.

Then I went back to work, because the two o'clock run would call again if the move held, and Monday does not care who I have written to.

***

At 3.40 the collateral database would not open.

I tried it twice. Then I tried the margin engine. Read-only. Then the member files: present, but the account-level view was gone, so I could see that a firm had paid and not what with.

The help desk said that a change had been applied to my profile at 15.32 at the request of the chief risk officer's office, and would I like a ticket. I said no.

So that was nine minutes. A disclosure went to the Bank at 2.21, and at 3.32 my access was cut to what a graduate gets on his first morning. An hour and eleven minutes. The Bank does not ring a firm to say who has written to it. Somebody rang somebody.

Priya had watched me try the database three times. She came round the desk without asking and stood behind my chair.

'Move,' she said.

I moved. She sat in my chair, logged me out of what was left, and logged in as herself. She opened the account-level view, the one that had been taken from me, and pulled the report I had been going to pull: forty members, how each had paid, what each had lodged this week. She sent it to the printer.

'You didn't see this,' she said.

I let her. She put the pages face down on my desk and went back to her own chair, her face the colour it goes when she is angry, which is not red.

The system will have logged it. Her login, my desk, that report, at 3.46 on the afternoon my own entitlements were cut. I knew that while she was doing it and I did not stop her. I would not have done it myself; I know what that line looks like in a log, and who reads logs. I let her do it for me. I am writing that down plainly, because it is the first thing in four years I have let someone else do that I would not, and I want the date on it.

At 4.00 Graham came to my desk. Not his room. Mine, in the open, where Priya could hear. He stood beside my chair and looked at the screen, which was showing a login error, and he did not look at me. In five years he has always looked at me.

'Richard has asked,' he said, 'and I'm passing this on as it was given to me, Jonas, so take it as that. Richard has asked that any personal notes relating to Walbrook's business be made available to him. Any notes, in any form, kept anywhere. He'd like to see them on Monday morning.'

'Personal notes.'

'Those were the words.'

'Whose words.'

He did not answer that. He said, 'It's a request. For now it's a request,' and he went back to his glass box and shut the door and sat with his back to the room.

Priya said, very quietly, 'What notes.'

I said, 'Work,' which is what I say to Laura.

***

Here is the thing I did not understand until 4.35, at a desk that no longer worked. I want it down exactly, because I will later pretend I had worked it out sooner.

*Personal notes.* Not *reports*, not *analysis*, not *files*. Haldane does not know the word *notebook* and he does not know the word *record*. He knows that something exists that is personal, in note form, about Walbrook's business, and he has known it long enough that the request was ready before my filing was an hour old. Graham, formal all Thursday, in at 5.50 this morning. Haldane's handshake at noon, *well held*, a half-second too long.

They did not learn this today. They learned it on Wednesday, at 6.41, when the phone chose the address for me. I wrote that night that someone had opened it. I did not know who.

I opened my sent items at 4.37. My mail still works; it is the only thing that does. The spread. #1399 on the right. On the left, the end of #1398: three lines of gilts, a member's code, a line promised to us that failed for two days, the cash that stood in for it, where I thought the bond had gone. Graham's initials on a quarterly report. The word contained.

So they have the first page of one entry and the tail of another. The page tells them my age. The tail tells them what the books are for. Together they tell them the thing that matters: that there are 1,398 others, and that someone has been counting.

That is what Graham's four words on Wednesday were. That is what the request at four o'clock was. That is why I went to Lewisham tonight, and why the old books stay there.

And one more thing, which I did not see until I had written the rest down. Carrow had our morning number by nine. The Chancellor had it by eleven. My filing went in at 2.21 and my access was gone by 3.32, and Haldane's request for my notes was ready and worded and in Graham's mouth by four. Walbrook does not move at that speed.

*Whose words*, I asked Graham, and he did not answer. I think he did not answer because the answer is not Richard Haldane.
