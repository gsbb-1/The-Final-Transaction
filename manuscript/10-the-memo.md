# Chapter 10: The memo

#1403

Graham rang at 11.10am. Saturday. He has never rung me on a Saturday.

'They want you at the Treasury at four. Horse Guards Road. Bring your pass. Bring nothing else.'

'Who's they?'

'Everyone, Jonas.'

Laura was taking Elsie to the park. I said I had to go in. She said, 'On a Saturday,' not as a question, and then watched me put the ninth notebook in my bag, the way she has watched me do it 1,400 times, and said nothing, which was worse.

Train from Herne Hill to Victoria. Walked the rest, across the park. Sky the colour of a screen that has been turned off. The building is a long stone thing with a courtyard in it, and the man at the desk knew my name before I said it. Visitor's lanyard, red. Bag through the scanner. Nobody looked twice at a black notebook. Why would they.

Third floor. A room with a long table and a view of the park and nothing on the walls. Eight of us, including me.

The Chancellor at the head. Caroline Strand. Smaller than on television, and stiller. Simon Carrow on her left, who Graham has mentioned over the years the way you mention a cousin. The Governor of the Bank of England, who I had seen on television and who is shorter in a room, and a woman from the Bank's supervision side, which for us is the regulator, so that was two chairs and one institution. <!-- VERIFY: the Bank of England supervises UK clearing houses; whether the Governor would attend in person, and whether a separate conduct regulator would also be present, is uncertain --> Richard Haldane. Graham, as far from me as the table allowed. A young man with a laptop who was not introduced at all.

Strand didn't thank me for coming. 'Mr Whitlock. Richard, if you would.'

Haldane: at 2.06pm Walbrook had received formal notice, through its clearing banks, from the fund I don't name here. I never have. Carrow, not looking up: the fund's chief had rung the Chancellor's private office himself at twenty past nine that morning, which is why we were all in the room before the banks had finished typing. Line one. Positions to be closed from Monday's open, in size order. Collateral to be returned as positions closed. Bonds to be sold as the collateral came free. All of it.

'I'm told you are the person who can explain what that means,' Strand said. 'In terms I can use.'

So I did. The thing I have written 1,400 times, out loud, in one go, to a Chancellor. It took less time than I thought.

The fund has lodged a very large quantity of government bonds with us, as security for what it owes. Those bonds don't sit still. The banks that cleared them have borrowed against them, and lent them on, and the people they lent them to have pledged them again, so the same bond is standing behind three or four promises at once. That's normal. It works as long as nobody asks for the bond back. On Monday the largest holder in the system asks for the bond back. Everyone who was using it has to find another one, that morning, from a market where everyone is looking for the same thing and prices are falling. The ones who can't find it, we call. The ones who can't pay, fail. And when a member fails, their losses are ours.

'How many promises,' Strand said. 'You said three or four.'

'On the lines I've looked at. On average. Some are worse.'

'How much, on Monday?'

'I don't know. My access to the collateral ledgers was removed yesterday afternoon.' I didn't look at Graham. 'Mr Haldane can tell you.'

Haldane said numbers were being run. Someone on my team is running them, then. I hope it's Priya. I hope it isn't.

Strand looked at Carrow. Carrow opened a folder, took out a document and slid it across the table to me. Six pages, stapled. He did it the way you pass the salt.

Walbrook letterhead. My name. A date, three years ago, nearly to the month. "Collateral re-use in cleared markets: a note on concentration and horizon." Below it, in a sentence I could have recited: "On current growth in re-pledging, the author estimates that the system has approximately three years before a moderate shock becomes unmanageable."

I was 37. I said about three years. I wasn't far out.

Top right, a stamp: RECEIVED, HM TREASURY, and the date, a week after I wrote it. Below it, in biro, a set of initials I didn't know and two words: "Noted. File."

'We have had this for three years, Mr Whitlock,' Strand said. 'You will understand, then, that nobody at this table needs the problem explained to them. We need it solved.'

I turned the pages. My tables. My footnotes. Someone had underlined "approximately three years" in pencil, lightly, as if they might want to rub it out.

Graham was looking at the window.

'Before the seven o'clock call on Monday,' Strand said, 'Walbrook will issue a statement.' Carrow passed a second sheet. Treasury paper, two paragraphs. Walbrook's collateral arrangements are fully sound and adequate to all foreseeable conditions. 'The Bank will issue one alongside it. I will say the same on breakfast television at 7.20. The withdrawal of one participant is being managed in an orderly way.' She said "orderly" without a flicker. 'The statement is signed by Richard, by Mr Pryce as chief risk officer, and by you, as head of collateral risk. I need the signatures by six.'

'Why me?'

'Because yesterday afternoon you filed a paper with the regulator saying the opposite, and by Monday someone will have told a journalist that you did.' Not unkind. Exact. 'If the man who raised the alarm signs the all-clear, there is no story. If he doesn't, the story is him, and a story about a man is easier to run than a story about collateral.'

'Is it true?'

'I'm sorry?'

'The statement. Is it true?'

She looked at me for exactly as long as it took to decide I had meant it. 'Mr Whitlock, the system is held up by the belief that it is held up. That is not cynicism. It is the mechanism. If the Chancellor and the clearing house and the Bank say on Monday morning that the collateral is sound, and nobody credible says otherwise, then the banks post their margin, the fund sells into a market that still exists, and the collateral is sound. If we say anything else, it isn't. You of all people know which of those two mornings you would rather have.'

Then the other thing. She did it in the same voice.

'I understand you have kept personal notes over some years relating to Walbrook's business. Those notes contain market-sensitive information about named institutions. The Treasury would like them lodged here, for safekeeping, until the situation has stabilised.' <!-- VERIFY: whether the Treasury has any power to require this; it is presented as a request with an implied threat, not an order --> 'Richard's view is that they are in any case Walbrook's property. I'd rather not test that. I'd rather they were safe with us than reported stolen from the clearing house. I think you would too.'

Haldane looked at the table. Graham looked at the window. The man from the Bank looked at me as if I were a number that hadn't come in yet.

'I'd like the night,' I said.

'You have until six on Monday morning, Mr Whitlock. That is more than the night.'

'Then I'd like the night first.'

Strand considered that and found it was arithmetic. 'Simon will give you a number,' she said. 'Use it before you need to.'

Carrow gave me a card. The young man with the laptop stopped typing.

I asked if I could keep the memo. Carrow said it was a copy. I said I knew it was a copy, and he looked at Strand, and she nodded, and I put six pages and the statement into the bag with the ninth notebook, and nobody in the room knew that they were sitting a foot apart.

Lift down. Graham in it with me, because he had to be. Three floors of nothing. Then, to the doors: 'I filed it, Jonas. I filed it and I told you I'd filed it, and you went home and wrote it down.'

'Yes.'

'Every day. For three years.'

'Four.'

He didn't say anything else. The doors opened.

Home 6.30. Laura had made supper. Elsie threw peas. I ate what was in front of me and could not have told you what it was. Laura asked how the Treasury was. I said big. She said, 'You've got your hands in your pockets again,' and went to run the bath.

Now. Kitchen table. Memo on the left. Statement in the middle, two paragraphs, a line for my name. This on the right.

Count. Three years I have been right and quiet. Six pages they have had the whole time. One signature they want, on one page, before 6am Monday. One set of notebooks, nine, eight in a place they have not found and one on this table. Thirty-one hours.

What I don't know. Whether Strand is right that saying it makes it so. She might be. It has worked for 400 years. <!-- VERIFY: rhetorical; no specific claim intended --> Whether Priya has the number yet, and what it is, and whether anyone will let her say it. Whether "reported stolen" was a threat or a kindness. Both, I think. That is how Strand talks.

I went through the memo again, slowly, because I didn't trust what I'd seen under the stamp with seven people watching.

Below "Noted. File." there is a second mark. Smaller. Different biro. "Retrieved from registry," then initials, then a date.

Wednesday. This Wednesday.

#1400. My birthday. A day and a half before a tanker went down in a strait that most of them couldn't find on a map. The morning Graham opened an email that wasn't meant for him.

They didn't pull my memo because the market fell. They pulled it before anything had fallen at all.

They weren't getting ready for a crisis. They were getting ready for me.
