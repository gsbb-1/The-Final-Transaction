# Chapter 51: Home

The aeroplane that Khalid Al-Mansouri took on Wednesday night was not the noon flight. The noon flight had left with his seat empty, and he had watched the clock pass twelve in a room in St James's with his hands folded on a table and the screens quiet for the first time in twenty days. He had asked Nasser to book the next one, for the evening, and Nasser, who booked things, had booked two seats.

'You need not,' Khalid had said.

'My sons are at home, sir.' It was the whole of the answer, and it was the first time he had mentioned them without being asked.

They landed at six on Thursday morning, home time. Khalid had not slept. He had spent the night in a window seat with the blind half down, doing what he did in a crisis, which was to write the worst case in a column and read it back until it was no longer interesting. Arrest at the foot of the steps: a car, a room, a man from the council's secretariat whom he had known since school. A decree read to an empty chair on Wednesday, for the record. The charter said what it said, and he had acted within its letter for exactly as long as he had meant to. He had decided in the small hours of Monday that it was a price he could pay. He found, at thirty-eight thousand feet, that the knowing had worn away the fear and left only the arithmetic, and that the arithmetic was that of a man of forty-seven with a bag.

Nobody was at the foot of the steps.

There was a car, which he had expected, and in the car a man he had not: the council's secretary, who had rung him on Monday to be kind. He was grey in the face and wearing the wrong tie. He did not say good morning. He said, 'Mr Al-Mansouri. The council has asked me to bring you directly,' and Khalid, who had been trained to hear what was not said, heard that the sentence had a second half that the man had been told not to use. *Directly* was not the word for an arrest.

***

The council sat in the long room with the windows on the water. He had been in it perhaps forty times. Nine chairs on the near side of the table, and one, at the far end, for the chair of the fund, which for eleven years had been his.

It was occupied. Not by a successor. By his own folder, which someone had set there on Wednesday morning, so that the decree should have something to be read to. He understood this from the secretary's face. The decree had been read at ten, home time, to the folder; and the folder had stayed where it was, because by the time anyone thought of taking it away, the London opening was a rumour on the secretary's telephone, and nobody wished to be the man who moved it.

The first news had been Nasser's, a single line at ten to two, home time: *the transactions are complete*. The numbers came after, made by the council's own people from their screens in the afternoon, in the manner of a family counting the silver after a burglary. The pound. The long end of the gilt market. The ten largest holdings of the fund, marked at Wednesday's close, which were worth more than they had been on the morning the tanker burned, by an amount the secretary read out in a flat voice and which Khalid did not repeat to himself. A man could not like a number of that kind and be seen to like it.

By evening, home time, the decree had been withdrawn. Not revoked: withdrawn. The wording, which the secretary now read to him as if it were a postal notice, said that the council noted the managing director's actions on the previous day *with appreciation*, and that matters of authority at the fund would be considered *in the ordinary course*. Khalid recognised it. It was the language of eight men who had needed to be on the right side of something by dinner. He thought of the four he had met in two capitals who had all said *not on my signature*, and of the fact that of all the signatures in the matter, the only one that had gone on the page without a second sentence was his own.

'We should like to thank you,' said the oldest of the nine, whom he had known thirty years. It was said stiffly, to the table, and it was true, and it cost the man something.

'I acted without the board.'

'You did.' A pause. 'The board has met. It would have refused. It has asked that the minutes record that it would have refused.' Another pause, and then, in the accent of the old men, the kind that does not translate: 'And that the managing director's judgement was nonetheless sound.'

He thanked them. It took eleven minutes.

***

There was a second empty chair, on the long side, where the family sat. He had not mentioned it, and nobody had.

Faisal.

Khalid had his cousin's name in his mouth and did not say it. It had been said at the council on Wednesday, he gathered, by one man, in a low voice, after the decree was withdrawn, and had gone nowhere. Faisal had not been at the table. He had not been at the office on Wednesday, nor at the house, nor at his club. His private office, the quiet one at the family's estate that had never once been named in London, had sent word at four o'clock on Wednesday afternoon, home time, that the principal was *indisposed*. A word that, at home, was a verdict. It would be said in this room, in the same way, for a month, and then it would not be said.

Khalid did not ask where he had gone. He knew, as he knew the combination of his own safe, that the answer would be shaped like the answer to the question of who had been paid and by whom and when, and that nobody in the room wished him to know it, or wished him to ask, and that if he did he would be told, kindly, by the oldest of the nine, that there were things a family did not put on paper. A shell with a correspondence address in the Gulf. A broker in London. A trade nine days before a ship was sunk. He had seen the page; Whitlock had held it up. Faisal's name had not been spoken on a screen by anyone. Faisal's name had not needed to be.

He was not seen in public again. Khalid learned it that way, without a day for it to have happened on: a diary that went on being printed, with one name omitted. It is the way things are done there. A man is removed from a list rather than from a room.

He thought, briefly and without satisfaction, of the account of the grudge he had been supposed to carry. Seven years, Crane. Seven years, Faisal. He had left the fund in Mayfair because he had decided that a man who did not think of the people on the other side of his trades as people could not be trusted with a pound. He had not decided anything about his cousin. He had simply never quite looked.

***

Nasser's boys were at the airport when he left the council. Three of them: nineteen, sixteen, eleven. They were standing in a row by the barrier in school jackets, though it was not a school day, because their mother had ironed them, and the eldest had a bunch of something from a garage forecourt. They had been told to wait. Nasser, coming through the doors behind Khalid with his case, stopped as a man stops who has been struck from the side, and the youngest broke the row and ran.

Khalid walked on a little way, and then, because it was a fine morning and nobody was watching, stopped, and let the car wait.

He had a telephone in his pocket, the plain one the office kept for him, with one number in it that was not on the register. At the entrance to the car park he took it out. He had drafted the message in the plane, on the back of the menu, and crossed it out four times. In the end it was formal and short, because he had no other kind.

*Mr Whitlock. The instruction was carried out as directed. I am in your debt and shall not say so again. K. Al-Mansouri.*

Twenty-two words. He counted them, which was a habit he had caught from the man he was writing to. He read it once, to see whether it would improve, and found that it would not, and sent it.

He was in the car and the gates had opened before the telephone moved in his hand. A single line. He had expected a courtesy, perhaps nothing, since the man was wanted by the police, or had been, and might be in a room somewhere without his telephone. Instead:

*Received. Thank you for the bid. I think we were both lucky. J.W.*

Khalid read it twice. Then, because he was alone in the back of the car with the glass up and had been awake for thirty hours and his father was waiting at a house forty minutes away, he found he had to look out of the window for a little while. He had been thanked that morning by nine men and a decree. It was this he would remember: not the thanks, but the correction. *I think we were both lucky.* Whitlock had put in the one phrase that a careful man leaves out, and had not been able to stop himself.

He did not reply. He deleted nothing. He told the driver to go on.
