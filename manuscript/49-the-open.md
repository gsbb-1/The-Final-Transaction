# Chapter 49: The open

Wednesday, #1421. A later sitting. I wrote nothing at six or at eight and I will not pretend that I did. This is ten o'clock, the thing the other 1,420 entries were for, and I am putting it down in the order it happened, with the times, because I trust no other way of putting it down.

I had been in the room less than ten minutes. It is Walbrook's risk room, where I have worked for five years, and nobody looked at me when I came through the door with blood on my shirt, which I took as a kindness. The wall screens were as they always are: the call sheet, the fails list, the balances. Only the lines were not going green. Nine of the twelve member lines were red. Two were black, with a word where the number should be. Richard Haldane stood at the back with his arms folded, the colour of a candle, and as I passed he said 'Jonas' in a voice with nothing in it, and I said 'Richard', and that was the whole of our conversation.

A desk had been cleared for me. A telephone with a lead running into the floor, and a microphone on a stalk. The lead went to St James's: a voice line and nothing else, because Walbrook had cut every other link at six on the Bank's advice, and a voice is the one thing that carries no virus. At the other end, the fund's sterling-book head, the woman who had put my map into her machine on Sunday, with Nasser standing behind her. At the desk beside me, a Bank of England officer in a headset, holding the deputy governor's letter, whose job was to repeat my words into the line a second after I said them, so that nobody could say afterwards that the order had been mine alone. On a speaker by my elbow, the hospital: Priya, in a side room with her wrist in plaster and Graham's sixteen box files open round her on the bed. I could hear paper.

On the central screen, the Bank's deputy governor, on video, in front of a flag. 'Mr Whitlock. You are the person who has the sequence.'

'Yes.'

'We shall need it without ambiguity.'

I laid the page on the desk face down. It has Walbrook's September figures on it, and one corner brown with Priya's blood. I did not need it. I turned it over anyway, so that I would not be tempted to look.

***

At ten the exchanges opened, two hours late, on a Bank statement that used the word *orderly* three times. In the first minute the 2038 line, the one the second member's dead parent was still being sold out of, went from 97.40 to 96.85. A sound in the room, not a word. It was the shape I wrote down on a Tuesday when I was 36, bonds failing to arrive, except that now it was every line at once and the whole of London on one side of it.

'First,' I said.

There is no thrill in saying it. It is a man reading the next line of something learned by heart, and it comes out as flat as a timetable. 'Buy the 2038. What is left of the second member's book. Four hundred and ten million. At the market.'

The officer said it into the line. Four hundred and ten million pounds of government bonds, which is the price of a hospital, spoken into a telephone in the City of London at 10.01.

Eleven seconds. 'Done,' said the woman in St James's. 'Four-ten, average 96.71.' And on the screen the 2038 stopped. Not rose. Stopped, like a man on a stair who has found a step that was not there. 96.71. 96.71. 96.72.

'Second. The 2045. The third member's collateral. Six hundred.'

That one I knew as I know my own address. The third had paid its call on the Bank's money and had been selling the 2045 for four hours. I said six hundred. It was said. 'Done.' And the 2045, which had fallen all fortnight in steps of a quarter, took a bid the size of a street, and did something I had not seen on any screen in that room for twenty days. It went up. Four pence on a thirty-billion line. I counted.

Someone at the back made a noise like a man who has held his breath since Tuesday.

'Third. The fourth member. The 2052, two hundred and ninety. I think the 2052 first, then the 2041.'

'You think,' said the deputy governor.

I had not heard myself say it. I have said it for four years. It is in the nine books in about every other entry, and nobody has ever asked me to stop. Now the deputy governor of the Bank of England had a clock and a country's money in his hand and was waiting, politely, for me to take it out of my sentence.

'Yes,' I said. 'I think. I have never seen it. Nobody has. I have seen what it leaves behind, and the fourth member's fingerprints say the 2052 goes about ninety seconds before the 2041. That is what I have. If you wanted a man who knows, you have not got one. You have the man who counted.'

The line hissed. Then Priya, from a hospital bed, under a name on the board that is not hers: 'He's said that to me for four years. He's been wrong about the order twice, both times by under a minute. I'd bet the lot on it.' A rustle. 'Go on, Jonas. Say it plainly.'

'The 2052 first. Two hundred and ninety.'

10.09. 'Done.' The 2052 printed 94.10, then 94.30. The deputy governor looked off camera, nodded to someone, and did not speak again for half an hour.

What the Bank was doing is the part nobody will see. Every purchase went through one of the fund's three banks, which took the bonds to the Bank of England and came back inside the hour with cash. The fund never held more than a name's worth at once. Behind it was one page of Treasury paper, signed a little after seven that morning by a woman who had put her initials beside a sentence about three years. What I saw was only the numbers going out and the numbers coming back, and a line on a side screen that said BANK OF ENGLAND: RECEIVED.

***

10.14. I called the fifth member. 'The 2036. Three hundred and forty.' The woman in St James's said, 'Three-forty, 2036, done,' and the speaker said, 'No.'

'No,' said Priya. 'Not the 2036. The fifth lodged the 2038, I've got it under my hand, Q2, the fourteenth. The 2036 is the sixth's.'

I had the page face down and my mouth had said 2036 and three hundred and forty million pounds of a country's money had gone on a bond the fifth did not own.

'Say again.'

'It's the sixth's. You've bought the sixth early, that's all. We lose a couple of minutes, not the morning.' Paper moved. 'The fifth is the 2038, and you took the best of it at 10.01, so it's two hundred, not three-forty. I'm sorry. Do you want it again?'

'No.' I had it as soon as she said it, as you have a name once someone says the first letter. 'The 2038. Two hundred. Then the sixth.' The sweat went cold down my back. 'Thank you.'

'Thank me later.'

I want it written that I got a line wrong in the one hour of my life when it counted, and that a woman with a broken wrist caught it from a bed, and that I had spent four years explaining to her why a man should be wrong in private.

By 10.20 the screens had a different colour on them. It is not that a price rises; it is that the red goes. The fails list, forty lines at ten, was nineteen, then eleven, then six. The call sheet went amber, then the pale grey that means *settled, awaiting confirmation*, then the first green, at 10.27, on the sixth member. And the bonds that Crane had borrowed and sold, because my map told him they would fall, were rising in every line, in step, because each of them had a buyer under it that did not go away, and a buyer who does not go away is the only thing that has ever turned a price. A price that turns is a debt somebody has to pay.

At 10.31 I called the seventh, and then, from habit, from the fingerprints, I began to call the eighth, and Priya said, 'No.'

'It is the order I—'

'It's the order you had. Nine, eleven, twelve. Their bonds are sitting at the thirteenth, we get them home before anything touches it. Then eight and ten. The thirteenth last, Jonas. If we go near it early we tell them where we are.' Then, because she is Priya: 'Say you've got it.'

The thirteenth is a code I had never once written in a notebook. I had learned it at twenty to nine, from her, by telephone. 'Nine, eleven, twelve,' I said. 'Eight and ten. Thirteenth last. I think—'

'No. I know.'

That was the second.

***

10.42. The woman in St James's said it, and her voice had lost its edges; she had been on the line forty-two minutes without a break. 'Mr Whitlock. One of our three banks is also a prime broker. It should not have told us what it has told us.' A breath. 'It sent a margin call to a fund in Mayfair at 10.42. Greywether. Two point one billion, due by two o'clock. They have, I am told, less than a third of it in cash.'

I thought: this is where they will look at me.

They looked at the screens. It was not Walbrook's number and had no business on the wall; it was a call between a bank and a fund. The deputy governor said, 'We shall not comment on any firm.' Then, after a pause that was the first human thing he had done in an hour: 'Thank you, Mr Whitlock.'

Greywether is not a bank. It cannot borrow from the Bank of England. It had a position that had paid by the minute for twenty days, and the position had been built on a map that I wrote, and that was taken from me on a Friday night on a staircase, and the same map was turning it inside out in my voice, line by line. It cannot meet the call. I knew that by 10.43 with a certainty I have felt about nothing else this month, and I did not feel what I thought I would. I felt a hand on my elbow. The officer, who had held his headset for forty-two minutes, said, 'Sir, you've got blood on your cuff,' and I said, 'It isn't mine.' It was mine. From the nose, from Cannon Street.

***

11.20. The last line. By then the room had stopped looking at me and started looking at the clock, which is how I knew it was working.

'Thirteenth,' I said. 'The code Priya gave me. Three hundred and eighty million, the 2049 and the short stuff behind it. At the market.' And then, because there was nothing left of the sentence to hang it on: 'I think the whole of it.'

It was said. For one second, nothing, the whole room hearing a woman in St James's not answering. Then, 'Done. Three-eighty, average 91.2.' And the thirteenth, which a man in Mayfair had spent five weeks quietly building a position against, which could have broken the chain at the end of the line, found a price already turned and a buyer already there, and did nothing at all. It held. There is no drama in a line holding. It is a number that stays where it is.

At 11.52 the last line on the call sheet went green. Eight minutes before the new noon. The clearing house met its deadline with the Bank's money gone round the building twice and the fund's proceeds on top. Haldane did not clap. Nobody did. A man by the window began to cry quietly, and no one looked at him.

'Mr Whitlock,' said the deputy governor. 'Is it over?'

Every one of the times in four years I have written *I think*, I felt it in my throat like a coin.

'Yes,' I said.

Then I wrote it on the back of the page, on the brown corner, before I had said anything else. It is the first line in 1,421 entries I have written without a hedge. I will see in the morning whether I was entitled to it.
