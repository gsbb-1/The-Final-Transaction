# Chapter 5: Hormuz

#1401

The telephone beside the bed did not ring. It had been chosen for that. It lit, and the light was enough.

Khalid Al-Mansouri was awake before he had read the name on the screen. It was 2.52 in the morning. He had been asleep for a little over two hours, and in the first second of waking he knew, as he had known on perhaps four other nights in his life, that the day had already started without him.

'Sir.' It was Nasser, his chief of staff, who did not telephone after midnight for anything that could wait until prayers. 'A ship has gone down in the strait.'

'What ship.'

'A tanker. Loaded. Out of the Gulf, eastbound, about twenty miles short of the narrows.' <!-- VERIFY: eastbound traffic lane through the Strait of Hormuz, approximate distances; the outbound lane runs along the Omani side -->  'There was an explosion a little after one. She was on fire within minutes and she has gone. The crew are in the water or on other ships. Nobody is saying yet how many.'

'Whose ship.'

'Flagged in the Marshall Islands, owned through a company in Athens, chartered by a trading house in Singapore, carrying crude for a refinery in Korea.' Nasser had the figures in order already, which meant he had been awake longer than he admitted. 'Everybody's and nobody's. The usual arrangement.'

Khalid sat up. The room was cool and black and smelt faintly of the sea, which was a hundred metres away beyond the glass. He put his feet on the floor and found his watch by habit.

'What are they saying.'

'The Americans have a destroyer on the way. The Omanis have put out a notice to mariners. The traffic is stopping.' A pause. 'Sir, nobody has closed the strait. Nobody has to. The ships are closing it themselves. Every owner who has a vessel in the Gulf tonight is calling his captain and telling him to drop anchor where he is.' <!-- VERIFY: whether a de facto closure through owner and insurer decisions, rather than a formal order, is the realistic first-hour response -->

'And the insurers will follow by breakfast.'

'By breakfast,' Nasser agreed.

'Who did it.'

The pause this time was longer.

'That is why I called you,' Nasser said, 'rather than letting you sleep.'

***

The car came at 3.20. The city at that hour belonged to the street-cleaners and the towers, which stayed lit whether anyone was in them or not. Khalid had dressed as he would for a working day because he did not know what else to do, and because the men he would be speaking to before dawn would notice if he had not.

In the car he watched the news on the small screen in the seat-back, with the sound low. He changed channels four times. Each time the picture was the same: a column of smoke in the dark, filmed from a long way off by someone's telephone, and under it a line of white text. The text changed from channel to channel and the changes were the news.

The British channel said *Tanker sunk in Strait of Hormuz: cause unknown.*

The first American channel said *Attack on tanker closes vital oil route.*

The second American channel had a retired admiral in a studio in Washington, where it was early evening, and the admiral was explaining with the help of a map which navies operated in those waters, and which of them had the means to put a hole in a ship that size, and which of them had been making statements lately about freedom of navigation. He did not say the name of Khalid's country. He pointed at it.

Khalid turned the sound off altogether and watched the man's hand.

He knew, or believed he knew, that it was not true. He knew the four men who would have had to give such an order and he had sat with two of them that week. Men who have decided to sink a ship do not spend an evening complaining about the price of a wedding. He had thought about that all the way from the house and he had reached the point, familiar from his banking years, where the only honest thing to say was: I believe this, and I cannot prove it, and the people who matter will not take my word.

The fund's building stood on the waterfront with its back to the city. On an ordinary day it was the quietest large building he had ever worked in. Tonight there were lights on three floors.

***

By four o'clock there were nine people in the room that the staff called the bridge, because the previous managing director had liked ships. Khalid did not like ships, particularly tonight, but the name had outlasted the man.

'Oil first,' he said.

The night desk had the number already. Brent crude was not trading in any normal sense at that hour, but the futures that traded in Asia had opened and gone straight up, and the number on the screen was the kind that would be in headlines by London breakfast. <!-- VERIFY: overnight trading of Brent futures during Asian hours, and the plausible size of a first-hour move on a Hormuz closure --> Someone gave him a percentage and he nodded and did not react, which was a thing he had learned in his twenties from a man in a trading room in Canary Wharf who had reacted, once, and never been allowed to forget it.

'Our own position in oil does not matter tonight,' Khalid said. 'The price can go where it likes. We own the ground it comes out of. What matters is the other side. Where is our money that is not here.'

That was a larger question than it sounded, and he watched the room understand it.

The fund existed to put the wealth of a small country into the rest of the world, in the hope that the rest of the world would still be there when the oil was not. It owned airports, pipelines, hotels, a stake in a football club that embarrassed him, and a great deal of government debt. Most of all it owned government debt, because governments paid, and because the debt of the United Kingdom and the United States and Germany was the thing that every other thing in finance stood on.

'I want a list,' he said. 'Every asset we hold in London. Not the summary. The lines. And against each line I want to know where it physically is: whose books, whose vault, whose name. Where there is collateral, I want to know where the collateral sits.'

'Sir, the London desk will be in at seven their time—'

'Then wake them. And wake Boston after that. I want London first because London is where they will think of first.'

'Who, sir?'

Khalid looked at the muted screen, where the retired admiral had been replaced by a younger man outside a government building in the dark, holding an umbrella.

'Anyone who needs to be seen to do something by Monday,' he said.

***

The list took two hours and arrived in pieces.

Khalid read it at the long table with the lights down and a glass of water that nobody had seen him pour. The others had learned to leave him alone with paper. The sky outside had gone from black to the colour of a bruise.

Most of it he knew already, in the way a man knows his own house: the office towers held through a company in Jersey; the stake in a water utility; the loans; the shares; the sterling that moved in and out each day through four banks on the same square mile. None of it was the problem. A government that wished to freeze a building could freeze a building, and the building would still be there when the lawyers had finished.

The problem was the bonds.

The fund held British government debt in a quantity that made it one of the five or six largest foreign holders in the world. <!-- VERIFY: scale of the largest foreign holders of gilts; a single sovereign fund among the top five is plausible but should be checked --> The bonds did not sit in a vault. They sat in accounts, and a large part of them did not sit still even there. They had been lent out, pledged, posted. They stood behind the fund's own hedges, which were cleared, like everyone's, through a clearing house in the City of London, and the clearing house held them as collateral against the day the hedges went wrong. <!-- VERIFY: a sovereign fund's cleared derivatives positions would normally be cleared through a bank acting as clearing member, with collateral posted via that member; the chapter leaves the exact route vague -->

He ran his finger down the column that said *Collateral location* and stopped where the figures were largest.

*Walbrook Clearing House.* <!-- VERIFY: amount deliberately not stated; author to decide the figure -->

He had known the name for years as a line on a quarterly report. He had never been inside the building and could not have said what street it was on. He knew, as every man in his position knew, that the clearing house was the place where the whole market's promises were netted against each other and secured, and that when people spoke of the system being sound, this was the kind of place they meant.

'In whose name,' he said, 'does Walbrook hold these.'

The head of the night desk looked at the paper. 'Through the members, sir. Three banks. Our bonds are posted in their client accounts.' <!-- VERIFY: client account segregation at a UK CCP, and whether a fund's identity is visible to the clearing house -->

'So if someone in London decides tomorrow to freeze what belongs to us,' Khalid said, 'they will first have to work out which of it does.'

Nobody answered. It had not been a question.

He sat back. Through the glass the first call to prayer was beginning somewhere in the city, thin and far off, and on the muted screen a caption had appeared beneath the man with the umbrella: *UK Treasury: "monitoring the situation closely".*

He thought about the word *closely*. He had spent his twenties in banks in London and Boston and had learned what the word meant in official English. It meant that someone had been woken.

'Nasser.'

'Sir.'

'I want a draft of the notice we would give if we were to withdraw our collateral from London and sell the bonds. The whole position. I want to know what notice we must give, to whom, and how many days it would take to be done.' <!-- VERIFY: notice periods for withdrawing collateral through a clearing member and for unwinding cleared positions; days rather than hours is the working assumption -->

The room had gone very still.

'I am not giving the order,' he said. 'I am asking what it would say. Have it on this table by the time London opens.'

'And then, sir?'

Khalid looked at the smoke on the screen. It had not moved in six hours. The ship under it was two hundred metres down.

'And then,' he said, 'we find out who did this before someone decides it was us.'
