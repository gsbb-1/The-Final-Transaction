# Chapter 50: Without screens

Sebastian Crane had never kept a screen in his room. The traders downstairs had eleven each. He had a desk, two chairs, a Persian rug he had bought for the pleasure of being told what it was worth, and a steel cabinet behind the desk that had held, until last week, nothing but cigars. He liked to say that a man who watches the price cannot see the market. The line had been quoted in two profiles. He believed it, which is the most dangerous thing that can be said about a line of that kind.

At 11.04 on Wednesday morning he was sitting in front of the telephone and not looking at it.

He had watched the broadcast at six with his lawyers on speaker, and had understood it before the second of them had finished saying *defamation*. Eleven minutes of a tired man in a borrowed shirt, naming the twelve in order, and then the dates, and then himself. He had admired the order of it. Then he had understood what the buyer in London at seven was for, and had said, quite pleasantly, to nobody, 'They're going to buy what I'm short,' and the lawyers had stopped talking.

He had done what could be done. At eight, before the exchanges opened, he had closed the shorts in the first eight lines, through six brokers, and kept the bonds, so that when the fund came to the open he would be standing there with the goods it needed. It was a clean move and it had made him money, and he had known as he made it that it would not be enough, because it touched the sum and not the man who was to give it. The rest of the book he had left exactly as it was. Then he had sent the two men to find the man. That, too, had been done. Crane did not examine it. The man had arrived at Walbrook at twenty to ten with blood on his shirt and his hands free, and what had happened between Crane did not yet know and was not going to ask.

At 10.42 a prime broker had rung his head trader with a figure, and the man had walked in and said it in front of him. Two point one billion, by two. At 10.50 the second prime broker had rung, politely, to say that the firm would not be extending the terms of the facility in the current environment. At 10.56 the third had not rung at all, which he understood to be the real message. A fund of his size runs on three lines of credit and a reputation, and by eleven all three were closed to him. He had spent twenty-six years knowing precisely what such a morning looked like from the other side of the table. He had been, he supposed, rather good at the other side of the table.

'It's a squeeze,' his head trader said. He had stayed in the doorway. He had been with Crane nine years and had been awake since four. 'Every line in the rest of the book is going up in step. There is nobody to sell us the other side. By one o'clock we will have been closed out in four of the five books. By two, in all of them. The partners' capital goes first, Sebastian. Then the investors'.' She waited. 'Nobody lends to a man who is short gilts on the day the Bank of England is buying them.'

'No,' he said. 'I suppose not.'

'I need you to tell me whether to call the lawyers.'

'You may ring whom you like,' Crane said. 'Thank you.'

He went. Crane heard him go down the stairs to the floor, and then the particular silence of a floor of eleven-screened desks on which every line is the colour of a bruise. He had known it, in his time, from the other end.

***

He rang Anthony Pell. He did it himself, from the handset, because there was nobody he wished to ask.

Twenty years. A man who sat at the board of Walbrook and had sat on his own board, and who had given him, a fortnight ago, twelve names over the telephone, from an appendix to a board pack, and a pleasant lunch afterwards. It rang six times. It was answered by a woman at Pell's house who said that Sir Anthony was not available, that she could not say when he would be, and that she would pass on the message. He thanked her. He rang the club. He rang the office, and a secretary he had known for fifteen years said, with a politeness he recognised as the voice of a person reading a script, that Sir Anthony had asked not to be put through to anybody this morning, and she was very sorry.

He put the telephone down and found that he was smiling, which was habit. The smile was for the room.

He tried to see it. He was good at that. He had always been the man who saw the second move; it was what he sold. A man in a tie, in a hospital, in a cupboard at a newspaper, with a pad. A man who wrote on pages. A man who had had the whole of the thing in nine books, in a steel box in a garage, for four years, and had done *nothing with it*. That was the part that would not close. Four years in which any other man in the City would have sold the position, shorted the twelve, sold the books to the highest bidder, and he had not even tried. He had written it down. For nobody. And when it was taken from him, the thing he had done was recite it to a camera.

It did not add. Crane had never in his life lost to a man who did not want the thing. He did not know how to price it.

He had read the books, all nine, in a day, in this room. He remembered them well: the dates, the codes, the small, tidy handwriting. Whole pages he had skipped, because they were about a child. He thought, briefly, of those pages, and then it passed.

'Mr Crane. This is a warrant to search these premises, and anything on them, in connection with the dates of certain trades in the sterling market in the fortnight before the sinking of a tanker in the Strait of Hormuz. It also covers property that we have reason to believe was taken from Mr Jonas Whitlock and Ms Priya Raman on the night of Friday last.' She said it in one breath, like a woman who had said it in the car. 'I would like to see the cabinet behind you.'

'Which cabinet?'

'Steel. Grey. Four-drawer, I'm told. With a key on your person.'

He considered it, as he considered most things. He had not told anyone about the key. Not his head trader, not his lawyers. Kessler had known; Kessler had stood in this room with a rucksack and seen him take the key from its chain. She had been told on Tuesday afternoon at 3.46 that her firm was finished. He turned it over and could not find the fault in it. It was, he thought, a fair return on the investment.

'It is a courtesy,' Crane said, and took the key from its chain, and put it on the desk, and did not stand.

The woman in the raincoat opened it herself. She wore gloves. The first drawer: cigars, a box of them, which she lifted out, looked at, and passed to a constable without comment. The second: nine black A5 notebooks with elastic bands, standing on their spines, in order, eight of them worn grey at the corners and the ninth thinner than the rest. She did not touch them for a moment. Beside them, a girl's rucksack with a hand-sewn strap, a purse, a bail notice, a red notebook. A cardboard folder with the stamp of HM Treasury on its first page, and under it six pages of Walbrook letterhead, headed *Collateral re-use in cleared markets*. A single sheet of Treasury paper with two paragraphs and a space at the foot for signatures, and no signature. A single sheet in a plastic sleeve, folded in four, with twelve four-letter codes down the left and a column of figures down the right.

'That is a copy,' Crane said.

'It's the original, sir. I understand the author has rebuilt it.' She said it without emphasis. 'And we shall want everything in it, and your phone, and your diary, and I'd be grateful if you could tell me where your laptop is before my colleagues start looking for it.'

He gave her the laptop. He gave her, he found, the name of the cleaner. His hands were quite steady. It occurred to him that this was the first morning in twenty-six years that he had looked at a woman in a doorway and not known her price.

The constable with the long bag was labelling the first notebook. Crane watched the pen. Whitlock's hand, he thought, was neater than the constable's. 'Is it permitted,' he said, 'to ask something?'

'You can ask.'

'Sir Anthony Pell. I couldn't reach him.'

'No.' She lifted the red notebook, looked at the cover, and put it in a bag. 'He came in to us at ten, sir, with his solicitor. He's been very helpful about the telephone calls.'

Crane nodded. It seemed to him a reasonable answer. It also seemed to him that the sentence had been placed in the room in advance, for him, on the carpet, a little to the left of the desk, and that she had waited all morning for the moment to put it down.

'I see,' he said. He looked at the nine books being counted into bags, one, two, three, and found that of all of it, of the twenty-six years, the fund, the carpet, the name, the only thing in the room he could not have said the price of was a row of notebooks with an elastic band round each, and the page in the seventh that he had not read.
