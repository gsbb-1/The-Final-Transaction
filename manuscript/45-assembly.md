# Chapter 45: Assembly

Still #1421. Two sittings. The cell I wrote in the back of a car at 8.25 this morning, on the reverse of my bail form, with my own pen, which they gave back with my laces; I knew an hour like that would go if I did not hold it. The rest I am writing tonight. I have left the join where it falls.

***

*The cell.*

They took the belt, the laces, the pen, the phone, and the page out of my coat lining. A sergeant at a high desk listed each in a ledger and read it back to me. The cheap phone was ringing while he listed it. He held it up so I could see the number, and I said I would take it later, and he wrote that down too.

Cell 4. Nine feet by six. A bench with a blue plastic mattress, a steel lavatory with no seat, a light behind a grille that does not switch off. A hatch in the door at the height of a face. I counted the tiles on the back wall, six across and fourteen up, eighty-four, and then the ones I had missed round the door, and then I did the order.

I did it under my breath, as a man says a rosary. Twelve codes, and after each, what it holds and how fast it goes. Nine books in a steel cabinet in Mayfair, and I said them from a bench in a police station in the way I once said them from a desk, and I found the third was thin. Two lines I had given Priya without a size. I said them again.

I had no watch. I thought it was twenty to eight. It was 7.52; I found that out afterwards, and was twelve minutes slow. Somewhere above me a door opened and closed, a radio said a number, and a voice that was not for me said, *the stream, on the stream*, and nobody said anything after.

What I could not do was know. The exchanges would open late if the Bank asked them. The Bank would ask if there was a signature. I had told a woman in a building I could not see to take out a page, and the whole of what I knew of the result was a light that did not switch off.

At 7.55 the hatch opened and a woman in a grey coat gave my name and then her own, and said she was the paper's solicitor, and that I would want to sit down. I thought: she is going to tell me they have charged me. She said, 'The Treasury has written to the commander. It arrived at 7.31. It withdraws their concern about you.' She looked at me. 'I should say that doesn't mean anything. A letter from a department doesn't tell the police what to do.'

'No.'

'It stops them being asked.'

***

The custody inspector read me the conditions himself, in the room with the high desk, from a sheet he held at arm's length. I was released on bail, to return in nine days. Reside at an address I would give. No contact with employees of Walbrook except through the Bank. And a new one, typed, on a line of its own. *To make himself available to assist the Bank of England, at its request, until further notice.*

'I have not seen that one before,' he said, mildly. 'The Governor's office rang the commander at 7.40, which is not how I'd have thought the day would go. I'm told the paper's printed the envelope, front and back. It stays with us. So does the rest of the evidence.' He gave me the form. 'Sign here, Mr Whitlock. This is the first time anyone in this building has asked me to bail a man to the Bank of England.'

I signed. They gave me my laces and my belt and my pen and the phone, which showed fourteen missed calls, nine of them one number, and the page from my coat, flat, in a clear bag. 8.14. It was light on the street. There was a black car at the kerb with its engine running, and Calloway on the pavement with a coat over one arm.

'They won't have me in the room,' he said. 'I'm to wait outside. I've been told it twice.' He held the coat out. It was mine. 'She watched all of it. She says the collar was wrong. She said to tell you the first sentence was slow enough.'

***

*The car.*

Nasser, who does not put a hand out first, put one out. He said nothing for the length of Southwark Street. Then, looking at the road, 'My principal watched all eleven minutes standing. I have not seen him do that.' A pause. 'The money is in the three banks. All of it, since 6.51. His father's two accounts went first.'

'Is he coming?'

'He is at the hotel. He would prefer you did not thank him.' Nasser's face did not move. 'The driver has seen a grey car since the bridge.'

I looked. A bus, a van, a taxi. Nothing grey. At Trafalgar Square the driver took two turns he did not need and the road behind us was clean. That is not the same as gone.

I am writing this against the seat, and my handwriting does not look like mine.

***

*The room.*

I write this part tonight.

St James's, a street I know and a door I do not. A lift, a corridor, a second door with a keypad, and a room that has been told not to look like what it is. Forty desks and nine of them occupied. Two walls of screens, all dark but one; on the one, a clock that someone had made on a computer, in white on black. 10:00, and below it, 12:00, and below that, a line of text I could not read from the door.

It was 8.36. The Governor's deputy was on a wall-sized screen from an office I could see a bookshelf of, a woman in her fifties in a blue jacket. The Bank, by video. She did not introduce herself and nobody asked her to. The fund's woman who runs the sterling book was at the end of the central desk, with three screens and a pen, and her legal man at her elbow with a laptop. Nasser took the chair at the head, and I was put at the middle of the desk, and a speaker the size of a saucer was pushed across to me with a light on it.

'Mr Whitlock,' it said. 'You're late. You've got a face like a bruise.'

'I've been in a cell.'

'I've been in a bed. We both look like it.' A chair squeaked in a room that smelt, down the line, of disinfectant. Behind her I could hear a trolley and a person saying *Raman, Raman* to somebody. 'I've got Graham's sixteen files open on a hospital table, and a nurse trying to take my temperature, and I'd like to start.'

The Deputy Governor gave the facts first. The indemnity had arrived at 7.09. The exchanges had agreed to open at ten. Walbrook had been told noon, and had complied: 'Mr Haldane has described it as a decision of his board. The board has not been told.' The Bank would lend through the three banks against each purchase at a haircut set line by line, on the sheet in front of us. <!-- VERIFY: "haircut" here is the margin the Bank retains below market value when lending against collateral; whether the Bank would stop lending if a line fell past its haircut is the author's dramatic device --> If a line fell farther in a morning than its haircut, the Bank would stop lending against it, and the fund would hold it. 'I want that said aloud in this room,' she said, 'so that nobody tells me afterwards that nobody said it.'

The sterling woman said, 'We have the page in the machine. We've had it since Sunday. We have the twelve, and the sizes to the end of September, and a limit price for every line that I've been guessing at for three days. What I do not have is what comes next.'

'Which line, and how fast,' Nasser said.

'Which, how fast, and when to stop.' She turned the screen so that I could see it. 'Go.'

So we built it, a line at a time.

She read the first. A member already gone, whose collateral Walbrook was still selling off into the long end by noon. <!-- VERIFY: that a defaulted member's remaining collateral would still be sold down after the auction; plausible, unconfirmed --> 'That is the first buy,' I said. 'It has to go regardless. It is the only selling we are sure of.' She put a number in. Priya said from the saucer, 'Eleven point four of the thirty-year, you said on Sunday,' and I said yes, and she said, 'No. Eleven flat. Graham's Q2 file restates it on page sixty-one. He initialled it.' I said yes. The sterling woman crossed the number out and wrote a smaller one, and I thought: she has checked me for four years, and I have never once been grateful for it in the room.

The third line. The one the Bank had lent to at half past twelve yesterday. I said: you will not buy it in the first hour. It will want to be bought, because everyone will see it, and if you move first you show them the order. You wait for its bonds to come back, which at three yesterday they did, and its cash to go, and buy it at the second wave. I said that I was not certain. I said: *I think*. She looked at me for a moment and wrote it down as I said it, *I think*, in the margin, and I understood that she was going to treat it as a figure.

The fourth, the fifth. At the sixth she asked how I knew. I said what that member did on the mornings its bonds failed was in a book I did not have. 'Then how do you have it?' I said I had it as what I remembered of the book, and she said that wasn't an answer, and I said it was the only one.

Nasser spoke then, in the voice for the formal question. 'Mr Whitlock. If you were not in this room at ten, who else could do this?'

'No one.'

Nobody moved.

'The page has the order. Priya has the codes and the order, and she's better than I am on the sizes. Graham has the history. Nobody has the pace, except me. Nobody has all three. I have it from nine books I haven't seen for a week, so some of it is my memory of the books, and some of it is my memory of the memory. I'm not saying that to make you comfortable.'

'We should like you not to be late,' the Deputy Governor said, to the screen, and I could not tell if it was a joke.

The legal man slid a sheet across the desk. One page. For the purposes of the morning I was the fund's adviser, in the terms of the managing director's instruction, for a fee of nil. 'A pound,' I said. 'If it's nil it isn't a contract, it's a favour, and I've had enough of those.' He wrote *£1* in the margin and initialled it, and I signed. It was the first time anyone had asked me to put my name to a page when I knew exactly what it was for.

We went on. I had given six lines and three had been changed, and I thought that if I could hold the next six I might have time, afterwards, to be sick.

At 8.38 the saucer made a small noise, like a woman putting down a cup.

'Hold on,' Priya said. 'Don't go on. Jonas, how many members did you flag from Graham's concentration appendix?'

'Twelve. That's the page.'

A sheet of paper turned over, close to the microphone. 'There's a line in Q2 I haven't seen you use. It isn't on your page. It's not big.' A pause. 'It's getting bigger. I need ninety seconds, and I need nobody to say anything.'
