# Chapter 6: Contained

#1402

Writing this in sittings. The morning first, and then the record. The rest of the day can wait until I have these down in order, because the rest of the day is why I went to Lewisham on the way home instead of coming straight back, and I want the numbers on the page before I write about the people.

Woke at 4.40. Not the alarm. The phone, face down on the bedside table, lighting the ceiling. Three messages from the night desk, one from Priya. Priya's said: *Have you seen.*

I had not seen. I lay there and read for eleven minutes before I got up.

A tanker. Hormuz. Sunk, not damaged. Oil up 14% in Asia and still going. Every channel with a different idea of who had done it and the two biggest American ones agreeing with each other, which is never good. Our own government, as of 4.51, "monitoring".

Laura asleep. Elsie asleep, for once. I dressed in the dark and did not shave. Left at 5.15.

***

Walbrook at 5.50. Not empty. The risk room was already at Monday strength and it was Friday, and the wall screens were all red, which I have seen before, and all red in the same direction, which I have seen twice.

Graham was in. Graham is never in before seven. He nodded at me across the room and did not come over, which he had also done all day Thursday, and I filed it under Thursday and went to my desk.

What we do at seven is this, for Elsie if she ever reads these and for no one else: every firm that clears through us has promised things to other firms, and we stand in the middle and guarantee the promises. To make sure we can, we hold a cushion from each of them, in cash or in bonds, and when prices move against a firm we take the day's loss from it in cash, that morning. The engine runs overnight and finishes before seven. At seven the calls go out, not by telephone but as an instruction to each member's payment bank, which has the money ready by eight and must confirm it to us by nine. Nine is the line. A member whose bank has not confirmed by nine is in default, and I have never seen it. Through the day the engine runs again every hour, and if a move has pushed a member over its threshold it calls again, whenever that is; there is no timetable for the afternoon, only the market. Normally the morning is a billion or so, spread across forty members, and nobody notices.

This morning the number on my screen at 6.52 was £9.6bn.

I counted the zeros. Then I counted the members above a billion. Four. One of them above two.

The reason is simple and I have written it a hundred times. Oil up means inflation up means interest rates up means the price of a government bond down. British government bonds fell further than most this morning because the pound fell with them and because we buy more of our energy than most, and whichever of those reasons is right the result on my screen was the same. Bonds down means every position backed by bonds is worth less and every cushion made of bonds is thinner. So we call for more, from everybody, at once.

That is the morning. The afternoon is what happens when they have to find it.

At 7.00 the calls went out. At 7.04 the first bank confirmed. I stood behind Priya's chair and we watched them come in, line by line, grey to green, like a register.

By 7.40, 31 of 40.

By 8.15, 37.

Three outstanding. Two of them the ones I would have guessed. One of them not. Priya said the name of the one that was not and I said nothing, and she looked at me the way she has been looking at me since Thursday.

At 8.52 the last one confirmed. Eight minutes inside the deadline.

Met. All of it. Everyone could say so and by nine everyone was.

I went and stood at the window for a minute with my coffee, which was cold, and I thought: the system is sound. That is what sound looks like. Forty firms and every one of them found the money. If you had asked me at nine o'clock this morning whether the system was sound I would have had to say yes, and it would have been true, and it would have been the truest thing I will ever say that is also a lie.

Because I had watched where the money came from. The call is paid in cash. Always cash; nobody pays a loss in bonds. So the question is never what they paid with. It is where the cash came from, and how late. Three of the last five confirmed after 8.40, with a number they had had since seven. Nobody sits on a billion for an hour and a half. The cash was not there at seven. It was raised this morning, and on a morning when gilts are falling there is one place a firm raises a billion before breakfast: it lends out bonds for cash. Repo. The bond goes out of the door, the cash comes in and pays us, and the bond is somebody else's until it comes home.

And on our own fails list, at 8.31, one line changed. The pension fund's £42m of the 2045, failed since Tuesday, due back from repo at two on Thursday and then at half past two today, was not sent again. It was cancelled. You do not cancel a lodgement you are about to make. You cancel one when the bond has gone somewhere else. At 8.52 that member's bank confirmed, in cash. I cannot see the repo market. I do not need to. The same gilt I had watched not arrive all Thursday had come home this morning and gone straight out again, for the cash to pay the call on the promise it was meant to be standing behind. I think. I cannot prove it. I have written that sentence a hundred times too.

Priya's check. The timing error.

***

The Chancellor was on the wall screens at 11.20. Sound off, captions on, the way we have it. Caroline Strand in a studio with a window behind her showing a river that was not the Thames, so the interview had been recorded somewhere else first and this was a repeat. Dark jacket. No notes. I have never seen her hold notes.

The captions said, in order:

*The financial system is sound.*

*We have been in close contact with the Bank and the regulators since the early hours.*

*Margin calls this morning were met in full by every clearing member.*

*The situation is contained.*

Then something about the election, which is in five weeks, and the interviewer trying to get her to say the word *crisis* and failing, four times. I counted.

*Met in full by every clearing member.* That was us. That sentence was our morning, on television, with the Chancellor's face in front of it, three hours after the last payment cleared. Someone had rung someone by nine. I looked for Graham and he was in his glass box with the door shut and his back to the room.

Richard Haldane came down at noon, which he does perhaps twice a year, and walked the floor shaking hands. He shook mine. He said, 'Good morning, Jonas, well held,' as if I had been in goal. He did not stop. I have thought about that handshake several times today and I will come back to it.

***

I left at 6.10 and went to Lewisham.

The lock-up is a row of nine garages behind the railway arch, and my father's is the sixth. The rent still comes out of my account on the 3rd and I have never changed the name on the agreement. Laura thinks I go there to sit with his things, which is true about one visit in ten.

The current notebook comes home with me every night. The other eight live here, in a steel toolbox on the shelf my father put up, under a strip light that takes two tries. I opened it tonight for the first time since the spring.

I did not need to look for the entry. I know the number. I have known it for three years. I took down the second notebook and found #212 and I sat on the upturned crate and copied it out, word for word, into tonight's entry, because I want it in front of me where I write, and I will not bring the old books into the house. This is what I wrote, 1,190 days ago, when I was 36 and the record was still a hobby:

> #212
>
> Second time. Two members, same morning, both failed a lodgement in the same line. Both told me timing. Both used the word "timing" without being asked. Both covered the hole in cash at nine and both swapped bonds back in at ten past three, within four minutes of each other, which is when the repo market gives the bonds back.
>
> Not fraud. Nobody is lying. Each of them has a piece of paper that says the bond is theirs tonight, and each piece of paper is honest. I think it is one bond, going round. I cannot prove that. Nobody can; one gilt is the same as the next and we only count what has arrived.
>
> It does not matter on a normal day. On a normal day nobody asks for the bond, they ask for its value, and its value can be in two places at once. It matters on the day everyone asks at once. Then the bond is in one place and the other promise is backed by air.
>
> I don't know how many times it has happened. I know it was two today. If it is two it is more than two.
>
> The sign to watch for is not a failure. A failure is too late. The sign is a call that is met, but only just, with cash that was not there at seven, and bonds that arrive in the afternoon and are gone again by the next morning, and the bonds are the ones you have seen before. The system will look soundest on the morning it has already broken. Everyone will have paid. Nobody will have the bond.
>
> Write this down every time. Nobody else is counting.

That is #212. I have not changed a word.

I put the book back in the box, and the box on the shelf, and the strip light off, and I stood in the dark of the garage for a bit with the trains going over.

Three of the last five paid with cash that was not there at seven. One lodgement cancelled at 8.31, for a bond I had watched not arrive all Thursday. The call met with eight minutes to spare.

I wrote the morning down three years and three months ago, before I had seen it. I got one thing wrong. I thought I would feel vindicated.

Then I came home. Laura and Elsie were out, and I sat down at the kitchen table with this before I had taken my coat off, because I wanted #212 copied in while I could still see the strip light. The rest of today is the people, and what happened between the handshake at noon and the lock-up at six, and I will need another sitting for it, because at four o'clock this afternoon Graham Pryce asked me for my personal notes.
