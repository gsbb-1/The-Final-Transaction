# Chapter 19: Cousins

The statement had been read on the evening news on Monday and printed in both the morning papers on Tuesday, and by Wednesday Khalid Al-Mansouri had heard it quoted back to him so often that he could have recited it in the voice of the man who read it. The nation's wealth had been protected by the prudent and timely action of its fund. The fund's leadership had acted with foresight. The nation did not seek confrontation with any party and would not be made the hostage of any party's politics. That was the whole of it, and it was true in every word, and he had not been consulted on a single one.

He read it once more, at the long table in his own house, with the sea beyond the glass, and then he turned it over and put it under the thing that was not public.

The thing that was not public was a sheet from Nasser summarising the week. Positions closed through the three London banks: a little over half, in size order, as instructed. Collateral released and sold: a little under half, because the clearing house released it only the morning after each position closed and the market, knowing this, waited for him at every open. The price he had got for the bonds already sold. The price he would get for the rest, if Thursday looked like Wednesday. The difference between those figures and the figure on Friday night was not a number he was prepared to say aloud, and Nasser, who knew him, had not put it in words either. He had put it in a column and let it speak. <!-- VERIFY: close-outs through clearing members settle same day and excess collateral is released the next morning (research/swaps-and-clearing.md); "a little over half" in two and a half trading days is a working assumption -->

That was the private question. Nobody had asked it in those words. They had asked it in others.

One of the four men had telephoned at seven that morning, which was early for him, and had spent some minutes on the health of Khalid's mother before arriving at it. Could the fund not have waited? A week. A fortnight. Until the Americans had said the word "freeze" in a sentence with a verb in it, rather than merely in a studio. Khalid had said, with respect, that a fortnight was the difference between selling and being sold, and the man had said that he understood, which meant that he did not, and that others understood less. Then he had said, in the tone of a man mentioning the weather, that Khalid's cousin had spoken very well at the council the night before.

'Which cousin?'

'Faisal.' A pause. 'He said that the fund had shown the way. That the nation's money should never again sit where a foreign minister could reach it. He said it rather better than I have. He had figures.'

'Faisal has always had figures.'

'He has more of them this week,' the man said, and rang off.

***

Faisal was the son of his father's younger brother, four years his junior, and had spent his twenties as Khalid had spent his, in London and in banks, with the difference that Faisal had enjoyed it. He had come home with a taste for the things that London sold to men like him and a facility for explaining them to men who had not been. He ran the family's private money, which was a phrase that covered a great deal: the interests of one branch of a large family, held through an office with a name that was not the family's, trading through banks that were not the nation's, in instruments that the fund, with its charter and its board and its auditors, could not have touched. A family office answered to a family. Khalid's fund answered to a state. The two were cousins in the same sense that he and Faisal were: by blood, in public, at weddings.

He had never disliked Faisal. He had found him expensive. Until this week that had been a sufficient thing to find.

Faisal rang at eleven, as Khalid had known he would, because Faisal was a man who could not hear that his name had been mentioned without wanting to be in the room where it was said.

'Khalid. I wanted to be the first to congratulate you, and I find I am the twentieth.'

'The first was the newsreader.'

'Then I am in good company.' Faisal laughed; he had a good laugh, which he knew. 'I meant what I said last night. You will have heard. You did the thing everybody has been saying should be done for ten years and nobody had the courage for. The money is coming home.'

'The money is being sold,' Khalid said. 'At a price. Home is where it will sit afterwards, and it will be smaller when it gets there.'

'A price worth paying.'

'You would say so. You were not paying it.'

A very small pause, in which Khalid heard his cousin decide to be amused.

'I did my modest best to be on the right side of it,' Faisal said. 'One does, when one can see it coming. You taught me that, you know. In London. You said a man who could read a balance sheet had no excuse for being surprised.'

'I said that of banks.'

'It applies to countries.' A sound of movement; Faisal walking, as he always did on the telephone, through some large room. 'Come to dinner on Friday. There are people I would like you to meet. People who think as you do, now. The council is going to want to talk about where the money sits from here, and it would be strange if the man who moved it were not at the table.'

Khalid said that he would see, which Faisal would understand correctly. Then he put the telephone down and sat for a moment with his hand still on it, as he had on Saturday after another call, and considered the sentence *one does, when one can see it coming.*

He had spent Saturday telling himself that his government had not sunk the tanker. He had asked, through the channels one asked, and been told no, and the men who told him had been frightened, and he had believed them. He still did. The four men who could have given the order had not given it.

He had not, until this moment, asked himself who else there was.

***

Nasser came at half past twelve with the midday figures and found him standing at the glass.

'Leave those,' Khalid said. 'I want the desk to do something for me, and I want it done by this evening and spoken of to nobody. Not the board. Not London.'

'Sir.'

'The week before the ship went down. Ten days, say. I want to know who in this market was positioned against us. Against the country. Our banks' shares. The sovereign, if anyone was buying protection on it. The currency, through the forwards, if anyone was foolish enough. The airline. The regional index. Anyone who sold what was ours before there was a reason to sell it.' <!-- VERIFY: the plausible instruments for betting against a Gulf state before a crisis: short local bank equities, buying sovereign CDS protection, forward positions against a pegged currency; and whether a dominant local institution could learn the names of participants from local brokers and banks. Treated here as gossip the fund can obtain because of its size, not as a formal data right -->

Nasser did not write any of it down, which was how Khalid knew he had understood it.

'Our brokers will know some of it,' Nasser said carefully. 'The banks here will know more than they should and will tell us a little of it, because we are who we are. The exchange will know what was sold. It will not be a complete picture.'

'I do not want a picture. I want names.'

'There is one name you will see, sir.'

Khalid turned from the glass. 'Say it.'

'The family office was selling banks from the Tuesday of the week before,' Nasser said. 'Everyone on the exchange knows it. They have been too polite to say so this week, since the fund did the same thing on a larger scale three days later and was praised for it.'

'Faisal is the family office. I know what Faisal did; he has just spent ten minutes telling me in a way that will not repeat. I am not asking what he sold. I am asking who bought it. Who was on the other side. A man does not sell a country short into an empty room, Nasser. Somebody takes the other side of every one of those trades and somebody writes the swap when he wants a bigger position than the exchange will give him. I want the counterparties.'

'That is harder.'

'It is harder,' Khalid agreed. 'Do it anyway.'

***

The afternoon was long. He took two more calls from men whose names he would not have written down, and a third from the London office, where the day had been as the day before, only lower.

He ate alone. He read the British papers on the screen, which had the word "theft" in every headline and, under it, a man with no name: a senior risk manager at the clearing house that held his collateral, who had apparently written down, over four years, what the week had proved, and whom the police now wished to speak to for having done so. The papers did not say what he had written. They said what he had taken. Khalid thought that he would like to read it, and that he was probably the only person in two countries who wanted to read it for its own sake rather than to make it disappear.

Nasser came back at nine.

He had a single sheet. He did not sit down to give it, which was unlike him, and he did not begin with the summary, which was more unlike him still.

'The family office sold the three largest banks here from the Tuesday before the ship, sir, through two brokers, in size. It bought protection on the sovereign through a London bank, also from the Tuesday, in a quantity the London bank will not confirm and has not denied. It sold the currency forward through the same bank. It bought oil, which anyone might do. And it did something I had not expected, which is that for the largest of the positions it did not go through the exchange or a bank at all. It went through a swap, written privately, with a single counterparty. One contract, very large, dated nine days before the ship went down.' <!-- VERIFY: a hedge fund writing a bespoke total-return or equity swap directly to a family office, rather than through a dealer, is unusual; it is used here as the thread Al-Mansouri's desk can pull, and marks the trade as deliberate. Nine days matches the date Priya finds in ch. 21 -->

'And the counterparty.'

'I had it from one source and did not believe it, and then from a second.' Nasser put the sheet on the table. 'The other side of your cousin's largest trade, and of a good part of the rest, is a fund in London. Greywether Capital.'

Khalid did not touch the sheet.

He thought of a voice on Saturday, unhurried and warm, saying *you're going to sell, everyone with a television knows you're going to sell*; and offering a price that was a forecast; and knowing, before he dialled, the position and the collateral and the morning's conversations in two capitals. More than a man in Mayfair ought to know. He had filed that away in the place where he kept things about Sebastian Crane, and he found now that the place was not large enough.

A hedge fund did not take the other side of a family office for charity. Either it had been hedged elsewhere, in a market big enough to hide in, or it had been paid in something other than money.

Faisal had been paid in money. Faisal had been paid very well. And Faisal, who had never in his life kept a thing to himself that would make him seem clever, had just invited him to dinner with people who thought as he did, now.

'Nasser,' he said. 'Who else knows you asked?'

'Two brokers and a man at the London bank. They think it was about the fund's own exposure.'

'Let them go on thinking it.' He picked up the sheet at last and read the name again, as if it might have changed. 'And find out for me what Greywether Capital was doing in London in the same nine days. Not against us. Against them.'
