# Chapter 48: Cannon Street

Still #1421, written at night, and the shirt is in a bin bag by the door because Laura says it is evidence and I say it is a shirt.

The car did the Strand and Fleet Street in nineteen minutes and stopped at Cannon Street at 9.24, behind a bus, in front of a bus, with a taxi alongside. The driver said, to the windscreen, that it was the delayed open: every dealing floor in the City had told its people to come in early and sit, and the roads had done what roads do. He said he could try Queen Victoria Street. I looked at the clock on the dashboard. 9.24. Walbrook's door was 600 yards ahead and a little to the left, past the station and up a lane. I have walked it two thousand times.

I said I would get out. He said Mr Nasser had said I was not to. I said Mr Nasser was not in the car.

I saw them when I stood on the pavement. One at the corner by the bank on the far side of the road, in a good dark overcoat, with a telephone held a little away from his ear so that his hand was in view. One at the mouth of the lane, hands in pockets, looking at nothing, at me. I do not know them. I have not seen their faces. But I spent four years looking for people who were not looking at me and I know what a man does with his shoulders when he is not looking at a man he is looking at. They had been there a while. Neither of them had a newspaper or a coffee.

The one at the lane was between me and the door. So I did not go up the lane. I went in at the station.

***

Cannon Street station on a weekday morning is a hall with a glass roof and a platform barrier every ten yards, and at 9.30 it is the tail end of the rush, which is a thing like a tide that has not quite gone out. Perhaps three hundred people. Suits, rucksacks, a man with a cello. The departures board said every train was on time and nobody believed it. I went down the middle of it with my head down.

I am telling it in order. I do not remember it in order.

I remember the second man coming in at the side doors behind me, fast. I remember turning, and a hand in my collar, and a pillar against my cheek. I remember the noise of it, a wet clap, and thinking *the page*, and putting my left forearm across my chest where it was, and the pain in the ribs that had not been worth mentioning for four days lighting up like a grid. A voice in my ear that I recognised only as a sound: patient, even, polite. 'Mr Whitlock. We would like you to come and talk to someone. There's a car.'

'No.'

'It's not a question.'

The shirt was warm down the front. I saw it before I felt it. The nose, I think; the cheek. Blood on a white shirt in a station looks like a decision somebody has made on your behalf.

He had me by the collar and the belt. He was strong. He had the quiet competence of someone who has done this with people who weren't going to ring anybody. And then a woman near the barrier, a woman in a navy coat with a lanyard and an apple, said in a very clear voice, 'Oi.' Not shouted. Said, in the voice you use to a dog on a train. A man in front of her turned. A man behind turned. The man with the cello moved it two feet to the left, which is the kind of detail I will have forever. And a girl of about twenty-two, with a phone in her hand, raised it. I saw the small lens. I saw it find us.

I have thought about that second more than any in the day.

He looked at the phone. He looked at the woman, and the people who had begun to make a space around us, the shape a crowd makes when it has decided it would like to see how something comes out. His hand did not let go. But I felt it make a calculation. A man of forty in a bloodied shirt, no bag, no weapon, a bad ribcage; and three hundred witnesses; and a telephone. I was worth a great deal to the man who had sent him. I was not worth killing in front of the 9.31 to Dartford.

The hand opened. He said, close, not unkindly, 'This isn't over,' and was gone through the crowd as though he had been part of it all along.

***

I did not stop. I think that is the only thing I did right.

I went out of the side doors onto the lane, and the first man was not at the mouth of it; he had gone round to the station front. I took a left, then a right, into Abchurch Lane, which has a church and a pillar box and was empty, and I put the heel of my hand under my nose and ran the way a man of forty with four days of bad ribs runs, which is not fast. Behind me a door banged. I did not look.

Nicholas Lane. A courier on a bicycle who swore and did not stop. Lombard Street, where for the first time in an hour I was one of a great many men in suits walking quickly, and I let it take me. At Bank the junction opened out: eight roads, a thousand people. I crossed with the lights against me and a taxi horn and found that I was laughing, and that it was not a good sound.

9.47. Walbrook's door. The lobby I have walked through at 7.40 every weekday for five years, and the man on the desk. He looked at the shirt. He looked at the face. He did not say a word. He pressed the button that opens the barrier from his side, and I want that written down.

The Bank's man was at the lift with a letter in his hand. I took it. I do not remember reading it.

The risk room. Wall screens from corner to corner, the room I built the desks for. Faces turned and nobody said anything.

On the centre screen, in the corner, in the colour I know best, a line was moving. The price of insurance on a bank that was not one of mine. It had not moved in a day except by a hair. It had started to move at 9.46, fourteen minutes before anyone was allowed to trade.

***

The grey car had been at the end of the road since ten past nine. It stood on the far side, nose out, under a plane tree, a hundred and fifty yards from the front window of Tom and Aisha's. Two shapes in it. Not moving. She stood in the front room with the curtain held back an inch. It was the same car. Same colour, same make, same absence of anything that made it a car you remembered. She had counted it for eight days at the end of a lane in Norfolk before she knew it was something, and on the Saturday it had been behind her all the way to this road. She knew now.

She had called 999 at 9.14 and been told that a vehicle would be allocated. She said there were men in the car and her daughter was in the house, and the woman said *I understand, madam*, and typed, and said a car would attend as soon as one was available, and gave her a reference number, which Laura wrote on her hand. It was the second time that week she had asked the state for help and been given a number.

At 9.19 the two shapes got out.

She had never seen them. She would have known them anywhere. The older man was broad and had a left-sided stoop, an old shoulder or a bad hip; he put his weight on the door to rise. The younger was thin and held his hands out of his pockets, and was looking at the houses on her side for a number. Both of them walked as though the road were theirs.

She took the back off her phone case. The card was where she had put it, white, plain, a mobile number and nothing else. She dialled.

It rang three times, and then it was answered.

'Yes.'

'It's Laura Whitlock.' Her own voice was perfectly level, which interested her. 'There is a grey car on Aisha's road with two men in it. They are walking towards the house. I think you know whose they are.'

A very short pause. 'Is the child inside?'

'Upstairs with Aisha. The door is locked.'

'Stay in the hall with the door shut. Do not open it until you have seen me through the glass.' Another pause, shorter. 'Eleven minutes. Do not telephone anyone else.'

She stood behind the door with her back to the wall, her own pulse at about ninety-four. The bell went at 9.29. It went again, and then a hand, flat, on the wood, close to her head. She did not breathe. And then, through the frosted glass, a third shape, from the left, at the pace of a woman who has not hurried in years.

'Good morning,' said Dana Kessler.

Kessler wore a grey coat, carried no bag, and stood at the foot of the step with her hands in her pockets. She spoke to the older man the way a registrar speaks to a consultant who has done something stupid.

'You rang me on Saturday from a car,' Kessler said, 'and told me what you had done on Friday at a quarter to eleven. Stairs, fifth floor, a girl. I wrote it down. The time you rang, the time you said it happened, what you said Mr Crane had paid you and what he had paid you out of. It is typed. It is with a solicitor who has been told to open it if I do not collect it by noon.'

'Dana—' said the older man.

'Let me finish. You will walk back to the car. You will not telephone Mr Crane from it, because his number is on a list that is not mine and is going to be read. You will drive until you are out of this postcode. If you go on from here, the envelope goes today. To the police officer who has Miss Raman's wrist on a form. To the editor of the paper whose stairs they were. To the regulator, with the trade dates. And to your wives. I would rather not. I would send them.' She took her right hand from her pocket and it was empty and she showed it. 'This is not a threat. I am telling you what will happen.'

The older man was looking at her. Laura, through the glass, watched the muscle at the corner of his jaw, and the way his left hand went to the shoulder and came away. She had seen that look on men who had come in about a chest pain and had been told what the scan showed.

The younger one said something Laura did not hear.

'Yes,' said Kessler. 'That is what I mean. There isn't a third thing.'

Then the older man turned, and the younger one, a beat after him, and they walked down the road with a sort of care, as though the pavement might break, and got into the grey car. It did not start immediately. When it did, it pulled out under the plane tree and went the other way, at thirty miles an hour, indicating, and was gone round the end of the road.

Laura unlocked the door. The chain she took off with a hand that was not quite steady, and noted it, as one notes it in anyone.

Kessler was still on the step. She looked, close to, exactly as she had looked in the kitchen in Norfolk, which was to say very ordinary. She said, 'I put the SIM back in at six. I thought someone might ring.'

'Your men broke a girl's wrist.'

'Yes.'

'Her head needed stitches.'

'I know,' said Kessler. 'I have been told.' It was the first time Laura had heard anything in her voice that was not exact. 'I'm not asking you for anything.'

In her hand a telephone buzzed. She turned it over. Laura saw the screen before Kessler did, or before she let her see: one line, a number with no name, *Dana. Please.* The word was the whole message.

'He has not said that in two years,' Kessler said. She laid the screen face down on her palm. 'He's frightened. Frightened men pay.'

'It's 9.38,' said Laura.

'Yes.' Kessler sat down on the low front wall, in her grey coat, in full view of the road. 'I'll wait here until ten.'
