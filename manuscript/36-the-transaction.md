# Chapter 36: The transaction

Sunday. First sitting, ten past five, on a pad with four letters at the top of every sheet, in a room in St James's with no window that opens. I said on Wednesday that I would write on whatever I had and not say so again. This is what I have.

I am going to write the morning before I write the thing, because the thing is the point of 1,418 entries and I want to get to it with my hands steady.

***

Nine o'clock. The payphone by the lifts on Priya's ward, with a pound. Margaret answered. Not Laura. Margaret has not answered that telephone in twelve days.

Laura left yesterday at twenty past ten with Elsie and the bag, through the photographers, for London. She is all right. She rang Margaret last night from a telephone that was not hers. I am to keep ringing at nine, every day, because that is how it works now. She said I would know where she had gone and that I was not to go there. She said she would find me. Margaret read the last part off a piece of paper, because Laura had made her write it down, and I stood in a hospital corridor and heard my wife use my own method on me.

I know where. I am not writing it. I am not going there, which is what she meant.

Twelve days of asking her to stay in one place, and she stayed for twelve, and yesterday I rang from a hospital and asked her to say she would not come, and she did not say it, and I heard her not say it, and I let the pips take the line because I had one coin. One week, she said on Monday at the door. It runs out tomorrow. She has come down to collect.

***

Priya's room. Not a ward; a side room off one, with a door, which Nasser's office arranged on Saturday afternoon in a way that was not explained to me and that I did not ask about. Her right wrist in plaster to the elbow. Stitches above the ear, eleven of them; she counted by touch and told me the number because she knew I would want it. A name on the board above the bed that is not hers, which she looks at every so often with the expression of a woman reading a figure she did not produce. They want to keep her until Monday. She wants to leave. They have taken her shoes, which seems to be how hospitals win.

She cannot write. For four years she has checked my work by rewriting it, and today she sat up against three pillows and said numbers and I wrote them down, and I understood for the first time what it is to be the hand and not the head. She is better at it than I am. She gave the figures and then the reason, in that order, as she always has, and twice she said 'No. Again,' and I crossed out and started again.

Graham came at twenty to ten with a briefcase. The briefcase I have watched him carry for five years, and inside it what he brought on Thursday and has not let out of his sight since: ten years of risk committee packs, the ones the committee saw and the ones it did not, kept at home because that is what a man does who signs things and would like, later, to be able to say what he signed. Member codes against names. Collateral balances, by member, by quarter, cash and bonds, the lines and the sizes. The concentration reports with "no material concentration" at the foot of every one, and his initials. He put it on the bed by Priya's feet and stood back from it, as if it might go off.

He looks ten years older than Wednesday week. He did not say "not yet" once all morning. I noticed because I was waiting for it.

Nasser came at ten with two people from the fund's London desk, a woman who runs its sterling book and a man who does its legal, and three laptops, and a ward sister who looked at the room and the laptops and the man in the suit and went away again. Six people and a bed. Priya looked at it and said, 'Well. That's a committee,' and we began.

***

Here is the thing. I am going to write it the way we built it, one piece at a time, so that anyone who finds this page can follow it without having done my job. Priya made me do it that way in the room. 'Say it so the sister could understand it,' she said. 'If you can't, it's not a plan, it's a feeling.'

The problem first. Twelve members of Walbrook are holding bonds that are promised to more people than own them. Every morning the price of those bonds falls, Walbrook calls them for the difference in cash, and they find the cash by selling bonds, which makes the price fall, which brings the next call. One of the twelve failed on the Monday before last. The second is the name on your debit card and it has been paying its calls since Thursday week with money borrowed from the Bank of England, and Graham, who knows the rules, says the auction of the first one's book begins on Tuesday, and that the second one will not survive the morning the market sees what that book fetches. When the second goes, the fund that stands behind every trade Walbrook guarantees is gone, and the Bank starts talking about a word that means Walbrook stops. Everything it stands in the middle of, frozen. Not falling. Frozen.

Now the buyer. The forced selling only stops if someone stands on the other side of it with enough cash and no need to sell. There is nobody in London. Everyone in London is a seller. There is one man in the world who has spent nine working days turning British government bonds into cash and moving the cash somewhere it cannot be frozen, and he is in St James's, and he is the reason the thing started, and he is the only one who can afford to stop it.

The map. The twelve codes, in the order they fail, and the right-hand column: what each one is holding that is promised twice, which lines, roughly how much. The page in my coat has the codes and the order and a column that Priya and I rebuilt from memory on Wednesday and Thursday. Graham's packs gave it names, and sizes to the quarter end, and Priya corrected the sizes forward from what she knows of the last fortnight, which is a great deal. The sterling woman put it into her machine line by line and said, at one point, 'I've been trying to guess this list for a week.' Nobody answered her. That is the map Crane has, in nine books in Mayfair. He can use it to know where to push. It is also, read the other way, a shopping list.

The transaction. On Tuesday morning, when the market opens and the twelve are forced to sell, the fund buys. Not everything; exactly what each of the twelve is forced to sell, in the lines the map says, in the sizes the map says, in the order the map says they fail, through the fund's three clearing banks, which are the same banks it sold through last week. The first name sells and the fund is the bid. The second name sells and the fund is the bid. The market, which has spent two weeks learning that there is no bid, discovers that there is one, and that it does not go away, and that it is standing exactly where the selling comes from. The man who does legal called it "meeting the market". Priya said, 'It's catching the plates.' Both are right.

Why the order. This is the part I had to say three times. If the fund buys in the wrong order, it is buying what nobody is selling yet, and the thing that is being sold still falls, and the fund has spent its cash early and holds bonds that are going down. Worse: the market reads the list from the buying. If the fund stands in the right lines at the right moment it looks like a buyer meeting sellers; if it stands in the wrong ones it looks like a man with a list, and the list becomes public in the first hour, and every fund in London that is short those names sells them harder. The order has to be the order. The order is in my head. Priya has it on paper, from memory, as I gave it to her; Graham's packs confirm the sizes to September. But the fingerprints behind every line, which is what tells you how fast each one goes, are in nine notebooks I do not have and in a man who read them for four years. The sterling woman asked, quite reasonably, who would be in her room on Tuesday morning to say which line was next. Nasser said, 'Mr Whitlock.' Nobody said that Mr Whitlock is wanted by the police. It did not need saying.

The money. Nasser wrote a figure and turned the pad round. I am not writing it. It is more than came out of London in the week, and it is nearly all of what the fund holds that is not bolted to the ground. Priya said the number back to him and then said what it meant: a buyer that size cannot carry what it buys, because by ten o'clock it is simply the next man holding bonds that are falling, with more of them than anyone. It has to be able to turn each purchase back into cash the same morning or it runs out by the fourth name.

The Bank. That is what the Bank is for. The Bank of England lends cash against government bonds every day of its life; it is the thing it does. On Tuesday morning it lends against the bonds the fund buys, as it buys them, through the fund's three banks, so that each purchase becomes cash again inside the hour and the cash buys the next line. <!-- VERIFY: the Bank's standing facilities lend to banks, and since 2025 to insurers, pension schemes and LDI funds under the CNRF; a sovereign fund is not a normal counterparty. Routing through the fund's three clearing banks is the plausible mechanism; whether the Bank would open a facility to a sovereign fund directly is unconfirmed --> The fund never holds more than one name's bonds at a time. The Bank holds them all, against cash, which is a thing the Bank has done since before there was a Walbrook. And if the Bank is the lender, the market knows the buyer does not stop, because the buyer is the Bank's money going round. Strand said to me at her own table that the system is held up by the belief that it is held up. She was right. On Tuesday the belief has to be that there is a bid. There is one. She only has to let the market believe it.

The indemnity. If the bonds keep falling, the Bank is holding them against cash it has lent and the Bank loses, and the Bank's loss is public money, and the Governor said to Strand a week ago, in a room with no note, that he will not do it without her signature on a piece of paper that says the Treasury carries the loss. Graham knows the piece of paper. It is one page. It has been signed before, in 2022, by a Chancellor who was gone within the month, which Graham said and then wished he had not.

The time. Walbrook's calls go to the banks at eight and are due at nine. The exchanges open at eight. The fund needs the selling to come to it, not to come before it. So the Bank asks the exchanges to open late, and asks Walbrook to move its deadline from nine to noon, which the rulebook allows and which Walbrook has never once done, and the room gets three hours in which the twelve can sell to a buyer instead of into nothing. <!-- VERIFY: ICE Futures Europe and the LSE can delay an open on their own authority; the Bank, as the exchanges' supervisor's neighbour rather than its master, would request rather than direct, and the FCA would be involved. Walbrook extending its PPS deadline is within a CCP's rules in principle; the research note says a CCP can "extend a deadline" and that this is almost never done --> Graham said the extension would itself be read as panic. Priya said that a market which has watched a clearing house eat its own default fund for a fortnight is not going to be frightened by a late start.

The squeeze. Anyone who has been short the twelve names since nine days before a ship sank, in their parents' shares and in the bonds under them and in the pound, has been paid every minute since. On Tuesday the thing he is short goes up, hard, in the first hour, in every line at once, and the bonds he has borrowed to sell are the bonds the Bank is now holding, and he has to buy back what he sold into a market with one buyer, which is not him. The sterling woman asked who. Nasser said the fund did not speculate about other participants. Priya said, 'Greywether,' and the room was quiet for a moment, and then the man who does legal wrote it down.

Done right, the forced selling stops in an hour. Not slows. Stops. The second name pays its nine o'clock, or its noon, with the price of its collateral going up instead of down, and the fund that stands behind Walbrook stops being spent, and the twelve are twelve banks with a bad fortnight instead of a list. Done wrong, or late, or in the wrong order, or without the Bank, the fund has brought its client's money back into the one city it spent a week leaving, and bought bonds that fall, and it fails with Walbrook, on the same morning, and a man who has one client does not go home.

Nasser said that last part. He said it in the voice he uses for everything, and then he said, 'My principal has instructed me to say it to you, Mr Whitlock, so that you have heard it before you ask him for it.'

***

It needs one signature. Not the Governor's; the Governor wants it, Graham says, and has wanted it for a week. The one that is missing is the one that goes on the indemnity, and it belongs to a woman who denied on Sunday that she had seen six pages with her department's stamp on them, and who asked me, nine days ago, for mine.

12.40. Nasser rang his principal from the corridor and came back and said two things. The Governor will be in the room. The Chancellor will see Mr Al-Mansouri at eight o'clock tonight, at the Treasury, with whoever he chooses to bring, for thirty minutes, without officials. Then he looked at me and said, 'He chooses to bring you.'

Priya said, 'Shoes,' and the sister would not give them to her, so she is not coming, and she has said what she thinks of that, and the man in the suit from legal has gone a colour.

Graham will not come either. He said he had sat at that table once and that his presence would not help, and he is right, and I think it is also true that he could not. He put his hand on the briefcase on the bed and then took it away and left it there, and said, 'It's hers now. She'll read it properly,' and went.

Horse Guards Road. Third floor. A long table and a view of the park going dark. I have sat at it once, with my own memo put in front of me and a statement with a line for my name, and I asked for the night and was given until six on Monday. Tonight I go back in with a sovereign wealth fund on one side and the Governor of the Bank of England on the other and I ask the Chancellor of the Exchequer for her signature on one page.

I have written 1,417 entries about what happens when someone asks me for mine. I am about to find out what it is like from the other chair.

Second sitting after. If there is anything to write.
