# Chapter 43: Six o'clock

Wednesday, #1421. Written after midnight in one sitting, on the fund's paper, because I want the day in the order it happened and I know what I do to order afterwards. The first part is the part I can write straight. It lasted eleven minutes. I counted.

***

Calloway came back at 4.31 with a shirt in a dry-cleaner's bag. White, a collar size too big, a crease across the chest where it had been folded for a drawer. I put it on in his bathroom with the door open, because Laura was in the passage with the bag on her shoulder and Elsie awake against her neck, and neither of us was going to shut a door.

She did not say good luck. She said, 'Say the first sentence.'

I said it.

'Slower. Look at me when you say it, not at the floor.' I said it again. She nodded, the way she nods at a pulse. Elsie put a hand on my face, flat, the whole palm, and held it there for as long as it takes to count five. Then Calloway said the car was at the end of the road, and they went down the back stairs ahead of me, to the loading bay and a van with the paper's name taken off the side. I do not know where they went. I asked him not to tell me, and he did not. A thing I do not know cannot be got out of me, and I am a poor liar.

***

The building at 5.10, empty. The loading bay, a lift that smelt of newsprint, which is a smell this building has not made for years and still has. Two floors below Calloway's desk: a door like a stationery cupboard's, and behind it a room the size of my study. A camera on a tripod. A lamp on a stand with a white screen over it. A blue cloth on the wall with a seam down the middle that had been ironed in. One chair. A monitor beside the camera showing the empty chair back to itself.

The editor was in the corridor. I had not met him. Sixty, perhaps, grey, in a jacket he had slept in or had not slept in, holding a mug he had forgotten. The lawyers had rung at 5.10, Calloway said, and the editor had taken the call at the end of the passage with his back to us, nine minutes, which I counted through the glass in the fire door. They had told him what Calloway said they would. Twelve banks named on air as about to fail. A man named as a party to the sinking of a ship. An order of the High Court, and a news organisation already in it to the neck.

Calloway had put it to him in the lift at 4.45, in my hearing. 'It runs on our air, or it runs at nine on everyone else's as "a source close to the story", and we are the paper that had him in the building and sat on it.' It was not a threat. It was a choice of how to appear in the account, not whether.

The editor came to the studio door at 5.52 and looked at me for some time. 'Say it so they can't cut you,' he said. 'Whole sentences. They will want to cut you.' He put the mug on a shelf and went away, and I understood that he had chosen.

At 5.40 the picture editor had photographed both sides of the brown envelope on a desk, and the page inside it. I had asked for that. I knew what Calloway meant by *the envelope goes in a bag*, and I wanted a copy that was not in one.

A young woman with a headset put a microphone on my collar and said, 'The light's yours at six. When you're done I'll put my hand up. Don't look at the monitor.' Calloway sat on a stool at the edge of the light, below the lens, with a sheet of notes he would not use. At 5.59 the clock in the corner of the monitor turned over and the red light came on.

***

I am not going to reproduce it. It is in every paper by now, and I said what I had written down for four years, in the order I had written it. What I want to put here is the chair.

He asked me three questions. The first was who I was, and I said that I was the man the police had been looking for since Sunday week, and that I was saying so because I would rather it came from me.

Then Walbrook, in the words I use to Laura: two banks that do not trust each other, a third party between them holding a deposit from each, and my job, to decide whether the deposits were real. For four years I had thought some were pledged twice. I said *I think*, and said it again at 6.02. I said I had never seen it, only the fingerprints, and that I could be wrong about the thing underneath them, and that a clearing house could still fail on the thing I could be wrong about.

Then why it could not pay. What was due at the start of business today, from members who had already lost a default fund between them. That the first was gone and the second was gone. That the Bank had said *resolution* in the rain.

Then the names.

I read twelve. I read them off the page, because I did not trust my mouth, and I read them in the order they fail, slowly, as a man reads numbers into a telephone. The first two you know. The fourth had been named by a broker at one yesterday afternoon, and the third paid its call at 12.44 with money from the Bank. It took fifty-one seconds. I have not written the names here either. The habit holds. In the corner of the monitor the number watching the stream went from nine thousand to forty thousand to a number the young woman stopped saying aloud.

At 6.05 a man appeared in the glass of the door and went away. A phone rang in the corridor and was picked up before it finished the first ring. Calloway did not look at it.

Greywether. The shell, the broker, the date on the confirmation, which I read out, and the date of the ship, which I read out after it, and nine days between them, which I did not need to say. I said: whoever sank the ship had a partner in Mayfair, who knew nine days early which way the market would go. I said his name. Sebastian Crane. I said I had met him. I did not say Faisal's, because I did not need to. There was an office in St James's listening, and a suite on Park Lane, and they would hear it in the silence where the name was not.

I held up the envelope. I turned it so that the lens saw the front, and the broker's name through the window of the sleeve, and it was lighter than I remembered.

Then the buyer. That there was one. That he had money in London by seven, which was by then fifty minutes off. That the Bank could lend against what he bought as soon as a name was put under the Bank's, and that the name was not mine and was not the Governor's.

I did not look at the monitor. I looked at the lens, as Laura said, and I said: 'Chancellor. You asked me for a signature once. I am not going to ask you for yours. It is a page, and it is in your building, and it is one page long.' It was 6.09. I said that I could not prove any of it until the morning had happened. I said that if I was wrong I would be wrong in front of everyone who was listening, and that I had thought about that, and it did not seem worse than the alternative.

Calloway asked, 'Mr Whitlock, are you sure?'

'No,' I said. 'I have never been sure. I have been right twice in public, in the order. I think I am right a third time. That is all I have ever had.'

At 6.11 the young woman put up her hand. I did not see it. I went on looking at the lens until Calloway stood and touched my arm, and then I was a man in a borrowed shirt in a cupboard with the light off, and the sweat had gone through the collar.

***

'Loading bay,' he said.

'No.'

'Jonas.'

'I said where I was. It would be a poor finish to go out of the back like a man who hadn't.' I did not say the rest, which is that I wanted to see the street with the sound off, after.

He walked down with me. The editor stood at the top of the stairs with his back to the wall, and as I passed he said, 'A million and a half on the stream, and the lawyers have stopped ringing,' and I did not know what to do with that and thanked him.

The lobby at 6.38. A reception desk with a night guard who had stood up. Glass doors, the street beyond them the colour of a bruise that is going, no rain, a bin lorry working its way up the kerb with its yellow light turning. Three people inside the doors, standing in the way people stand who have come to wait. A man and a woman in plain clothes; a uniformed officer behind them with his hands behind his back. The woman said my name. I said yes.

She said the caution, all of it, in the voice of a person who has said it four hundred times and means it each time. *You do not have to say anything.* She said that I was being arrested on suspicion of offences connected with the complaint of Walbrook Clearing House, and named the Act. <!-- VERIFY: arrest on suspicion under the Computer Misuse Act 1990 (unauthorised access / securing access) in connection with the Friday report and removal of confidential material; the earlier chapters say "theft" is the press's word and never a charge. Wording of caution for England and Wales is accurate (PACE Code C) --> No handcuffs. A hand at my sleeve, above the elbow, not tight.

'The envelope, Mr Whitlock.'

I had told Calloway I would not fight for it and I did not. I took it out of the inside pocket of the coat, where a cloakroom ticket had been, and gave it to her. She had been told what it was; she held it by the corners. The uniformed man had a bag open already. The envelope went in with its flap towards the label, and she wrote the time on the label with a pen from her top pocket. 6.43.

It is the first thing of mine that has ever been given a number by someone else.

Calloway stood behind them with both hands in his coat. He said, to the woman, in a perfectly level voice, 'He was never going to run. I'd like that noted,' and she said that it was noted.

They walked me out through the glass doors. I looked at the street, as I had wanted to. Two taxis. A man with a dog. The bin lorry, its yellow light going round on the front of the building. And on the far side, a grey saloon with its engine running and one man at the wheel, the grey car I had taken, since Kessler's visit on Tuesday, to be Crane's.

I did the thing I do. I counted. One car. One man that I could see. The window a hand's width down.

The detective put me into the back of hers, and a hand on my head, and got in beside me. The car with the yellow light went on up the kerb. The grey one pulled out when we pulled out, and stayed three cars behind, and did not hurry.

The man in the front passenger seat was looking in the mirror too.

'Friend of yours?'

'No.'

'Nor mine,' he said, and wrote the number plate on the back of his hand.
