# Chapter 20: Released

Thursday. First sitting. A kitchen table in Kentish Town that is not mine, at twenty to six in the evening, with the owner out and the other person in the flat asleep on the sofa in her coat. I will write the rest tonight when there is a rest. There is going to be.

Priya is alive. I am putting that first, as I put the decision first on Monday, because for four days I have been writing round a hole in the middle of every entry and the hole had her name on it. On Sunday I counted four possibilities and stopped. It was the fifth.

The police had her.

***

On Wednesday, against my own rule, I rang the friend's number, said the date, and gave Calloway the number of the second phone, because a man who can only ring out cannot be found by the one person he wants to find him. Calloway's word came at 11.20 this morning on that phone, by the arrangement we made on Tuesday, which is in Tuesday's entry and which I am not repeating. Four words. *She's here. Come now.* He did not need to say who.

Priya had walked into the front hall of his paper, south of the river, at twenty to ten, with a rucksack of her own and no phone, and asked for him by name, and sat in the lobby for 40 minutes while a receptionist decided whether she was a story or a problem. She had come from Leicester on the first cheap train with £28 in her bag, counted on the platform. She told me the number because she knew I would want it. It was not kindness.

I will not write how I got from behind King's Cross to here. It was not far and I made it long.

She was at this table when I came in. She stood up, and we looked at each other, and she said: 'You've got them with you. Of course you have.' Then she sat down, and Calloway, who knows when a room does not need him, went out for the things we would need later.

What happened to her, in order, because she told it in order.

***

Sunday, 10.07am. Eight minutes after she hung up on me, she sent the figures: to the Bank's whistleblowing address, and to Calloway. From her own laptop, at her kitchen table. Two mugs of tea, both hers, neither drunk. I had stood in her kitchen building a visitor out of them.

11.55. Two police officers at the street door, plain clothes, a uniformed one in the car. Would she come with them to answer questions about an incident at her place of work on Friday afternoon. She asked whether she had to. The older one said he would rather she came voluntarily. She said that was not an answer. He said then he was arresting her, and said the words, and the offence, which was unauthorised access to a computer, and the time, 3.46pm on Friday, and the place, a desk that was not hers. <!-- VERIFY: PACE Code G requires an officer to consider a voluntary interview first; arrest on a Computer Misuse Act s.1 allegation for using one's own credentials at a colleague's desk is weak in law (research/information-security.md: Bignell, Allison) but plausible as an allegation an officer acts on when the request has come from a government department -->

'Like a train announcement,' she said. 'Platform, time, destination.'

They let her take her bag. The laptop went into a clear bag with a label. She gave them her phone out of her pocket because she did not yet understand what she was giving. It had been off since eleven, after the emails, so that she would not have to take my calls until she had decided what to say to me. She asked to lock the door and the younger one said she would be back in an hour. The torn sheet was hers: while they labelled the laptop she tore the bottom half off the top page and put it in the back pocket of her jeans. The member's name, in full, and the 2.8 beside it. They did not search her for paper. Nobody searches anyone for paper.

A police station east of the City; she will not say which. A custody sergeant, a form, her belt and the laces out of her trainers. A cell with a window of glass bricks. She counted them. 48. She knew I would ask.

A duty solicitor at six. The interview at 8.40pm, under caution, 50 minutes. The questions, as she remembers them. Did she log in at Jonas Whitlock's workstation on Friday afternoon. Did she know his access had been restricted. Did she run and print a member-level report. Did she give it to him. Had she passed Walbrook data to any third party. Did Mr Whitlock keep personal records of Walbrook business. Where. Had she seen them. Did she know where he was now.

She said no comment to all of it, on the solicitor's advice. The hardest thing she had done in four years, she said, because every question had an answer and she knew them all.

'Nobody said money,' she said. 'Nobody said a fund. Nobody said a woman's name. They wanted the report and they wanted you. That was all they wanted, and they wanted it on a Sunday, which the solicitor said was the only interesting thing about any of it.'

The solicitor had seen the paperwork. The complaint was Walbrook's: a report run under her credentials after the system had locked mine. The reason two officers were at a door in Bethnal Green on a Sunday instead of a letter asking her to attend on Wednesday was a second document behind the first, which the solicitor was shown and she was not, and which he described to her afterwards in the corridor as "a letter from the Treasury. A director. Asking for urgency." <!-- VERIFY: whether a duty solicitor would in practice see or be told of a referral letter from a government department in pre-interview disclosure; research says the request would be in writing from a senior official, as the Home Office permanent secretary's was in 2008, and that police decide. A cell overnight. At 8.10 on Monday morning they gave her the bag back without the phone, and a sheet of paper. Released on bail, pending further investigation. One condition: not to contact, directly or indirectly, any person employed by Walbrook Clearing House. A date in four weeks. The phone and the laptop retained for as long as necessary. They had asked for the passcodes. She had refused. They had told her what the notice was called that would make her, and that it carried two years, and that they would be in touch. <!-- VERIFY: ledger and outline say "released under investigation" with a bail condition; in practice RUI has no conditions and conditional release is pre-charge bail under PACE s.47 (28 days, extendable). Twenty hours and fifteen minutes. She said that too. I did not have to ask.

***

Monday, 8.10am. She walked out of a police station into the morning I had watched from a café. A ticker in a bookmaker's window gave her the name of a member of Walbrook Clearing House, at 7.58, in default. She knew which one before she read it. Her parents' savings clear through it.

She went home. Her door as she had left it, open, 21 hours. Nothing taken. On the top stack, in my handwriting, a receipt. *Ring me. J.*

'I gave the man in the corner shop a pound and rang you from his,' she said. 'Twice. Not available. You'd turned it off, I expect, with that look you get when you've thought of something. I'd come out of a cell to a note that said *ring me* from a man I couldn't ring.'

8.31. I did not say so. Being exactly right about the time is not always a thing to say aloud.

She took the 2.15 to Leicester. Her parents had to be told in person that the fund with their savings in it cleared through a bank that had failed to pay Walbrook at 7.58 that morning; and it was, she said, the one address in England where she knew who would open the door.

Monday night she bought a notebook. A stationer's near the station in Leicester, £4.99, A5, soft cover, red. She said the colour was not a comment.

Monday night, Tuesday, Wednesday. No phone. 'I wasn't going to put a new one in my pocket so that somebody could ask the network where it was. I got that from you. Everything I've learned this week I got from you, Jonas, and most of it I'd give back.'

She wrote. Thursday's £42m of the 2045, Friday's seven of 40, every figure of the forty-member report she could remember, which was most of it; she has the memory that made her the best analyst I have worked with, and I never once told her that was the reason. Sunday's number. The half-sheet from her back pocket, copied out and the original burnt in her mother's sink. Then what she could reconstruct from four years of exceptions reports read on Saturday night on a laptop the police now have. Not the record. A record. Hers.

'Paper doesn't have a login,' she said. 'I learned that from you too.'

Wednesday she read Calloway's paper from front to back and found he had printed nothing. Thursday she got on the first train.

***

Now the part I have to write, because it is the point of the record.

I had it wrong. From 12.51 on Sunday, when a woman declined to answer a question in a park, I have carried one version: Kessler's client took Priya. Every decision since was built on it. I did not ring Carrow on Sunday night, partly, because I believed the Treasury was the lesser danger and the woman by the gates the greater.

The Treasury took Priya. A director wrote a letter on a Sunday and two officers went to a blue door and took the one person in London who could read my work, and held her 20 hours, and asked her where I keep the books. Kessler, in the park, said that if she told me she did not know where Priya was I would not believe her. I would not have. She was being, as she said, careful with me. I have been hating the wrong person for the right reasons for four days, and the result is the same as being wrong.

So. Two. Not one hunter with a card and a client; two, and they want different things. Her client wants the books before the market finishes burning, because they are worth less every hour. The state wants them never to have existed, and me in a room saying no comment, and it has what she has not: the right to ask a network where a phone is, to a warrant, to 20 hours. The one is paying; the other is in no hurry at all. I had been afraid of the one I could see.

And the police have her phone. My number is in it. My text at 10.41 on Sunday: *Ring me. Please. Not to argue.* In a clear bag, with a label.

***

I said I had not told her because I was trying to protect her. I said it at this table at about half past four, and she let me finish, which was the cruellest thing she could have done.

'Protect me,' she said. 'Jonas. I pulled that report under my own login at your desk, with the camera over it, on a Friday afternoon, because you were sitting there looking like a man who needed one more number. You've always needed one more number. I thought I was giving you one. I didn't know I was giving you the hundredth of something you'd been counting since before I joined. If I'd known what was in those books, if you had told me on Thursday, I'd have done it differently. I'd have done it in my own name, properly, to the Bank, with a lawyer, on the Friday morning. Instead I did it in a cell with my laces out.'

I said I knew.

'You don't. Twenty hours, Jonas. My parents didn't know where I was, and for a while on Monday morning I thought the first they'd hear of it would be a paper with the word "theft" above my name instead of yours. You kept four years to yourself because it was safer. It was safer for you.'

She is right. I have written it down so I cannot say later that she was not.

She has not forgiven me. She said so, in those words, so that it would be on the record, and it is. She had not come for me. She had come because this was the only table in London with the numbers on it, and it happened to have me at it.

Then she took the red notebook out of her rucksack and put it on the table, and put her hand flat on it, and said:

'Mine has Monday to Thursday in it. Yours has four years. Somewhere between them is a list of who falls over and in what order, and Calloway is on his way back with every public filing he can carry, and I am not leaving this flat until it is on one page.'
