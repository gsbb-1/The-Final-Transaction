# Chapter 32: I filed it

Graham Pryce read the second article on the 7.02, standing, because the train was full of people reading it sitting down. Thursday. He had the paper folded to the page the way he folded it to the crossword, and he read the piece twice, and at the second reading he found that he had been expecting it since Monday week, in the way one expects a result one has already seen in the draft.

*Treasury asked police to detain Walbrook analyst.* A letter from a director at the Treasury, on the Sunday, asking for urgency. The analyst's name. Twenty hours. The Treasury did not comment on matters that might be the subject of police inquiries, which was what the Treasury said when the matter was the Treasury.

He had worked it out on Tuesday, without the article, and had spent two days not quite saying so to himself, which was a skill he had and had been paid for. The firm's complaint about Priya's login had gone to a police inbox on the Saturday morning, because the lawyers said it must, and the general counsel had told him on the Tuesday, in the lift, that nobody at the firm had expected anything to happen before Wednesday, if then. But Richard had rung Graham at five past eight on the Monday morning and told him the police had the girl, and had given him the word to put in a text, and Graham had used it. Richard had known on the Sunday night. The firm's own lawyers had not known until the Monday afternoon. Graham had put those two times side by side, the way Jonas put times side by side, and had understood.

The train went into the tunnel. He folded the paper the other way.

***

He was at his desk at 7.35, as he was every day, and he wrote the letter in longhand first, because he found he could not type it. Four lines. Effective immediately. No reasons; a letter that gave reasons was a document, and he had spent thirty years learning what a document was for.

At 8.50 he went up to the fourth floor and asked Richard's assistant whether Richard had five minutes, and was told that he had until nine, and thought that this was where he had come in.

Haldane was at the window. He had been at the window, Graham thought, since the Sunday before last.

'Graham. You've seen it.'

'I've seen it.' He put the letter on the desk. 'That's my resignation. Effective this morning. I've left my pass and the laptop with your assistant. My phone is on my desk. I haven't taken anything out of the building, and I don't want anything sent on.'

Haldane read it without picking it up. Then he looked at Graham with the expression of a man who has been told a difficult thing and is finding out whether it is manageable.

'This isn't the week, Graham.'

'No.'

'You understand the Bank has to be notified. Chief risk officer, mid-crisis. They'll want to know why.' <!-- VERIFY: a UK CCP must notify the Bank of England of the departure of a senior manager (SMF-equivalent under the Bank's FMI regime); the mechanics are assumed, the timing invented -->

'Tell them.'

'Tell them what, exactly?'

Graham considered the hedges available to him. Take some leave. Think it over. Let's take this offline. He could feel the shape of each in his mouth, and found that he did not want any of them, and that this was the first time in his working life that this had been true.

'Tell them that on Sunday the firm's chief executive issued a statement saying that a memorandum was considered by the risk committee in the normal way and its conclusions were not accepted. I chaired that committee for eleven years. The memorandum was never put to it. I know, because I would have had to write the paper, and I didn't. I sent it to the Treasury with a note calling it one analyst's view, and I told Jonas it had been filed, and that was the whole of the committee's consideration.' He heard his own voice and was surprised by how level it was. 'And tell them that on Sunday night you knew the police were going to hold a member of my team before the firm's lawyers knew, and that you gave me a word to put in a text message that no lawyer in this building has ever been willing to say.'

Haldane did not move. He was good; Graham had always thought so.

'You're a decent man, Graham. You're also three years from your pension.'

'Yes.'

'Then I'd think very carefully about what you say outside this building, and to whom. The order against Whitlock covers documents containing the firm's confidential information. All of them. Wherever they are.'

'I'm aware of the order,' Graham said. 'I helped draft the schedule.'

He went down in the lift, three floors, with nobody, and out past the staff door at 9.14 with an empty clip on his coat where the pass had been, and walked to the river because he did not know what else to do with his feet.

***

The paper's number was on its back page. He rang it from his own mobile, standing on the embankment, because he had nothing left to hide from anyone and found the condition strange. He asked for Rhys Calloway and was asked who was calling, and said, 'Graham Pryce. I was, until an hour ago, chief risk officer of Walbrook Clearing House. Mr Calloway has written about my department twice this week. I'd like to speak to him before he does it a third time.'

A pause that meant a hand over a mouthpiece. Then a man's voice, quick, London, saying his name as one word.

'Mr Pryce. I'll say this once. Don't tell me anything on this line. Nothing at all.'

'I wasn't going to. I'd like to see him.'

'Who?'

'I'm not asking you to tell me where anyone is. I'm asking you to tell him I'd like to see him, that I have something he'll want, and that I'm no longer anybody's employee. He'll know what I'd bring.'

Silence. Traffic behind it, and a keyboard.

'Six o'clock,' Calloway said. 'Our front hall. Ask for me. Bring whatever it is with you, and don't bring anyone.' And the line.

He took the train home, at ten in the morning on a weekday, for the first time in eleven years.

***

The packs were in the box room, in box files, on the shelf he had put up for them, one quarter to a file, going back ten years: a decade's habit, begun when the committee had still sent paper and continued after it had stopped, because he had never trusted a thing he could not hold. Forty box files. Each pack a hundred pages or so. The agenda, the minutes, the market-risk section, the default-fund stress numbers, and at the back, every quarter, the collateral concentration appendix: each clearing member, by code, what it had lodged with the firm, in which lines, in what size, with what haircut, and a column headed *observations* that for the last four years had said, in Graham's own words, *no material concentration*. He had signed the Q2 one on the 14th. He had seen his own initials on it in a photograph on Jonas's birthday.

It was, he knew, exactly the thing the order described. He had kept it at home for ten years without once thinking about it, and had spent the last ten days helping to pursue a man for the same offence, and he stood in the box room and let that be true for a full minute before he did anything else.

Then he took down the last sixteen. Four years. He knew when Jonas had started.

They went into the wheeled case he and his wife used for the one holiday a year, and it was heavy, and he did not care.

***

The paper's front hall, south of the river. Calloway came down himself at 6.04: younger than his byline, a look at the case and then at Graham that did the arithmetic in one go.

'Up,' he said. 'Fourth floor. They're in a room at the back.'

They.

The room had no windows, a table, a whiteboard with four-letter codes on it in two hands, and a printer in the corner with its light blinking. Jonas stood up when Graham came in. He had lost weight and his coat had a tear at the knee and there was no bag. There had always been a bag. Graham noticed, and did not ask.

Priya did not stand up. She looked at the case and then at him and said, 'What time?'

'I'm sorry?'

'What time did you resign.'

'Nine. A little before.'

'Then I can be in a room with you,' she said. 'As of nine. Sit down, Graham.'

He sat. Jonas sat. Calloway stayed by the door, which Graham understood to be a courtesy and a precaution in the same movement.

He opened the case and put the box files on the table, sixteen in two rows, and then he said what he had come to say, without one of the words he had used for thirty years to make a thing smaller than it was.

'The risk committee packs. Every quarter for four years. The collateral appendix at the back of each one has every member's lodgements by code and by line, with the haircuts and the substitutions for the quarter. It's what you'd have had on your screens and can't now. It's what she pulled on the Friday and the police have.' He looked at Priya when he said it, and then at Jonas. 'The memo is not in any of them. It was never put to the committee. Richard said on Sunday that it was, and he was lying, and these show it. That's the second thing.'

Nobody spoke. The printer's light blinked.

'The third thing. The police action against Priya was not the firm's. The firm's complaint went in on the Saturday because the lawyers said it had to, and nobody at Walbrook expected a car before midweek. The urgency was the Treasury's. Carrow wrote on the Sunday. And Richard knew on the Sunday night, because he rang me at five past eight on Monday and told me what to text you, before our own lawyers had heard a word. I worked it out from the times. This morning's paper says the same thing, and I'm ashamed that I needed it to.'

Then the thing itself. He had not rehearsed it. There had been nothing to rehearse.

'I filed it, Jonas. Three years ago I read six pages that were right, and I was frightened of what they meant for me, and I filed them with a note that made them smaller, and I told you over a coffee as if it were good news. I filed Priya when I asked her on the Friday if you kept notes. I filed you when I sent that text.' He put both hands flat on the table. 'I'm sorry. I'm not asking for anything. I'm saying it because it is true and it has not been said, and I'd like it to have been said once by the person who should say it.'

Jonas looked at him for a long time and said nothing at all, which Graham found he had expected, and could bear.

Priya said: 'It's not enough.'

'No.'

'I want you to know that I know that. It isn't a thing you say and it's done. Twenty hours in a cell with my laces out, Graham, and my parents' money in a bank that fell over while I was in it, and you're sorry. Fine. You're sorry.' She pulled the first box file across the table towards her and opened it at the back, and he watched her eyes find the appendix and start down the column. 'What I want from you is the four years. Not the apology. The four years, every quarter, and you sitting there until I've got it onto one page, and then I'll decide what else you're for.'

'Yes,' Graham said.

'Good.' She was already reading. 'Jonas. Q3, three years ago. Your second name. Read me what you had for it.'

Jonas had taken a pen out of his coat.

Calloway said, from the door, 'You'll want to know you've got until when.'

'Tuesday,' Graham said. He had not meant to say it yet. He said it anyway, because the hedges were gone and he could not find where he had put them. 'The firm's own forecast, as of yesterday afternoon. The second member meets Monday's call with what the Bank lends it. It does not meet Tuesday's. When it goes, the default fund goes with it, and Richard rings the Bank and says the sentence nobody has ever said.' He looked at the sixteen box files, four years of his own signature. 'Whatever you are doing in this room, you have until Tuesday morning to have done it.'
