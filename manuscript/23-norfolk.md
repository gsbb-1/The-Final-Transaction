# Chapter 23: Norfolk

3.04am. Margaret's kitchen. The light over the cooker, because the main one shows from the lane. Elsie is asleep against my chest with her mouth open and I am writing round her, left-handed on the page edge, which is why this looks the way it does. I am not putting her down. That is a decision and I want it recorded as one.

Friday. In order.

***

Nine o'clock, the call box on the Kentish Town Road, Margaret's landline, as every day this week. Laura answered on the first ring and did not say hello. I wrote what she said on the previous page in the first sitting, back at Calloway's table at 9.30, while my hands were still doing it, because I wanted her words down before I started to improve them. I have read them back. I did not improve them. She said a woman called Dana Kessler had stood in her mother's kitchen with Elsie on the floor, and had been very polite, and had left a card. She said come tonight or I go to the police myself, and she said *say yes*, and I said yes, and the line went.

Then eight hours. Calloway could not leave the paper before five on a day when a second member was wobbling on every screen in the building, the car had to be in his name, and I was not going to stand on a platform at King's Cross in daylight with the rucksack on. Good reasons. I sat behind the curtains and counted them and would have given every one away to be on a train.

Calloway hired the car. His name, his card, his licence, a branch on the Kentish Town Road. If Kessler still has someone on his door, they saw a hire car leave with two men in it, and I have decided not to think about that until I have to. At 4.50 Priya said, 'Go. We've got the page. I can copy a page.' Then: 'She's right, you know. Whatever she said.' She did not know what Laura had said. She did not need to.

We left at 5.25. Calloway drove. Rucksack between my feet, a foot through each strap. He did not talk, and I watched the mirrors because he could not.

The M11 was Friday. Nothing until Stansted. Then, at the Cambridge turn, a marked car came off the slip road and sat behind us, two cars back, and stayed there for nine miles. <!-- VERIFY: M11 junctions for Cambridge; the route M11 to Cambridge station -->

It did not close. It did not drop back. Calloway said, 'It's a motorway. There's a police car.' I said yes. We both watched it. The car was in his name; my name was on nothing. That is true, and it did not help, because the thing I have learned this week is that it is never the form you have thought about.

Cambridge. He came off for the city, and the car came off behind us. Into the traffic by the station. He slowed for a bus. I said, 'Here,' and he said, 'Here,' at the same time, and I had the door open at four miles an hour with the rucksack already on both shoulders, and I was on the pavement among people with wheeled cases before the bus had moved. I heard him pull away. I heard, a few seconds later, a second engine do the same, and I walked into the station like a man who was late for a train, which I was.

The 19.38 to Norwich. <!-- VERIFY: Cambridge–Norwich direct service, roughly 80 minutes, evening frequency --> I paid in cash. Eighty minutes in the vestibule with my back to the door. Thetford, in the dark, where Laura had driven on Sunday night with Elsie asleep behind her and the radio on.

Norwich, 20.57. No taxi; a taxi is a man who remembers where he dropped you. I walked out towards the ring road with the rucksack and a torn coat, and at a garage a man in a van who was filling up asked if I was all right, and I said I was trying to get to the village, and he said he went through it, and I got in. <!-- VERIFY: plausibility of hitching a lift from Norwich ring road out towards a village to the north-east at about 9.15pm -->

He talked about the week. Everyone does. The cash machine at his bank had been empty on Wednesday. He asked what I did and I said insurance, which is the nearest true thing. He dropped me at the war memorial at 21.52.

The grey car was not at the end of it. I looked. I looked for a long time.

***

Laura opened the door before I knocked. She had been standing in the hall. She looked at me, and then at the rucksack, and then at my hands, in that order, and said, 'Kitchen.'

Margaret had gone up. Elsie was asleep. The card was on the table, in the middle, squared to the edge, with nothing else near it, the way you would leave a specimen. Laura sat down across from it and put her hands flat on the wood and said, 'From the start. Not from Wednesday. From the start.'

So I did. 22.40 to 00.50 by the clock on her mother's cooker. I am not going to write it all out; it is in these nine books already. I will put down what she heard.

That I was 36 and something in a reconciliation did not add up, and I wrote it down because I did not trust myself to remember, and the next night there was something else, and by the end of that year it was every night. That I was 37 and I wrote six pages to Graham and to the Treasury and said three years, and they said noted, and I was relieved. I told her that. I had never told anyone that. Relieved, because I had done the thing, and nobody had asked me to do anything else.

That Elsie was born and I went on writing and it was different, and I could not tell her how, so I told her instead what #1 says: that I wanted to be wrong. That I kept the books in Dad's garage so that they would never be in the house. She asked why, and I said because I knew, somewhere, that one day somebody would come for them, and she looked at me for a while and said nothing.

Wednesday. The wrong inbox. Thursday, the pension fund's gilts, and the ship. Friday, the call, Graham, the Bank, the man in the grey jacket, the study. She stopped me there. 'They were in the study while I was at Aisha's.' I said yes. She said, 'You came up to bed after,' and I said yes, and she did not say the next thing.

Saturday, the Treasury: the Chancellor, the Governor, my own memo put in front of me on a table, the statement I did not sign. Sunday: Priya's number, Priya's flat, Kessler in the park, the three million. Her face did not change at the three million. It changed at Brambles. The lock-up, the gap in the wall, the fence, the river. Monday on a bench. The hotel. Calloway. Priya, held twenty hours by the police on a request from the Treasury, and I had thought it was Kessler. Last night: twelve codes on a page, and a name, Greywether, and a man, Crane, and a date nine days before the ship.

I said: I cannot prove it. I said that about four times, and the fourth time she said, 'I know, Jonas. You've said.'

She watched my hands for the first hour and my face for the second. She is a doctor taking a history, and I know what a history is for. It is for finding the thing the patient has not said.

When I stopped she sat for about a minute. Then she asked one question. I had been waiting for who, or why not the police, or how much danger; I had built answers to those on the train.

'Did you ever think of telling me?'

I said: it was work. I said: I didn't want to bring it home. I said: I thought if I told you, you'd make me do something, and I didn't know what to do. I heard each one for what it was before it was finished. She let me get to the end of them. Then she said, 'Those are reasons you didn't. I asked if you ever thought of it.'

And the answer was no. In 1,408 entries I have never written the sentence *I should tell Laura*. I have written her name 300 times, perhaps. Her hands, her questions, the dates she asked. Never that. I sat at her mother's table at one in the morning and found that I had no good answer because there was no answer, only the fact.

I said, 'No.'

She nodded, as though a result had come back and it was the one she expected and had hoped not to see. She did not cry. I would have known what to do with that. She got up and put the kettle on and stood with her back to me while it boiled, and I looked at Kessler's card in the middle of the table and understood that the woman had used my wife's own method on her, and that I was the reason she had been able to.

***

Elsie woke at 1.30. Not crying. The sound she makes. Laura moved and I said, 'Let me,' and she looked at me and let me.

She was standing in the cot, holding the rail, and she looked at me in the dark for a long moment as if checking the face against the record, and then she said the thing that is nearly Dad and put her arms up.

She was one yesterday. I was in Kentish Town. She is heavier than on Sunday, which is not possible, and smells of her grandmother's house, and she went back under in four minutes and I have not put her down.

Laura came up after a while and stood in the doorway. Then she said, very quietly, 'Three million. You could have said yes and none of this.'

'Yes.'

'Would you say it now?'

I did not answer quickly, and she watched me not, and I think it was the first thing all night she was glad to see.

'No,' I said.

'Good,' she said. 'Then it's the other thing. Whatever the other thing is, you do it. Not write it. Do it.' And went to bed.

***

3.04 when I started this. 3.31 now.

Here is what I know at half past three in the morning with my daughter asleep on me. A woman who does not threaten people stood in this room and did not threaten my wife, on behalf of a man I could not name on Sunday and can tonight. There are people less patient than her and I have not met them. I have rung a landline that is on every form her mother has ever filled in, every morning at nine, from the same box 200 yards from a journalist's door, and the police did not need to follow a car; they only need to ask.

The map is a page, and Priya has a copy, and a journalist has asked to print it and been told not yet. Four years of not yet. Laura asked if I had ever thought of telling her and I had not. The honest record of whether I have thought of telling anyone at all is 1,408 entries and a memo.

Tomorrow morning I find a call box and ring the number on Calloway's card and say the date, and when he picks up I tell him to run it. The memo, the date, the three years, and my name. Not the map; not yet; the map is the only thing that man does not have. But my name. Being right in a garage has been worth exactly nothing to the one person who asked.

Elsie is one. I am writing this with my left hand. Her right hand is holding my shirt, and I have counted her fingers, and there are five.
