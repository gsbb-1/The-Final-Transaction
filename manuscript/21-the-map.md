# Chapter 21: The map

Second sitting. Kentish Town, a kitchen table that is not mine, 11.40pm. The first sitting is on the previous page and it is about Priya and it is not finished, because she walked in halfway through it and I put the pen down. I am not going back to it tonight. Tonight is the page.

Elsie is one today. I said it to Laura at nine, from a call box, inside the four minutes, and did not say it again all day, and the record is where I put the things I do not say. Fifty-two weeks. I was in a flat in Kentish Town with the curtains drawn.

***

Calloway's table seats four. He gave it to us and sat on the arm of the sofa with a laptop he said was the paper's and not his, and for the first hour he did not speak. I had not expected that of a journalist. I had not expected most of today.

Nine notebooks on the table, spines out, in order. Priya's book next to them: a red school exercise book, lined, forty pages, bought at a newsagent in Leicester for 80 pence, with three days of her figures in it in two colours of biro. She put it down beside mine and looked at the row and said, 'Well. Now there are ten.' It was not kind and it was not meant to be. She has not forgiven me. She has decided to work with me anyway, which is a different thing, and I will take it.

She is not supposed to be in a room with anyone from Walbrook. She said so when she came in, and sat down.

The method was hers. I would have started at #1 and read forward, because that is how I have read them all week, and it would have taken until Sunday. She said: 'No. Give me the members. Every code you've ever written next to a fingerprint. I don't want the story. I want the list.'

So I went through four years for codes.

For anyone who finds this. Walbrook has about 40 clearing members, the banks and brokers that stand between everyone else and us. I have never written a member's name in these books. I write the code: three letters, Walbrook's own, meaningless outside the building. Beside the code I write what I saw. A settlement fail with the excuse "timing", paired with another member's fail with the same excuse on the same day. The nine o'clock call paid in cash that was raised that morning by lending out bonds, and the bonds swapped back in by the afternoon. A substitution of one line of gilts for another at a time of day when nobody substitutes anything. Twice, a position report that should not have reached me and did. None of it proves anything on its own; I have said so in these books more often than I have said anything else. Together, over four years, the same codes keep standing next to the same fingerprints. That is all the record is. A list of who kept doing it.

I read codes aloud. Priya wrote: a mark against each code, a different mark when the fingerprint was one of the worse kinds. Two hours and ten minutes. My voice went about halfway, and Calloway got up without a word and put a glass of water by my hand and sat down again.

Thirty-one codes appeared at least once. Twelve appeared in every one of the last eight quarters, and those twelve are the map.

She wrote them down one side of a sheet of Calloway's printer paper, in the order of how many marks each had, and then she stopped and said, 'That's not the order.'

'No.'

'Order by when they'd fail.'

That is harder, and it is the only part of this that I have ever been any good at. How much each one posts to us. How much of what it posts is the kind of line I have seen going out on repo in the morning and coming back in the afternoon. How late it pays on a bad day, which is the honest number, and Priya has four years of it in her head because she has done the Thursday reconciliation for four years. We argued about three of the twelve. She won two. The one I won is the one that failed on Monday, which I had at the top and she had at third, and she conceded it because it had already happened.

Then the right-hand column. What each one is holding. Which lines of gilts, roughly how much, and how much of it I think is promised elsewhere at the same time. I say think. I wrote "think" at the top of the column and she did not cross it out.

By half past nine it was one page. Twelve codes. An order. A column of what each one holds. Four years in a bag that smells of my father's garage, and it comes to one side of A4 in Priya's handwriting, which is better than mine.

I looked at it for a long time. You cannot see a map when you are drawing one line of it a night.

Priya did not look at it for long. She turned to Calloway and said, 'Now I need your laptop.'

***

The next hour is hers, and I am going to put it down as exactly as I can because I did not do it and I want to be able to check it.

She cannot see what a hedge fund is short in gilts, or in the pound. Nobody can. Those positions are reported privately if they are reported at all, and the people who hold them do not talk. <!-- VERIFY: under the UK short selling regime, net short positions in UK sovereign debt are notified to the FCA but not published; only net shorts in shares at 0.5% and above are made public, daily, with the position holder's name, the issuer, the percentage and the position date --> What she can see is shares. Anyone short more than half of one per cent of a company listed in London has to tell the regulator, and the regulator puts it on a list every afternoon, with the name of the fund, the name of the company, the size and the date. It is public. It is dull. Nobody reads it except people like Priya.

'Your twelve are members,' she said. 'Members are banks. Banks have parents. Parents have shares.'

Nine of the twelve have a listed parent in London. She wrote the nine parents beside the codes and then she went to the regulator's list and started looking for who was short them.

There were a lot of names. Every fund in London with a view on British banks has had the same view since the spring. She was looking for one name that was on all nine, and she found it in about twenty minutes, and when she found it she did not say anything. She turned the laptop round so that I could read it.

Greywether Capital LLP.

I did not know the name. I want that on the record, because by the time anyone reads this it will seem impossible. I had spent four years at the centre of this and I did not know the name of the fund, and I was sitting in a kitchen in Kentish Town learning it from a list.

Calloway knew it. He had come off the arm of the sofa. 'Sebastian Crane,' he said. 'Mayfair. Thirty-odd staff. Made his first money shorting a currency twenty-something years ago and has been waiting for the next one since. He doesn't talk to us. He doesn't talk to anyone.'

I wrote the two names at the top of this page before I wrote anything else. Greywether. Crane. I have written "her client" eleven times since Sunday. I counted them while Priya was typing. I am not going to write it again.

She did the rest with a ruler. Greywether was short all nine of the listed parents. Of the three biggest shorts on its list, the biggest was the parent of the member that failed on Monday. The second was my second. The third was Priya's third, which she had argued for and I had argued against, and she did not look at me when it came up, which was a kindness. Below that the order wandered, but not far: no name was more than two places from where our page had it.

'Nine out of nine, and the top three in order,' she said. 'Anyone would be short British banks this year. Anyone with sense would be shortest the weakest ones. That's not a coincidence I need to explain. The order, nearly. The order I can nearly explain, if I'm generous, if he's got very good people and they've been reading the same fails I have. Nearly.'

'Could they?'

'Could I have done this from outside the building? In a month, with the public data and a lot of luck. Maybe. Kessler told you a month.' She stopped. 'He didn't have a month. That's why she was in the park.'

I asked her the question I was paid to ask. 'How much of this is our page, and how much is any sensible short?'

'I'd have said half and half,' she said, 'an hour ago.' She was scrolling. 'Then I'd have said the order tips it. Now—' and she stopped scrolling.

The list gives dates. The date on which each position crossed each threshold. She had been reading names and sizes. She had not read the dates. She read them now, down the column, nine parents, and then she went back to the top and read them again, and then she took the biro and wrote one date on the page, under the twelve codes, and put a box round it.

Greywether's shorts in all nine parents had been running at modest size since the spring. On one day, the same day for all nine, every one of them had stepped up: the biggest of them more than doubled. Not the day the tanker sank. Not the day after, when anyone with a screen would have done it. Nine days before.

A Tuesday. The Tuesday before #1399. The week before my birthday. I took the ninth book out of the row and found the entry. #1392. Half a page. Two timing fails, paired, the usual two codes. A member's call paid eleven minutes late. The weather. Nothing. An ordinary Tuesday in the record of a man who thought he was watching everything.

'Say it,' Priya said. 'You're the one who doesn't say things.'

'Somebody knew the date.'

'Somebody knew the date, and the shape, and the order, and he put the position on nine days early so that it would look like it had always been there. And it nearly did.' She put the biro down. 'You asked me which I think it is. I think the names are coincidence. I think the order could be. I don't think there's a coincidence big enough for the date.'

Calloway said, very quietly, 'I'm going to need to be able to print that.'

'Not yet,' I said, and heard myself say it, and heard Priya hear it. Not yet. The phrase of my life.

'Not yet,' she said. 'Not never.'

***

It is 11.40 and the page is in front of me, between the nine books and the red one. Twelve codes. An order. What each one holds. A name. A date with a box round it.

I have been reading these books all week as a record of what I saw. They are also a record of what I did not see. On the Tuesday before my birthday a man in Mayfair whose name I did not know bet very heavily that a ship would sink nine days later, and it did, and I wrote half a page about the weather.

Elsie is one today. The man who knew the date has had the money since Monday. I have had the map since half past nine tonight. Her client has a name now. He also, it turns out, had a calendar.
