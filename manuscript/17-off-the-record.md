# Chapter 17: Off the record

Tuesday. The same room. Rain since five. I slept from about two until the bins at 6.10 and read the third notebook in bed with my coat on. The day in order, because it is the only order I trust.

9.00, a different payphone, by the canal. Laura answered on the first ring. Elsie had slept. Margaret had not. I said I would ring at nine every day, and she said, 'Say one true thing. One. Then go,' and I said that the member that failed yesterday was in my first notebook at entry #38, three years and nine months ago, and that I had known it would be first. A silence long enough for the coins to tick. Then: 'That's not what I meant,' and the line. Four minutes. I am writing it so I do not improve it later.

***

The paper's number is on its back page. I rang the switchboard on the new phone from the hotel room at 9.40, asked for Rhys Calloway, and was asked who was calling. I said a former colleague of Priya Raman's. A pause that meant a hand over a mouthpiece. Then a desk phone, and a man's voice, quick, London, saying his name as one word.

I said: 'Priya sent you a number on Sunday.'

He said: 'Don't say anything else. Where are you?'

I said north. He named a café on a street off Clerkenwell Green, said twenty to two, not before, he had a desk to get through, said, 'Come without a bag if you can,' and hung up before I could tell him I could not.

The rucksack came. It has been on my back or between my feet for 36 hours and I am not sure I could put it down in a room if someone asked me to.

Four hours in the room with the fourth notebook. Then I walked. Up through the back of King's Cross, across the Gray's Inn Road, down through the squares, hood up, counting umbrellas, because counting is what my hands do now that they have no keyboard. Forty-one between the hotel and the café. One of them green, held by a woman at the bus stop across the road from the café, reading her phone with the umbrella low, so that I saw the umbrella and the hem of a coat and not much else. I noted it the way I note everything. I did not think about it. That is the honest record.

Wooden tables, a blackboard. He was at the back, facing the door, two coffees already, at a table where you could not sit beside him, only across, which I noticed because I would have done the same.

Rhys Calloway. Younger than his byline, older than his voice. He did not stand up. He said, 'Phone.'

'Sorry?'

'Your phone. Is it on you?'

'One of them.'

'Christ.' He said it mildly, the way Graham says "not yet". 'Give it here.' He took the little phone, took the back off, took the battery out, and laid the three pieces on the table between us like cutlery. 'Where's the other one?'

'Off. In the bag.'

'Off since when?'

'Yesterday morning. On for three minutes at 2.35.'

He looked at the rucksack and then at me, and I saw him decide not to ask. 'Sit down. I'm going to say some things you'll hate.'

'You rang the switchboard. The switchboard logs every call: number, time, who it went to. That log sits on a server with my name next to your number. I rang you back from a desk phone the paper pays for, so now your number is on a bill as well. If anyone with a warrant wants to know who Rhys Calloway has talked to this week, they don't ask me. They go to the network with a form. A few years back the Met did exactly that to three reporters at another paper over a row about a bicycle and a gate, found the source without asking the reporter a question, and then did it to eighty-odd more journalists before anyone noticed. There's a judge in the way now. Judges say yes.' <!-- VERIFY: "Plebgate" (2012–14): Met obtained call records of three Sun journalists under RIPA; later reporting found forces had obtained records of about 82 journalists over three years; judicial approval required since 2015 -->

'I bought it for cash.'

'Good. Doesn't matter. The number's the number now. Throw it away when you leave here. Not in that bin. The one in your bag is worse: that's yours, and when it's on it tells them where it is. Three minutes yesterday. Where?'

I told him. A hill, four miles out.

'Then they know you were on a hill at 2.35. They don't know where you slept. Keep it that way.' 'Second thing. This.' A small movement of his hand at the room. 'A café. In daylight. If I were looking for you I'd be looking for me, because I'm the one in the phone book.' Then, in the voice from the phone: 'What I can do for you is this. I will never give your name to anyone. Not the desk, not the lawyer, not a judge. There's a section of an Act that says a court can't make me, unless it decides it can, and if it does I'd go to prison, and I'd like you to understand that that is worth exactly nothing to you. They won't ask me. They'll find you the way they find everyone, through a phone and a bank card and a habit, and then it'll be my byline over your arrest.' <!-- VERIFY: Contempt of Court Act 1981 s.10 protects a journalist's sources unless disclosure is necessary in the interests of justice, national security or prevention of crime -->

'Noted,' I said, and heard myself say it.

'Now. Priya.'

He had an email, on paper, folded in his jacket, and he did not take it out; he told me what was in it. Sunday, 10.07am, from a personal address, four pages of figures: the number I know and will not write, the assumptions, the gap, one member short on its own. Signed with her name and job title. He had rung the number at the bottom at 11.30 and got a dead line, and every three hours since. Walbrook's press office did not comment on individuals. He had not run it.

'Why not?'

'Because I had four pages from a Gmail address and a name I couldn't reach, about a number that hadn't happened yet, on a Sunday. The desk doesn't run that. I ran the default yesterday like everyone else, with her email open on the screen next to it, watching it come true one line at a time.' He put the cup down. 'I've got the number. I haven't got the source. I can't check a single figure in it, because the figures are inside your building. What have you got?'

So I did the thing I had decided on at 4am, and I did exactly that thing and nothing else.

The second notebook. I took it out under the table and found the page without looking, because I had marked it last night with the hotel's card. One entry, #304. The day I sent the memo. I turned the book so he could read it and kept my hand on the facing page: the date, the title, who I had sent it to, Graham's phrase about one analyst's view, and the sentence I had copied in from the memo because I had wanted it in my own hand: approximately three years before a moderate shock becomes unmanageable.

He read it twice. Then he took out a spiral notebook, and I said, 'No photographs,' and he said, 'I wasn't going to,' and wrote the date, the title and the sentence in shorthand, and looked at the date for a long time.

'Three years, near enough,' he said. 'Walbrook and the Treasury. And the Treasury did what?'

'Received it. Stamped it. Wrote two words on it in biro.' I did not tell him the words, or that a copy of the stamped page was in the bag a foot from his knee, with a printer's footer that says when they went and found it. One page. That was the decision.

'What else is in there?' he said, to the rucksack.

'Four years.'

'Of this?'

'Of what this turned into.'

'Then give it to me. Not to keep. To read. One night, in a room with no windows, and I give it back.' Both hands flat on the table. 'You came to me, Mr Whitlock. You want it out. I can get it out. But I can't print a date and a sentence I saw in a notebook belonging to a man the police are looking for, whose name I can't use, about a memo I haven't seen. I need the memo. I need the figures checked against something. I need you.'

'You have a date.'

'I have a date.'

'Then you have more than you had at lunchtime.' I put the book back into the bag under the table and did the straps up, and he watched my hands do it. 'When I know you, you'll have the rest.'

'When you know me.' He sat back. 'Priya knew me. She's been gone since Sunday.'

That went in and stayed in.

'One more thing,' he said, when I stood. 'You can't steal information in this country. Not under the Theft Act, not under anything. A judge told a student that in 1978 and nobody's told a judge otherwise since. Walbrook's lawyers know it. What they've got is a civil order about documents, with a return date, at a hearing you're entitled to turn up to with a lawyer and argue. The word in the papers is for you. It's meant to make you feel like a man who took something. Get a lawyer before you talk to me again.' <!-- VERIFY: Oxford v Moss (1979); interim injunction granted without notice carries a return date for the respondent to be heard -->

'I don't have one.'

'Then that's the first thing you don't have.' He put a card on the table, a number written on the back in biro. 'Not my mobile. A friend's. If you need me, ring that from something that isn't yours and say the date you just showed me, and nothing else. I'll know.'

I took it. I left the three pieces of the phone on the table.

***

Rain, still. I turned left, away from the Green, because left was the long way, and went two streets and crossed and went another, and at the corner by a church there was a woman under a green umbrella waiting to cross, not looking at anything, the umbrella low so that I saw the umbrella and the hem of a coat and not much else.

I did not stop. I want that on the record. I saw a thing and kept walking at the same speed, and the only part of me that changed was the part that was counting. Forty-one umbrellas between the hotel and the café. One green one. Now two, or one, twice.

I went through the market on the next street, between the stalls, where a man with a rucksack is one of forty, and down into the Underground at the first station and up again at the next and walked. <!-- VERIFY: a street market near Clerkenwell on a Tuesday afternoon (Exmouth Market or Leather Lane); unnamed on the page --> On the way I went into a shop that was not yesterday's shop and bought another small phone with buttons and another SIM, £29 in notes, and the man asked for nothing. I am a man who now owns one of these a day. Nobody has this number either. The rucksack is on the bed, and nine, and it is 4.40 and I have written this in one sitting with the door locked.

Here is what I think. I was not followed to the café. Nobody needed to follow me. They needed to be where I would go, and there is one person in London I was always going to go to, and his name was on Priya's kitchen table on Sunday, on the top sheet of the middle stack, on the half that had been torn off and taken.

I have been asking for two days what was on that half. I think it was a name and a number. I think they have been standing across the road from Rhys Calloway since Sunday afternoon, waiting for me to be careful enough to find him.

She stopped asking at nine on Sunday. Nobody has asked me anything since.

I do not have a lawyer. I have a phone nobody has rung, a card with a stranger's number, nine notebooks, and a woman with a green umbrella who knows what I look like in the rain.
