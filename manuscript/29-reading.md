# Chapter 29: Reading

She brought it up herself, at nine, which Sebastian Crane had not expected. He had expected one of her men and a receipt.

Wednesday. The rucksack was black and old and had a tear in one shoulder strap that had been sewn, badly, by hand. She put it on the desk, not on the floor, and stood back from it, and he understood that she wanted him to see that it had not been in anyone's hands but hers since the hotel. A man he had not seen before waited at the door with his coat on; she had not introduced him and did not now.

'Nine books,' Kessler said. 'Black, A5, in order. The page he made with the analyst is folded inside the last one, about thirty pages from the end. It's one side. Twelve codes, an order, and a column.'

'You've read them.'

'I've read enough to know what I was carrying.' Her hands were in her coat pockets. 'I told you on Monday that I'd have them by the weekend. It's Wednesday. The invoice will follow.'

In two years she had never once referred to money in his room. He noted it and, since the rucksack was on his desk, let it go.

'Dana. You've done what I asked.'

'Yes.' She looked at the bag rather than at him. 'What will you do with the order, Mr Crane?'

'Read it.'

She waited a moment, as if there might be more, then nodded once and went to the door, and the man there stepped back to let her through first. Crane looked at the man's face for the two seconds it was turned towards him. Broad, patient, forty or so, nothing in it. Then the door was shut, and he was alone with four years of another man's evenings.

Something about the handover had registered. Thirty pages from the end. She had counted. He turned it over, found that it was simply what a careful woman did with goods before delivering them, and set it down.

***

He did not go to the page first. That surprised him a little, and he was seldom surprised by himself.

He took the first book out. The elastic band had gone slack with four years of stretching. Inside the front cover, no name. A date, a Tuesday, two lines about a bond that had not arrived and a man who had said "timing" too easily, and a question mark. The handwriting was small and upright and did not change over the pages in the way most men's did when they were tired or had been drinking. This man had been neither. He had sat down every night at about the same hour and written about the same thing in the same hand, and the hand was a clerk's.

Crane read for an hour before he looked up.

The entries were short. The man counted everything: minutes, confirmations, the number of times a word was used in a day. He never wrote a name. He wrote codes, four letters, which Crane did not know and did not yet need to. He wrote what each code had posted at seven and what it had at three, which lines had failed, who had paid late and by how many minutes, and then, about one entry in twelve, a sentence that was not a fact. *I think this is one bond going round. I cannot prove that.*

By the end of the first book Crane had eleven codes down the margin of a sheet of his own paper with a mark beside each for every bad morning it appeared on, and found that he was doing, with a pencil, exactly what the man in the books had never once done. The man had written it all down one day at a time and never turned round to look at the row of days behind him. It had taken a stranger an hour.

His assistant brought coffee at eleven and did not speak. The floor hummed through the carpet, thirty-one people watching the thing he had not needed to watch since the spring.

***

The second book had the entry he had heard about and not seen. #212. Two members, the same line, the same morning, both saying timing, both paying cash at nine and swapping bonds back in within four minutes of each other at ten past three. The man had written: *I think it is one bond, going round. I cannot prove that.* Crane read it twice. It was good. It was exactly right, and it had been exactly right 1,200 days ago, and the man had closed the book and gone to bed.

The fourth book had the memo. #304: a date, a title that Crane thought rather fine, the recipients, the covering phrase, *one analyst's view*, and the sentence about three years. And then, three weeks later, an entry that Crane read standing up, because he had got up to stretch and had not sat down again.

*Graham says the Treasury have noted it. He was relieved. I will put down what I have not put down. So was I.*

Crane put the book down and went to the window. A warden was writing a ticket in the quiet street. He had spent twenty-five years being told by men like the one in these books that he was lucky, and had come to understand that they were describing themselves: a man who believes in luck is a man who has seen the thing coming and decided that seeing was enough. Here was one who had seen it at thirty-six, written it down at thirty-seven, been *relieved* when nobody listened, and gone on seeing it for three more years, every night, in a hand that never shook, and had never once in 1,400 evenings done anything about it that could not be filed.

So much knowledge. He could not remember when he had last felt contempt for a man he had never met, and he took a moment to enjoy it, because it was rare.

***

He read through lunch. The seventh book changed somewhere in the middle, and he saw where before he understood why: the entries grew a last paragraph that was not about bonds, and the paragraph had a child's name in it. He skipped those. A baby's weight to one decimal place told him nothing about the gilt market.

The ninth book he read slowly for its last fortnight. The birthday. The wrong inbox. A pension fund's bonds in two places, Thursday, and that night a ship. The Treasury on the Saturday, in a voice so level that Crane had to remind himself the man had been looking at his own memo across a table. Sunday: a woman by a park gate, a figure, *the house, cleared, four times over*. Monday on a bench. Then the entries went short and frightened and were written on buses and in hotels, and Crane found himself less interested, because a frightened man was any man.

He turned back, before he went to the folded page, to the week before the birthday, and found the Tuesday. #1392. Half a page. Two timing fails, the usual pair. A member's call paid eleven minutes late. The weather.

He read it with real pleasure. On that Tuesday, at a desk with no screen on the wall, he had put on the largest positions of his life. The man whose job it was to see everything had seen two fails and some rain.

***

The page was where she had said it was.

One side of A4, printer paper, in two hands: a woman's, quick and clear, for the codes and the order; his, small and upright, for the right-hand column. Twelve four-letter codes down the left. Beside each, a rank. Beside each rank, a line or two: which gilts, roughly how much, how much of it the man believed was promised elsewhere at the same time. At the head of the column, one word, underlined: *think*.

Under the twelve, a date with a box round it. Nine days before the sinking. In the woman's hand, with no comment.

He looked at the date for some time. Then he turned the page face down, because he had seen what he needed from it and did not care for the box.

The codes meant nothing to him. They were Walbrook's own, four letters, and the man had been faithful to his rule: not one name in nine books. Crane had, from the tallies in his margin, a reasonable idea which code had failed on Monday week, because it was the one that had stopped appearing, and he could guess two more from their size. Guessing was for the men downstairs.

He used the ordinary telephone, not the one in the drawer.

Anthony Pell answered on the fourth ring from somewhere with a dog in it. Crane said that he had a small question about the firm, and read him a four-letter code.

The silence told Crane that Pell knew what he was being asked and where the code had come from.

'Sebastian. You're asking me to read you the membership list.'

'I'm asking you what twelve words mean. The codes are in the appendix of every pack Richard has ever sent you.' <!-- VERIFY: whether a CCP board pack would list members by internal code alongside their names; plausible for a risk appendix, invented here -->

'Those packs are confidential.'

'So, you told me, is everything in these books, and you wanted them ash. The next best thing is a room with no screens and one reader. Twelve words.'

Pell gave him twelve words, in the voice of a man reading out his own evidence. Twice he asked Crane to repeat a code; once, at the fourth, he said 'Christ' quietly. The second was the name people had on their debit cards. The dog barked and was told to stop.

'I don't want to know,' Pell said at the end, 'what you're going to do with those.'

'You never have,' Crane said, and thanked him, and put the telephone down, and wrote the twelve names on his own sheet beside the twelve codes in a hand that was, he noticed, not as steady as the clerk's.

***

Then he planned it, which took less time than reading had, because he had been planning it since the spring without knowing the names.

The first on the list was gone; the auction of what was left of its book had started yesterday. The default fund had paid for that, and the Governor's money had paid for the rest; the market had steadied on Monday in the way a market steadies when a central bank is lending to anyone who asks. The second on the list was alive on that lending and on nothing else. Its collateral, the man's column said, was long gilts, three lines in particular, and more than half of it the man believed was working another job in the repo market at the same time.

Three. The second, the third and the fourth. Not the whole map; a man who sold twelve things at once would be seen, and three would do the work. He knew now which lines each had posted. One could borrow those lines and sell them, specifically, hard, so that the exact bonds those three had lodged fell faster than the market; one could, through the right desks, make those lines expensive to borrow on the days the three members needed them back. <!-- VERIFY: feasibility of targeting specific gilt lines by short-selling them via repo and tightening their repo availability, so that one member's collateral falls faster than the market and its repo-funded cash raising becomes dearer; mechanically plausible, scale uncertain --> Walbrook would mark the collateral down and call them for cash the same morning, and they would go to the repo market for the cash, and the repo market would have been there first. The fund was built for two large members at once. Three, within a day, and the fund was gone, and Walbrook would have to say to the Governor what a clearing house was required to say, and the Governor would have to do the thing nobody had ever done.

He did not think of that as a bad outcome. He thought of it as the market telling the truth about itself, at last, with his help, and of nobody, anywhere, ever again saying luck.

The day. The books had it. The man had logged payment times for four years, and the three names paid latest on Tuesdays, every bad Tuesday, by a margin Crane could see without a pencil; something in how their collateral came home from the repo market at the start of each week left them short on Tuesday mornings. <!-- VERIFY: invented pattern; a weekly settlement or repo-roll cycle that leaves certain members thinnest on Tuesday mornings is plausible but not sourced --> And by Monday the auction of the first member's book would be done, the loss known, and Walbrook calling the survivors to refill the fund, so that on Tuesday morning every one of the twelve would be meeting the ordinary call and the refill at once. The man had written, in the ninth book, *the one that failed this morning was not the weakest. It was first in the queue.* Crane intended to shorten the queue.

Tuesday. He wrote the date at the top of the sheet. Four trading days to build it without being seen, which was enough, because nobody was looking at anything but the pound.

***

The sheet with the day's number came at six and he read it once and put it in his pocket without comparing it with anything.

Nine books on his desk, in order, spines out, the way she had brought them. He put the elastic band back on the first one and it would not hold, and he left it. He had been going to telephone her, on the phone in the drawer, to say the work had been satisfactory, and found that he did not want to hear her ask again what he would do with the order. He had never, in two years, declined to pick up that phone.

He turned the folded page face up again, and looked at the box round the date, and at the two hands on the one sheet, and at the word *think*, and understood that the clerk had been right about one more thing. A map that two people had was a map that nobody had. The second person was in Kentish Town with the woman whose hand was on this page, and he had read it, by his own account, a hundred times.

Crane picked up the ordinary telephone again and asked the man at the door to come back up.
