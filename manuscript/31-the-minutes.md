# Chapter 31: The minutes

The second story was shorter than the first, and Caroline Strand, who had read a great many documents whose length was a measure of their weakness, knew what that meant before she had finished the headline.

Thursday, 6.15am, the car, the glass up. *Treasury asked police to detain Walbrook analyst.* Six hundred words. A letter from "a senior Treasury official", dated the Sunday before last, to the Metropolitan Police, "asking that the matter be dealt with as one of urgency", described to the paper by "a person who has seen it". The analyst, named now, thirty-one, held about twenty hours, released on bail with one condition. A line from a former Director of Public Prosecutions that a government department asking the police to hurry was "not improper in itself, but unusual, and the kind of thing one would expect to be recorded". And, two-thirds down, where he had put the Treasury's reply on Sunday, the Treasury's reply from Saturday, printed again, word for word, in her own sentences: *The Treasury does not comment on matters that may be the subject of police inquiries.*

He had asked her eleven questions and she had answered one and a half. He had answered the ninth himself. <!-- VERIFY: whether a journalist could obtain a description of a departmental letter in a police file this quickly; the leak's route is deliberately left unstated (duty solicitor, police, Walbrook's lawyers are all possible) -->

She read it twice, which she did not usually do, and then she put the phone face down and looked at the park, where nobody was running this morning, because it was raining properly.

'Simon.'

'Chancellor.'

'Who has seen your letter?'

Carrow did not open his folder. 'The police. The Crown Prosecution Service, if it went that far, which I doubt. Walbrook's solicitors, possibly, as the complainant. The analyst's duty solicitor, in disclosure before interview, if the officers chose to show it, which they should not have.' He said the last part as a man says the name of a colleague who has disappointed him. 'It did not come from this building.'

'It has my department's name on it and the word "urgency" in it, and on Tuesday week, in a room under the Cabinet Office, with the Governor and the Cabinet Secretary present, I said that the matter was one for the police and for Walbrook and not for this government.' She did not raise her voice. She had never found that raising it did anything a pause could not. 'Those minutes exist.'

'They do.'

'And they record me saying it twice.'

'They record you saying "theft" twice, Chancellor, attributing it both times to Walbrook, and saying once that the matter was not one for government.' He had read them; of course he had. 'They do not record the letter, because the letter was not raised.'

'By me.'

'By anyone.'

She let that lie where it fell. It was a perfectly good answer, and it was the answer of an official, and she was not going to be examined by an official. She had spent twenty years asking the question underneath the question, and the question underneath this one was: had a Treasury director written to the police on his own motion, as the minutes implied by their silence, or had the Chancellor, in her room, with the door shut, at ten past three on a Sunday, been told that it would be prudent, and said that he should do whatever he judged appropriate. Both were true. One was on paper. The other was in a room with two people in it, one of whom was being very careful with her this morning.

'When were you going to tell me that it was in writing?'

'You knew it was in writing, Chancellor. I said so.'

'Yes,' she said. 'You did.'

***

The prime minister saw her at a quarter to eight, in the study, standing, which was how he conducted conversations he intended to be short.

He had the paper. He had, she saw, the online version on his phone as well, as though the two might differ. He did not sit down and he did not ask her to.

'Caroline. On Tuesday you told the room it was nothing to do with us.'

'I told the room, Prime Minister, that a clearing house had made a complaint and that the police were dealing with it, and that it was not a matter for this government. All of which was accurate.'

'A Treasury director wrote to the Met.'

'A Treasury director drew the attention of the police, in writing, to a matter of concern to the department. Officials do that. It is recorded, as the former DPP helpfully says it should be, and it was.' <!-- VERIFY: whether a senior official's letter to the police would be regarded as routine; the research notes treat it as unusual but not improper, after the 2008 Home Office pattern -->

'Did you know?'

She had known he would ask it, and had decided in the car that she would answer it exactly.

'I knew that Simon considered it prudent to put the department's concern in writing, and I did not object.'

He looked at her for a moment in the way that he had, which was to seem not to be looking at anyone. He was not a stupid man. He was a man who had worked out, some years ago, that being thought a little stupid was restful.

'So you knew.'

'I have told you what I knew.'

'All right.' He put the phone in his pocket. 'Here is where I am. I have a clearing house that the Governor tells me, in words of one syllable, may not be a clearing house by the end of next week. I have a pound that I am not allowed to say the price of. I have a Bank that wants to buy bonds and a Chancellor who will not sign for it, and I backed the Chancellor, and I would like to go on doing that, because there are three weeks to polling and the alternative is a new Chancellor with three weeks to polling. But I now also have a Treasury that has been hunting a thirty-one-year-old analyst through the police while its Chancellor said on television that she had never seen a memo that turns out to have been in her box for a fortnight.'

'Prime Minister—'

'Monday.' He said it quite gently. 'I would like the situation stabilised by Monday. I don't mean the pound; I am not a fool. I mean this.' He touched the paper. 'I would like there to be no third story. I would like the Governor to stop ringing me at six in the morning. I would like, when I am asked on Monday whether I have full confidence in my Chancellor, to be able to say yes without my press secretary closing her eyes. Stabilise the situation, Caroline. You are the one who keeps telling me that is what you are for.'

'Yes, Prime Minister.'

'Good.' He opened the door for her himself, which he did when he wanted a thing to look courteous in the corridor. 'And Caroline. The memo. I'd read it, if I were you. Before somebody asks you a question about page four.'

***

The day was the day. There was a call with the Governor, who told her that the Bank had now lent to Walbrook and to eleven of its members, that the second member had met the morning call at five to nine on the Bank's money and would do so again tomorrow and after that he would not say, and that the auction of the first member's book was in its third day and the prices were "instructive". He told her, in the tone he used for things he was obliged to tell her, that he would be meeting the managing director of the Gulf fund that afternoon, at the fund's request, and that the Treasury was welcome to send someone. She said that she would consider it, and did not send anyone, because a Treasury official in a room with the Bank and the fund whose withdrawal had started this was a photograph she could not afford, and because she already knew what the fund would ask for.

There was the eleven o'clock, where she said "monitoring" and "the Bank has the tools it needs" and did not say the other thing. There was a lunch she did not eat, and the Opposition's letter, and a request from the Treasury Committee for the minutes of all meetings on the Walbrook matter, which Carrow said would be resisted in the normal way and she told him to resist in the normal way and found that she did not enjoy saying it. There was Carrow, all day, at her left, being very careful.

At twenty past seven the private office emptied, and she said that she would read for an hour and did not need anyone, and the door closed, and the Treasury went quiet in the way it did at that hour, a building the size of a town with the sound turned down.

The box was on the side table. The folder was where it had been since the Wednesday before the sinking, with Simon's yellow flag on it, and his cover note on top, two paragraphs, which she had read. She took the cover note off and put it face down on the desk and sat with the six pages in front of her and did not, for a moment, turn the first one.

She had cross-examined on a thousand documents. She knew how to read one: not for what it said, but for where it was weak, for the adjective that was doing work the facts would not do, for the table whose columns did not quite add. She had read the first page of this one a week ago Saturday, across a table, upside down, while a man in a torn coat told her what it meant, and she had read the sentence everyone had read, and she had turned no page because she had not needed to; she had needed the man's signature, not his argument.

She turned the page.

It was a note, not a memorandum. It did not argue. It set out, in a prose so plain that she found herself looking for the trick, what a clearing house could see and what it could not, and then it showed her, in four tables and a footnote, what the writer had seen over eleven months: fails that came in pairs, with the same excuse on the same mornings; cash paid at nine against bonds returned at three; the same lines of government debt arriving and leaving by routes that had no reason to be the same. He did not say that the collateral was pledged twice. He said, in a sentence she read three times, that the pattern was "consistent with" collateral financing more than one obligation at a time, that he had "no direct evidence of any individual chain", and that he was "unable to exclude" the benign explanation but could "no longer regard it as the more probable". Page four had the estimate, and the working under it, and the working was an assumption about how fast the thing was growing, stated as an assumption, with a range. Three years was the middle of the range. The bottom of the range was two.

The sixth page was a list of five things that could be done, in order of how little they would cost. The first was a reconciliation that would have taken one analyst a month. None of them had been done. She knew that without asking, because if any of them had been done the man would have said so in the room, and he had not said so, and he was not a man who left things out.

It was right. Not clever: right, in the way that a well-drawn pleading was right, so that you could not find the sentence to pull at. She had spent twenty years finding the sentence. There was not one. He had written down every weakness in his own case before she could, and then he had estimated three years, and it had been three years, and a week ago Saturday she had put two paragraphs on Treasury paper in front of him and asked him to say the opposite over his name.

She sat for some time with page four under her hand.

Then she did a thing she had done on every document she had ever read in practice, without thinking, for twenty years, and had not done on any document since she had entered government, because in government the rule was that a minister's hand on a page was a fact that could be requested. She took the pen from the holder, and in the margin beside the sentence with the three years in it she wrote the date, and the time, and her initials, in the small hand that had filled a hundred blue notebooks.

She looked at it. Three letters and a date. The only record that would ever exist of the Chancellor of the Exchequer having read the thing she had said she had not seen, in her own hand, on the Treasury's copy, where anyone with a file reference could ask for it.

She did not cross it out. She put the six pages back in the folder, and the cover note on top, and the folder in the box, and the box where the box went, and she turned off the lamp.

In the car she rang nobody. She watched the park go by in the rain and thought about the first of the five things on the sixth page, the one that would have taken a month, and about the word she had made Simon put in a letter, and about the minutes of a room under the Cabinet Office, which existed, and would exist in thirty years, and had her saying twice, in her exact voice, the thing she had known at the time was not true.

Monday, he had said. She had until Monday to stabilise the situation. She had just, with three letters, made it a very small amount less stable, and she found that she knew precisely why she had done it, and that she was not going to tell anyone, and that it was the first thing she had done in a fortnight that she would have advised a client to do.
