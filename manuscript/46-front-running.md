# Chapter 46: Front-running

They had wheeled a television into the room with no screens, and it was the only thing in the building Sebastian Crane had ever wanted taken out again.

His assistant had done it at 5.55 on her own initiative, with an apologetic face and a trolley from the boardroom, and had stood by the door in case she was needed. She was not. He watched eleven minutes of a man in a borrowed shirt explain his firm to him. At 6.12 he said, 'Thank you, Miranda,' and she took the trolley away, and he sat for a while in the clean square where it had been.

It had been well done. He thought that now, at eight, with the telephone on the desk face up and three lawyers breathing in it. The shirt was wrong, the chair was wrong, the lamp put a shine on the forehead. None of it mattered. Whitlock had spoken in short sentences, with numbers, in the voice of a man reading from a ledger, and he had named Greywether's trade dates against the sinking one by one and held up an envelope. Crane did not need to ask where the envelope had come from. He had asked a woman for a result once, and told her nothing else.

'Sebastian,' said the senior partner. 'Are you there?'

'I am.'

'Then please hear the point again. Nothing in the broadcast is actionable by you tonight. Everything he said about the dates is a question for the police and we will answer it in writing, with counsel, at a time of our choosing. What you must not do, this morning, is trade on any information that has not been made public. Anything. Not a position, not a hedge, not a conversation with a friend.'

'Quite.'

'Do you understand?'

'Entirely. He has made it all public. It was the neatest thing about it.' He heard the silence at the other end and enjoyed it, a little, which he recognised as the habit of a man who had enjoyed things for fifty-two years. 'Thank you. I'll ring you.'

He ended the call. The two others stayed on for the length of a breath before they understood that they had been dismissed.

***

Then he did what he had been putting off since 6.12, which was to think about it.

They would buy. That was the whole of it, and he had known it by the second minute, before the man said the word *buyer* and held it a beat. A man does not build a map of who must sell, in what order, over four years, in order to stand and describe it. He builds it to be on the other side. Somebody with money, and a state behind the money, and a central bank willing to lend against what was bought, would stand at the open and take every line that the twelve were forced to throw. In the order they were forced to throw it. At the price that suited the buyer, which was a price that stopped falling.

Everything Greywether owned depended on prices that went on falling.

He had, in the cabinet behind him, the original of the page. It was a plain folded sheet in a clear sleeve. He did not take it out. He did not have to; it was in his head the way the books were in his head, and for the first time since the Wednesday before last it did not feel like a possession. It felt like a bill.

His telephone rang at 8.04. He knew the number.

'Anthony.'

'Seb.' Pell's voice was low and fast, a man talking from a corridor. 'I have a board call in twenty-six minutes. I cannot be on a line with you. I will say three things and then I will put this down. The Bank has told the exchanges to hold London's open until ten. Walbrook's nine o'clock has been moved to noon. And at six the Bank told us to cut every external connection we have, every link, every feed, on the grounds that the broadcast was a security matter. I do not think it was a security matter. I think they did not want anyone with a login to know what they were doing.'

'Who?'

'I don't know. I have not been told who the buyer is. I am on a board call.'

'Anthony.'

The line was dead. Crane held the handset for a second longer than the call needed, looking at the grain of the desk, and thought: *he has stopped saying my name.* He put the information where it belonged, which was nowhere yet. A fund with money and no authority. An open held to ten. A clearing house that had cut its own wires.

He got up. He walked to the window, which looked across a quiet street at the blank back of a bank, and where nobody could see in; he had paid for the glass. Two hours. A little under two. The market in London was shut but the world was not. In the over-the-counter market, between dealers, by telephone, it was possible at this hour to buy the lines the fund would want; the Bank could hold an exchange's door and could not hold a man's phone.

He lifted the handset again and pressed the number for the floor.

'Seb.' His head trader's voice, thin, awake since four.

'Listen carefully. I want the shorts in the first eight lines closed. All of them. Buy them in pieces, through six brokers, nothing over a size that anybody would look at twice. Do it now, at these prices, before the room at ten decides what it thinks they are worth. Then keep what you've bought. When the fund comes in at the open I want to be standing there with the goods it needs.'

A pause. 'Buy the lines we're short.'

'Yes.'

'And sell them back at ten.'

'To a buyer who has told the whole country it must have them.' He found he was smiling, though not for long. 'It is called getting there first. The rest of the book stays exactly as it is, and you will not tell the floor why.'

'The rest is the—'

'The rest stays. Do not say it on this line.'

He put the handset down. It was a clean move, and it left Greywether richer by the difference whether or not the fund won, and it was what he would have done to anyone, and it was not enough. He knew that as he put the handset down. It was a hedge against being wrong about the sum. It did not touch the man who would be giving the sum.

***

He had thought about the man for twenty-five minutes without noticing how.

Whitlock did not hold the plan; the plan would be in a room with a dozen people in it, and the dozen would write it down. He held the order. The page in the cabinet said what the twelve were and where they stood. It did not say what to do when the map was wrong, and a map is always wrong by ten o'clock. That judgement was in the man's head. Crane had read four years of his evenings and had found that nothing the man understood had ever been put on paper, only the evidence for it. It would be the same today.

And the man, Crane's solicitor had said at a quarter past, was being released on bail from the police station where he had been taken at twenty to seven, with a condition the solicitor had not been told. The Bank had asked for him. *The Bank.* In the corner of his eye the television was not there any more, and he saw it anyway.

He pressed a different number and did not give his name.

'Where are you?'

'Outside, Mr Crane.' The older one. The voice of a man who read his own custody sheets. 'Where we were told.'

'Good. Follow him. He is about to be driven somewhere by people who will think they are being careful. I want the address by nine. Not before nine, if it means being seen.'

'And then?'

He had expected the question. He had not expected the little cold lift of the stomach that came with it, a sensation that in his own account he would later describe as irritation. Kessler would have said the child was not a price. Kessler would have said it standing in his room with her hands in her pockets, in a voice that made one want to put the question again to see whether it would crack. Kessler was gone. This one had been sent to the stairs on Friday and had gone. This one was waiting.

'There will be someone to meet you. The man has a daughter,' said Crane. 'You know where she was. You followed the car on the Saturday, and lost it at the roundabout, and found it again on Monday outside a house with a plane tree. She moved on Tuesday and I am told she moved back this morning, because the man she is married to has stood in front of a camera and a mother goes where her friends are. Go there first. Bring her to me. Nobody is to touch her.' The cold went through him again and he put it into his voice as patience. 'I should like to talk to him and I should like him to listen. That is all I am asking. Do you understand what I am asking?'

'Yes, Mr Crane.'

'Say it.'

'Bring her to you.'

'Thank you.' He heard how it sounded. He did not, for the first time in his life, wait to enjoy it.

***

At 8.31 he took the key from the chain and opened the steel cabinet and looked at the nine books in their row, and the Treasury's stamped copy of a memo on top of them, and the unsigned page of a statement that had never been released. He had not touched any of it since Sunday.

It occurred to him, with the hurry he could not now put aside, that a man who had the original of everything could burn it, and that he was not going to, because the day was not over and only a man who had decided it was over burned things.

He sat down. He picked up the handset a third time and rang the one number he had never rung from this room, a firm in Bishopsgate that provided guards to office buildings and did not ask what a guard was for.

'I need two people on Walbrook,' said Crane. 'The street, not the building. Cannon Street, and the lanes behind Bank. They will be looking for a man of forty in a shirt that does not fit. He will be coming on foot. I want them there by nine.'
