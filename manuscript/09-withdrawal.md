# Chapter 9: Withdrawal

#1403

Khalid Al-Mansouri had slept for two hours and did not feel the want of it. He had been in the room the staff called the bridge since five, and he had not been alone in it. The Western news channels ran on the far wall with the sound off, and he found he could follow them well enough from the maps and the faces. The map was the same on all of them: the Strait, a red mark where the tanker had gone down, and to one side a coastline he had grown up looking at from the other direction. The faces changed. Overnight a senator in Washington had used the word "freeze". A British minister had said that no option was ruled out, and the woman who hoped to replace her in five weeks had said that was not good enough. By the time the sun was fully up, a man on an American business channel was explaining to his viewers how much of the British government's debt was owned by "the regime", and getting the figure wrong by a third.

His own government had not sunk the tanker. He had asked, through the channels one asks, and been told no. He had been told no before about things that turned out to be yes. But the men who told him this time had been frightened, and in his experience frightened men were rarely lying. It was possible they were wrong. He set that aside. It changed nothing he had to do today.

He had taken two calls before dawn from men whose names he would not have written down, with two opposite instructions, and had told each that he was studying the matter. That was true. He had been studying it since Thursday night, when he had asked for a list of every asset the fund held in London and where its collateral sat, and had found the largest line to be a clearing house near the Bank of England called Walbrook.

The list lay on the long table now, under his hand, and beside it the draft he had asked Nasser for on Friday morning: what notice the fund must give, to whom, and how many days it would take. He had read the draft four times. He had the figure by heart and did not care to say it aloud.

It was not complicated. The fund held British government bonds in quantities that made it one of the largest foreign holders of that debt. A great part of them were lodged as collateral, through the three banks that cleared for the fund, against its own hedges at Walbrook, where they secured promises the fund had made to other people. <!-- VERIFY: a sovereign fund would normally access a clearing house as a client of a clearing member bank rather than directly; the exact arrangement is left vague --> As long as the bonds sat in London they were safe from everything except London. And London, this morning, was discussing on television whether to take them.

He knew what freezing meant. The money would not vanish. It would be there, exactly where it was, and untouchable, for as long as it took the lawyers of three countries to agree on who had sunk a ship. He had seen it done to others. It took years. He had one client, and he had promised that client, in a room he could picture precisely, that its wealth would never be hostage to anyone's foreign policy, including its own.

Moving too slowly lost everything. Moving too fast cost a great deal. The market knew he was there. If the fund sold at Monday's open, it would sell into a market that had watched the same channels he had, that knew he had no choice, and that would price his lack of choice to the penny. He had worked at a Western bank in his twenties and remembered how it felt to be on the other side of a seller who had to sell. It had felt very good.

And there was the other thing. The thing he understood and most of the men on the television did not.

He could not simply take his bonds. The positions they secured had to be closed first, and the clearing house had its procedures, and the procedures had their hours. <!-- VERIFY: how quickly a sovereign fund could unwind cleared positions and withdraw collateral, and what notice is required --> What he could do today was give notice: so that on Monday morning Walbrook, and every bank that cleared through Walbrook, would know that the largest line in the book was leaving. Those bonds had been lent and borrowed and lent again. Every bank that had used them to back a promise of its own would find, on Monday, that the bond it had borrowed was being called home, and would have to find another, in a market where everyone was looking for the same thing. He thought of it as a man asking for his coat at the end of a party and discovering that four other guests were wearing it.

It would not be his doing. He knew that it would be his doing.

At 10.20 Nasser came back in and closed the door, which in that room meant that the nine other people in it stopped pretending not to listen. Al-Mansouri gave him the shape of it, for the desks that would carry it out. Notice to Walbrook, through the clearing banks, today. Positions to be closed in order of size from the open. Bonds to be sold as the collateral came free, in London and then in New York. Proceeds to be settled anywhere that was not London or New York. <!-- VERIFY: whether sale proceeds settled through London or New York custodians could themselves be frozen; and whether selling before a freeze is legally clean -->

'The three banks will tell the clearing house,' Nasser said, 'and the clearing house will tell the Bank of England, and the Bank will tell the Treasury. The British will see the notice before the market does.'

'Yes.'

'They will call.'

'They will call,' Al-Mansouri agreed. 'Put it in final. Do not send it until I say.'

Nasser went. Al-Mansouri read the notice when it came back, changed one word, and put it face down on the list. Then he sat with his hands folded and watched the smoke on the far wall, which had not moved since Thursday night. It occurred to him that it was the only thing in the world that had not.

The call came at 11.40. Nasser said, 'Mr Crane, from London,' and waited, because he knew the name and knew what his principal's face did when he heard it.

'Put him through.'

'Khalid.' Crane's voice came down the line unhurried, warm, as if they had spoken the week before. 'It's been, what, six years?'

'Seven.'

'Seven. I'm sorry about the circumstances. I imagine your morning has been long.'

'It has been a morning.'

'Then I'll be brief, for both our sakes. You're going to sell. Everyone with a television knows you're going to sell. When you do, you'll be selling to a market that knows you have no choice, and it will charge you for the privilege in a way you won't enjoy. I'm offering to take your gilt position off your hands tonight. All of it, or as much of it as you can free. Quietly, in one piece, settled wherever you like. At a price.'

'What price?'

Crane named it without hesitation, as if he had carried it around all morning in his pocket. It was steep. It was, Al-Mansouri judged, about where the market would be by Tuesday afternoon, which was the point.

'That is not an offer,' he said. 'That is a forecast.'

'It's both. That's what makes it fair.'

'You would then hold a very large number of bonds you believe are about to fall.'

'I'd be hedged, Khalid. I'm a cautious man.'

'You were not cautious with my money.'

A pause, and the smallest change in the warmth. 'That was a long time ago, and the fund was up when you left it.'

'The fund was up,' Al-Mansouri said. 'I did not leave because of the fund.'

Crane let that go by, which was itself an answer. 'Think about it for an hour. You'll get a worse price from anyone else, and you know it, and I'm the only buyer who will still be a buyer on Monday. You know what your order will do to that clearing house.'

'Yes.'

'Then take the money and let it be someone else's weather.'

'No,' Al-Mansouri said.

'No to the price, or no to me?'

'You have always found it difficult to tell those apart, Sebastian.'

He put the telephone down. He sat for a moment with his hand still on it, and considered how much Crane had already known before dialling: the position, the collateral, the morning's conversations in two capitals. More than a man in Mayfair ought to know. He filed that away, in the place where he kept things about Sebastian Crane, which was not a small place.

Then he turned the draft over and read it once more.

What he was about to do would be called, by Monday evening, the act that broke a clearing house, and by the following week the act that broke a government, and he would be named in both accounts and in neither would he be wrong. He had one client. He had been given one duty. He had been told, by people whose only interest was in keeping the money where it was, that there was nothing to fear, and he had been hearing the word "freeze" since midnight.

He signed it at 12.14 and gave it to Nasser, and watched him carry it out of the room.

Then he picked up the telephone again. 'The Chancellor's private office, in London,' he said. 'Tell them it is not urgent.' He paused. 'They will know that it is.'
