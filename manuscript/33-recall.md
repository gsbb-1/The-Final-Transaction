# Chapter 33: Recall

The call came at ten to six on Friday morning, London time, which was mid-morning at home and the hour at which the men who ran his country liked to have done something before lunch. Khalid Al-Mansouri was dressed. He had been dressed since four, at the table under the window of a suite on Park Lane that his office kept for him and that he had not asked for, reading the overnight prices on a single sheet because he would not have a screen in the room.

He knew the voice. The secretary to the council had been a clerk when Khalid was a boy and had risen by never once being the man who delivered news; he delivered texts, and read them in a tone that made clear he had not written them. He read this one in full. The council thanked the managing director for his service in a period of exceptional difficulty. It wished to consult him in person on the future disposition of the nation's assets abroad. He was recalled for consultations with effect from Monday. A seat had been reserved on Sunday night's flight. In his absence from London the office would report to the council directly.

'Who drafted it?' Khalid said.

'It was agreed,' the secretary said, which was the answer, and then, because he had known Khalid's father: 'Your cousin was in the room. He did not speak. He did not need to.'

'Thank you.'

'Sir. Sunday night.'

'I heard you.'

He put the telephone down and sat with his hand on it, as he had sat twice before in a fortnight, and found that this time there was nothing to consider. He had expected it. He had said as much to a man with a rucksack, ten days ago, in this room. *Before I am recalled, which will happen, because my cousin is not stupid.* It was almost a relief to be right about something.

***

Nasser came at seven with the morning's figures and the knowledge already on his face. The office at home would have rung him first; that was how a recall worked. One did not tell the man. One told the men around him, so that by the time he knew, they knew what they were.

'You have seen the text,' Khalid said.

'I have been sent it, sir. For the file.'

'Sit.'

Nasser sat, which he did not usually do with the figures in his hand. Outside, the park was coming up grey and the traffic on Park Lane had begun to make the glass hum, as it had every morning since Tuesday.

'Tell me where we are. Not the council. The book.'

'Closed, all but a fraction. The last of the positions go today and Monday. The collateral released, sold, settled. The proceeds are where you sent them, in four places, none of them London or New York.' He did not give the figure for what the fortnight had cost; he had stopped giving it on the Wednesday of the first week, and Khalid had let him stop. 'We are, as of this morning, very nearly out. The thing you set out to do on the Saturday is done.'

'Yes.'

'And the thing you set out to do on Tuesday night is not.'

'No.'

He had gone to the Bank on Thursday afternoon with nothing in his hands. That was the honest account, and he made himself give it, silently, in the words he would have used to his father. He had asked for the meeting on the Tuesday night from this table, within an hour of Whitlock leaving, and had been given Thursday at three: a deputy governor, two people from the side of the Bank that watched clearing houses, a room on the second floor with a portrait in it, tea. <!-- VERIFY: a sovereign fund's head would plausibly be received at deputy-governor level at short notice during a crisis; the room is invented --> He had been meant to bring a page with twelve names on it and an order, and the man who had the page. On Wednesday morning, at a quarter past nine, Whitlock had rung the office from a telephone number the office had on a visitor's slip and had said, to Nasser, that the page had been in the bag, and the bag was gone, and he was rebuilding it, and had rung off before Nasser could ask him anything, which Nasser had reported with something close to respect.

So Khalid had gone to the Bank with a proposal and no map. He had said what he could say. That the fund was prepared, in principle, to bring a part of its proceeds back into London to buy what certain clearing members would shortly be forced to sell, at the moment they were forced to sell it, if the Bank would lend against the purchases the same morning and if the Treasury stood behind the Bank. He had said that he had reason to believe the order of those sales could be known in advance. He had not said how. He had not said the name of the man, though they knew it; he had not said that the man had lost the thing that made him worth listening to.

The deputy governor had been courteous. Courtesy, Khalid had learned at nineteen in this city, was what the English did instead of saying no. The Bank could not lend against what the fund had not yet bought, to buy what the fund could not yet name, under an indemnity which the Chancellor had declined, in writing, to give. The Bank was already lending to the clearing house and its members, from Monday, under its own powers; that was what was keeping the second member standing. Beyond that, the Bank's hands were where the Treasury had put them. If the fund were to come back with something more concrete, the Bank would of course be glad to see it. The deputy governor had used the word *concrete* twice, and had looked, both times, at the empty table in front of Khalid's hands.

Forty minutes. He had walked back along Threadneedle Street in the rain and had understood that he had done something he had not done since he was a young man at a Western bank: he had gone into a room without the number.

'It went nowhere,' he said now, to Nasser. 'You know that. You were there.'

'It went nowhere because there was nothing to carry, sir. Not because the room was closed.'

'The room is closed on Monday. I am recalled on Monday. Whoever sits at this table on Tuesday will not go to the Bank.'

Nasser said nothing. He had learned long ago that a silence would be filled.

'Say the name.'

'They are saying a name at home, sir. It is not confirmed. It is a man who has dined with your cousin twice this fortnight and who said at the council on Tuesday that the nation's money should never again sit where a foreign minister could reach it, in words that were not his. If it is he, he will sell what is left, keep the proceeds where they are, and recommend that no part of them ever return to London in his lifetime. He will be praised for it. He will be right, by every measure the council uses.'

'And London?'

'London is not his client, sir. It was not yours.'

That was fair. Khalid let it be fair. He had said it himself, in the bridge, on the Saturday, with his hand on an instruction: one client, one duty. He had pulled the largest line out of a clearing house because the alternative was to watch it frozen, and he had known what the pulling would do, and it had done it. A member had failed. A second was standing on money the Bank lent it each night. A man who had written down the shape of it for four years had been chased across two counties and had lost his books to a woman with a forged cloakroom ticket, in the lobby of this hotel, while Khalid sat upstairs being told it was never an accident.

He had believed for two weeks that he served his client best by leaving. He had been told, by a stranger, that he might serve it by coming back, and had not yet decided whether the stranger was right. He found that the recall had decided it for him, in the way a deadline decided things: not by argument, but by removing the time in which to go on not deciding.

'How long,' he said, 'before the new man is in this chair and able to sign?'

Nasser considered. 'The council meets Monday. Consultations, they say, so there will be a form of hearing, which is to say they will let you speak. A week, sir, perhaps. Perhaps less, if your cousin has done his counting. Before the end of next week, somebody else will be the fund in London.'

'Then I have a week in which I am still the fund in London.'

'You have until Monday morning in which you are the fund in London with authority, sir. After that you are a man awaiting consultations.'

'Yes,' Khalid said. 'I know the difference. I am going to ask you to pretend, for three days, that you do not.'

***

He stood, and went to the window, which he had not done all week because the man who had sat across this table from him had told him that a fund manager in Mayfair stood at his window, and he had not wished to resemble him.

'I want the London office ready to transact on Tuesday morning. Lines reopened with the three clearing banks, in writing, today, so that they can take the other side in size at the open without ringing anyone for permission. Custody accounts in London able to receive cash from the four places by Monday night; the instructions drafted and signed now, dated and held. Dealers on call from six on Tuesday. A mandate letter to the Bank, ready for whoever may be in a position to accept it, setting out what we would buy, in what order, and against what we would borrow. The order to be left blank. The man who knows it is not in a position to give it to us yet.'

Nasser did not write any of it down.

'And the authority for the purchases themselves?'

'I will sign on Sunday. Everything, dated Sunday.'

'Sir.' Nasser had stood up as well. He did not often choose his words in front of Khalid; he chose them now, and Khalid watched him do it. 'I have been with you seven years. I was in the room when you took the money out of Mr Crane's fund and I will go where you go after Monday, wherever that is. So I am going to say this once, so that it has been said by somebody who is not your cousin.

'A recall is not a dismissal. It is the step before one, and the step is there so that a man may come home and be dismissed with honour and a pension and his name. If you sign on Sunday what you have just described, and it is acted on Tuesday, after you have been told in writing that your authority ends Monday, then you have moved the nation's money back into the one country it was taken out of, on your own signature, against the council's instruction, on the word of a man wanted by the English police, using a list he cannot prove and no longer has. If it works, the fund did it, and you will be forgiven in the way men are forgiven at home, which is quietly and not entirely. If it does not work, you did it. Alone. With money that was not yours.' He paused. 'You know what they will call that at home, sir. It is the same word the English have been using about Mr Whitlock all week. And they do not have a civil order for it there. They have another thing.'

The glass hummed. A bus went by below, red, full of people on their way to a Friday.

'You are telling me not to do it.'

'No, sir. I am telling you what it costs. You asked me on the Saturday what the instruction would cost and I told you, and you signed it. I would not have you sign this one knowing less.'

'And if I had asked you what it costs to do nothing?'

Nasser looked at him for a moment. 'Then I would have said that you have already paid that, sir, and so has London, and it is on the sheet in my hand, and you have asked me not to read it to you.'

Khalid almost smiled. 'Prepare the office. All of it. Bring me the papers tonight.'

'Sir.'

'And ring Mr Whitlock. The number on the slip. Tell him Sunday, here, at whatever hour he can manage, with whatever he has by then. Tell him the Bank said *concrete*.' He turned back to the window, to the park, and the roofs beyond it, and somewhere beyond those a clearing house that was standing up each morning on borrowed money. 'And Nasser. The flight on Sunday night. Do not cancel it. I would like my cousin to believe, until Monday, that I am on it.'
