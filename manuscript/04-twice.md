# Chapter 4: Twice

#1401

Thursday. Ordinary. I am writing that down because I want it on the page before whatever comes after it.

Up at 6. Elsie slept through, the first time in nine nights, and Laura and I lay in the dark not saying so in case it heard. Photographed last night's page. Checked the address. Checked it again. Sent it to myself and watched it arrive.

The 7am call went out and was met. Gilts down a little overnight, nothing to see, the kind of day the screens could be switched off and nobody would notice until Friday.

Graham was in at 7.30, which is early for him, and said 'Morning, Jonas' from the far side of the risk room without stopping, and that was the whole of our first conversation. I did not ask about the email. He did not mention it. Two men not mentioning the same thing all day is a kind of conversation, and by the evening I knew most of what it had said.

***

Priya found it at 10.20.

The check is routine. Every morning the team takes the list of securities each member has posted to us and lays it against what has actually settled into our account, line by line, and anything that was promised and has not arrived by the cut-off goes on a list. <!-- VERIFY: that a clearing house reconciles pledged securities collateral against settled holdings daily, and what the realistic cut-off is --> Most mornings the list is short and dull: a fat finger, a bank holiday abroad, a bond that matured and nobody told us. Priya does the list on Thursdays because she asked to, four years ago, and nobody has ever asked for it back.

One line. A pension fund, not a large one, a client of one of our clearing members; a scheme for people who had worked for a chain of shops. £42m nominal of a single gilt, the 2045, posted to us on Tuesday against their swaps and not settled by Wednesday's cut-off, and still not settled this morning. <!-- VERIFY: that a pension scheme would post gilts to a clearing house via a clearing member and that a two-day settlement fail in a posted gilt line is plausible -->

She rang the member. The member rang the fund. The fund rang Priya back, which they should not do, and told her, and she wrote it down as they said it, that it was a timing error. The bonds were theirs. The bonds had been out on repo and were due back at 2pm, at which point they would be delivered to us and everyone could get on with their day.

She came and stood at my desk with the sheet.

'£42m,' she said. 'The 2045. Posted to us Tuesday. Out on repo to a bank since last Thursday. Same line. Same nominal. I asked them for their position report and they sent it, which tells you they don't know what it says.' She put it in front of me. 'There. That line is in two places. We think we've got it. The bank thinks it's got it. Nobody has got it except the fund, who have lent it out and promised it to us in the same breath. And they call that timing.'

'It might be timing.'

'It might. It's the second time in a month. I checked. September the 9th, same fund, same line, "timing".' She said the quotation marks aloud. 'Timing happens once.'

I read the sheet. I had read it before. Not this sheet, not this fund, but the shape of it. The same bond in two columns, and a phone call that says *timing* in a voice that would like you to stop asking.

'Again,' I said. I did not mean to say it aloud.

Priya heard it. She hears everything. She let it go, for then.

***

She came back at 2.15, because 2pm had come and gone and the bonds had not.

'Now it's half past two tomorrow,' she said. 'They've gone from timing to "operational". I want to tell the member to post cash against it by close or we'll call the line short.' <!-- VERIFY: whether a clearing house would demand substitute cash collateral from the clearing member when a client's pledged securities fail to settle, and how quickly -->

'Do that.'

'I'm going to. I want to understand first why you went white.'

'I didn't go white.'

'You said "again" and you went the colour of the sheet. Explain it to me. Not what a repo is; I know what a repo is. Why this matters, to us, if it's one fund and £42m.'

I looked for where to start. She had the maths; she had more maths than I do. What she had not had, because nobody has unless they have sat through one, was the morning.

I said: 'The deposit we hold is a bond, and a bond has a price. When the price falls, every deposit in the building is worth less the next morning, so at 7am we ring everybody and ask for more. On the same morning, every bank that has lent money against gilts rings its borrowers and asks for more as well. That is the system working. That is what it is meant to do.'

'Fine.'

'Now put that fund in the picture. Their £42m is sitting behind their promise to us and behind their promise to the bank. Two promises, one bond. On an ordinary day no one notices, because no one asks for the bond. On the morning the price falls, we ask, and the bank asks, and the fund has one bond and two phone calls.'

'So one of you gets it and one of you doesn't.'

'One of us gets it. The other gets a promise, and promises don't count at 7am. So the one with the promise sells something to cover the gap. That pushes the price down a little more. At 7am the next day everybody gets rung again, for a little more, and the fund is still one bond short.' I stopped. 'That's it. That's the whole of it. It isn't complicated. It's just arithmetic that only gets done once.'

She did not say anything for a while, which for Priya is a while.

'And if it isn't one fund.'

'Then it isn't one fund.'

'How many is it?'

'I don't have that number.'

I have that number. It is in the bag under my desk. I am not proud of the sentence and I am recording it because that is what this is for.

'My parents,' she said, and stopped, and started again in the voice she uses for figures. 'My parents have their savings in a scheme that does exactly this. Not this one. One like it. They think it's safe because it's in government bonds. I've told them it's in government bonds. I didn't tell them that the bonds are out on loan half the week so that the scheme can make an extra quarter of a per cent.' She picked up the sheet. 'Write it up, you're going to say.'

'Write it up.'

'For whom?'

'Graham.'

'Graham will file it.'

'Yes.'

'Then what's the point.'

'Then it's written.'

She looked at me as if I had said something in a language she did not know I spoke, and went back to her desk, and wrote it up. Two pages. She sent it to me at 4.50 and it was exact and unkind and correct in every figure, and I would not have changed a word, and I did not.

***

Graham, the rest of the day.

He came by at 11 and asked whether the fail on the list was 'in hand'. I said it was. He said, 'Good.' He stood a moment longer than the word needed, as he had yesterday, and then said, 'Anything that comes out of that, I'd like to see before it goes into the daily. Not after. Before.' He has never asked for that. He has never needed to, because I have always copied him. I said, 'Of course,' and he said, 'Thank you, Jonas,' in the voice of a man reading the name off a badge.

He went past again at 3.40 and did not look in. He left at 5.15, early, with his coat already on from the lift, and he said 'Night' to the room.

In five years he has asked about Elsie every working day since she was born. 255 of them. Yesterday and today, nothing. I know what that is. I knew it at 11.30 last night in the kitchen with the phone in my hand, and today confirmed it without a word being said, which is how Graham confirms things.

He has read the page. He has read both pages. He has done what he does, which is to tell someone, and I do not know who, and I do not think he would tell me if I asked, and I am not going to ask. Asking tells him I know. Not asking tells him nothing. That is the only piece of this I still hold, so I am holding it.

***

Priya came back at 6.10 with her coat on.

'Sent it to you. Not to him. You can forward it, that's your job.' She stood. 'How many times have you seen this, Jonas? Exactly.'

'I don't count.'

'You count everything. You counted the signatures on your own birthday card. 31; I heard you.' She was not smiling. 'You said "again" before you'd read which fund. You knew the shape of it before you knew the name. You've seen this a hundred times, and it isn't in a single report I've ever read, and I've read all of them, because I check your work. That's why you keep me.'

I did not say anything. There is nothing in the handbook about what to say when someone is right.

'Fine,' she said. 'I'll write it up, and he'll file it, and you'll say, then it's written. And then I'm going to go and find whatever it is you're not putting in the reports. Because it's in there, in the data, somewhere, or you wouldn't look like that when a two-day fail comes across your desk. And you're not the only one here who can read a spreadsheet.'

She went.

Entries in this record where one line of bonds stands behind two promises at once: 94, with today's. Times it has been formally written up: twice. Once at 37, by me. Once today, by her.

She is the best analyst I have worked with. She will find it. I could save her a month by opening the bag. I have 94 entries that say what she will find, and no answer to the question she will ask me when she does, which is why she is going to have to find it on her own.
