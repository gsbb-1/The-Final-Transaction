# Chapter 4: Twice

#1401

Thursday. Ordinary. I am writing that down because I want it on the page before whatever comes after it.

Up at 6. Elsie slept through, the first time in nine nights, and we lay in the dark not saying so in case it heard. Photographed last night's page. Checked the address. Checked it again. Sent it to myself and watched it arrive.

The 7am call went out and was met by nine. Gilts down a little overnight, nothing to see.

Graham was in at 7.30 and said 'Morning, Jonas' from the far side of the risk room without stopping, and that was the whole of our first conversation. Neither of us mentioned the email. Two men not mentioning the same thing all day is a kind of conversation, and by the evening I knew most of what it had said.

***

Priya found it at 10.20.

The check is routine. Every morning the team takes the list of securities each member has instructed into our account and lays it against what has actually settled there, line by line. A bond that has not settled is not collateral. It is nothing. It does not exist in our books until the settlement system says it has arrived, and until then the member covers the hole with cash, whether it wants to or not. So anything instructed and failed goes on a list. Most mornings the list is short and dull: a fat finger, a bank holiday abroad, a bond that matured and nobody told us. Priya does the list on Thursdays because she asked to, four years ago, and nobody has ever asked for it back.

One line. A pension fund, not a large one, a scheme for people who had worked for a chain of shops, hedging through the fund that does its hedging for it, clearing through one of our members. £42m nominal of a single gilt, the 2045, instructed into our account on Tuesday against the scheme's swaps. Failed Tuesday. Failed Wednesday. Sent again this morning, and at ten still not settled. The member had been paying cash against the hole since Tuesday afternoon, which members do not like doing for three days, and that, not us, is why the member had rung the fund.

The fund rang Priya back, which they should not do, and told her it was a timing error. The bonds were theirs. They had been out on repo and were due back at 2pm, at which point they would be delivered to us and everyone could get on with their day.

She brought me the sheet.

'£42m,' she said. 'The 2045. Instructed Tuesday, failed three times. Out on repo to a bank since last Thursday, by their own account. I asked them for their position report and they sent it, which tells you they don't know what it says.' She put it in front of me. 'There. That line is out on loan for cash, and they've promised the same line to us against the swaps. The bank's got it, or has lent it on. We've got the member's cash and a promise. The fund has lent the bond out and promised it to us in the same breath. And they call that timing.'

'It might be timing.'

'It might. It's the second time in a month. I checked. September the 9th, same fund, same line, "timing".' She said the quotation marks aloud. 'Timing happens once.'

I read the sheet. I had read it before. Not this sheet, not this fund, but the shape of it. A bond that is always about to arrive, and a phone call that says *timing* in a voice that would like you to stop asking. I knew something about the 9th that she did not. Another member's client had failed a line that morning too, a different fund, a different gilt, the same word. It is in here. Two fails on one morning with one excuse is not two timing errors. It is a tight repo market, and two people who had lent out what they had promised.

'Again,' I said. I did not mean to say it aloud.

Priya heard it. She let it go, for then.

***

She came back at 2.15, because 2pm had come and gone and the bonds had not.

'Now it's half past two tomorrow,' she said. 'They've gone from timing to "operational". The member's treasurer has stopped being polite about the cash. I want to refuse the next instruction until the fund can show me the bonds are back in its own account, and I want to tell the member's credit officer that its client is instructing us bonds it does not hold.'

'Do that.'

'I'm going to. I want to understand first why you went white. You said "again" and you went the colour of the sheet. Explain it to me. Not what a repo is; I know what a repo is. Why this matters, to us, if it's one fund and £42m.'

She had the maths; more than I do. What she had not had, because nobody has unless they have sat through one, was the morning.

I said: 'Part of the cushion we hold is bonds, and a bond has a price. When the price falls, every cushion in the building is thinner the next morning, so the call goes out to everybody at seven, and by nine the cash has to be in. On the same morning, every bank that has lent money against gilts calls its borrowers for more as well. That is the system working. That is what it is meant to do.'

'Fine.'

'Now put that fund in the picture. Its £42m is out on repo: it has sold the bond to a bank for cash and promised to buy it back. The same £42m is promised to us. Two promises, one bond, and on an ordinary day nobody notices, because nobody asks for the bond. On the morning the price falls, the bank wants more collateral or its cash back, and we want the bond we were promised, and the fund has one bond and two calls.'

'So one of you gets it and one of you doesn't.'

'One of us gets it. The other gets a promise, and promises don't count at nine. So the one holding the promise sells something to cover the gap. That pushes the price down a little more. The next morning everybody is called again, for a little more, and the fund is still one bond short.' I stopped. 'It isn't complicated. It's just arithmetic that only gets done once.'

She did not say anything for a while, which for Priya is a while.

'And if it isn't one fund.'

'Then it isn't one fund.'

'How many is it?'

'I don't have that number.'

I have a number. It is in the bag under my desk. I am not proud of the sentence and I am recording it because that is what this is for.

It is not the number she thinks it is. I have never seen a bond in two places. Nobody can: one gilt in the 2045 is the same as every other, there are thirty-odd billion of them, and our system only counts what has arrived. What I have seen is what it leaves behind. Fails that come in pairs, at two members who have never met, on the mornings the repo market is tight. The word *timing* twice on one day. Cash paid at nine and swapped out for bonds at a quarter past three, when the repo market hands the bonds back, and the same swap the next day. Once in a while a position report nobody should have sent, with a line marked *on loan* that we were also promised. Fingerprints. I have been logging them for four years and I have drawn the chains they imply, and I cannot prove a single one, and I write that down too, most weeks.

'My parents,' she said, and stopped, and started again in her figures voice. 'My parents have their savings in a scheme that does exactly this. Not this one. One like it. They think it's safe because it's in government bonds. I didn't tell them that the bonds are out on loan half the week so that the scheme can make an extra quarter of a per cent.' She picked up the sheet. 'Write it up, you're going to say.'

'Write it up.'

'For whom?'

'Graham.'

'Graham will file it.'

'Yes.'

'Then what's the point.'

'Then it's written.'

She looked at me as if I had said something in a language she did not know I spoke, and went back to her desk and wrote it up. Two pages, sent to me at 4.50, exact and unkind and correct in every figure. I did not change a word.

***

Graham, the rest of the day.

He came by at 11 and asked whether the fail on the list was 'in hand'. I said it was. He said, 'Good.' He stood a moment longer than the word needed, as he had yesterday, and then said, 'Anything that comes out of that, I'd like to see before it goes into the daily. Not after. Before.' He has never needed to ask that; I have always copied him. I said, 'Of course,' and he said, 'Thank you, Jonas,' like a man reading the name off a badge.

He left at 5.15, early, coat already on from the lift, and said 'Night' to the room.

He has asked about Elsie every working day since she was born. 255 of them. Yesterday and today, nothing. I knew what it was at midnight last night in the kitchen with the phone in my hand, and today confirmed it without a word, which is how Graham confirms things.

He has read both pages. He has done what he does, which is to tell someone, and I do not know who, and I am not going to ask. Asking tells him I know. Not asking tells him nothing. That is the only piece of this I still hold, so I am holding it.

***

Priya came back at 6.10 with her coat on.

'Sent it to you. Not to him. You can forward it; that's your job. How many times have you seen this, Jonas? Exactly.'

'I don't count.'

'You count everything. You counted the signatures on your own birthday card. 31; I heard you.' She was not smiling. 'You said "again" before you'd read which fund. You knew the shape of it before you knew the name. You've seen this a hundred times, and it isn't in a single report I've ever read, and I've read all of them. That's why you keep me.'

There is nothing in the handbook about what to say when someone is right.

'Fine,' she said. 'I'll write it up, and he'll file it, and you'll say, then it's written. And then I'm going to find whatever it is you're not putting in the reports. Because it's in the data somewhere, or you wouldn't look like that when a two-day fail comes across your desk. And you're not the only one here who can read a spreadsheet.'

She went.

Entries in this record where the fingerprints say one line of bonds is standing behind two promises at once: 94, with today's. Times I have seen the bond itself in two places: none. Times it has been formally written up: twice. Once at 37, by me. Once today, by her.

She is the best analyst I have worked with. She will find it. I could save her a month by opening the bag. I have 94 entries that say what she will find, and no proof, and no answer to the question she will ask me when she does, which is why she is going to have to find it on her own.
