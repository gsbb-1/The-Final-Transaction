# Chapter 8: The study

#1402

The room had no screens. Visitors noticed, and Sebastian Crane enjoyed watching them notice: the glance to the walls, the second glance, the small adjustment as they understood that the man who ran Greywether Capital did not look at prices. There were two chairs, a desk with nothing on it but a telephone and a fountain pen, and a window onto a street in Mayfair quiet enough that he could hear a parking warden's shoes. Prices were for the floor below. He paid 31 people to watch them. He had found, over 25 years, that people who watched prices all day could not see them at all.

The telephone rang at 4.55pm and he let it ring twice.

'Anthony.'

'You'll have seen the day,' said Sir Anthony Pell.

'I'm told it was eventful.' He had seen the day. British government bonds down hard at the open, a stagger back at lunchtime when the Chancellor went on television to say the word "contained", down again when New York woke up. Sterling as expected. Oil doing what oil did when a strait closed. Greywether had been positioned for exactly this since the spring, and the day had paid for the building. He felt about it as he felt about weather. 'How was the board?'

'Richard took us through it at a quarter past four. Twenty minutes, by telephone. The margin calls were met.'

'Just, I hear.'

A pause. Pell did not ask where he had heard. 'Just. That isn't why I'm calling.'

'No,' Crane agreed, and waited. He had known Anthony Pell for 30 years, since a dinner neither of them remembered, and in all that time Pell had never once said anything quickly.

'There's a man in Richard's risk function. Head of collateral. Whitlock. He went over Pryce's head this afternoon, straight to the Bank, with a paper saying the collateral in the system is pledged several times over and a sharp enough move would show it.'

'Is it?'

'Sebastian.'

'I'm asking what the paper says.'

'The paper says what a man like that would say. Richard says it's a model, and models say things. That isn't the point either.' Pell's voice dropped, as if the boardroom had followed him into his car. 'He keeps a diary. Pryce found out on Wednesday, by accident, some nonsense with an email. Every day, for four years, by hand. What he saw in the collateral, who he told, what they said back. Richard's phrase was "where the bodies are buried".'

Crane looked at the wall where a screen would have been.

'Four years,' he said.

'Nearly. Pryce says about 1,400 entries.'

'And Richard would like to have it.'

'Richard would like it not to exist. He has asked the man for any personal notes relating to the firm's business, as if it were a matter of policy. He's narrowed the man's access. He has told the board that we are managing it.' Pell gave the phrase the exact weight it carried in meetings. 'Pryce has a friend at the Treasury. Carrow. I gather the Chancellor's office was told on Wednesday evening, which is why I'm telling you tonight and not next week. I didn't want you hearing it from her.'

'Caroline doesn't call me any more, Anthony. I'm a donor. One hears from donors at Christmas.'

'Well. Now you've heard it from me.'

'I have,' Crane said, 'and I'm grateful. Go home.' He put the telephone down and sat for a moment with his hands flat on the empty desk.

Greywether's position was large and it was right, and he had spent the summer explaining to investors that right was not the same as paid. A short was a bet that something would fall. The money came when it fell, and only then, and the timing was everything. He knew that the British government's bonds were worth less than their price. He knew the pound was carried on a confidence it had not earned. He knew that somewhere under the clearing houses the same bonds had been promised to too many people at once, so that when the first promise was called, the others would find their hands empty. What he did not know was where. Which member bank would be first to fail. Whose collateral was thinnest. On which morning. He had been guessing, expensively, for six months.

A man who had written it down every day for four years had not been guessing. He had a map.

Crane opened the desk drawer and took out the mobile phone he kept there, which had one number in it.

'Mr Crane.'

'Dana. Are you busy?'

'I'm at work.'

'There's a man at Walbrook Clearing House, near Bank. Jonas Whitlock. Head of collateral risk. He keeps a diary. By hand. About four years of it.'

'And you'd like to read it.'

'I'd like to own it.'

'Where is it?'

'If I knew that, I'd have sent a courier.'

Kessler said nothing for a moment. He heard, faintly, a keyboard. 'Herne Hill,' she said. 'Married to a doctor.' It had taken her perhaps fifteen seconds.

'How long do I have?'

'Markets open on Monday. Before then would be elegant. Before Sunday night would be useful.'

'Then I start now. I'll have someone outside his building within the hour, and I'll look at the house, and at anything else with his name on it. Most of what people think is hidden is on a public register for a few pounds. You'll hear from me when there is something to hear.'

'And not how.'

'You've never wanted to know how, Mr Crane.'

'No,' he said. 'I've never needed to.'

He put the phone back in the drawer and closed it. Below him 31 people were watching prices, and the prices were going his way, and he found that for the first time all day he had stopped being interested in them.

Third sitting. Past midnight. I said the rest of today was the people. There is one more thing, and it is not people.

Left at 6.10. Wrote that already. Didn't write how I got to Lewisham, because I wanted #212 down first and because by the time I sat down I had half decided I'd imagined it.

A man in a grey jacket on the far side of the street when I came out. Noticed him because he wasn't looking at his phone. Everyone else was.

Bank. From Bank I take the DLR to Lewisham, straight through, no changes, I could do it asleep. He came down the escalator 15 steps behind me. So I didn't take the DLR.

Northern line, southbound. He got in the next carriage. London Bridge: I got off, stood by the map, let the doors beep, got on, got off again. He didn't.

His face through the glass as it pulled out. Nothing on it. That's what I keep coming back to. A man who had missed his stop would look annoyed.

Up and out and round to the mainline side. The 7.02 to Lewisham. Looked back nine times. Nobody. By the time I had the strip light on at the second try I had decided he was a man in a grey jacket going to Borough. London is full of them.

If I had got on the DLR he would have stood in the dark of that garage with me, with the trains going over, and watched me open a steel toolbox.

Home from Lewisham by a way I'm not writing down. 8.20. Laura had texted at 6.40: at Aisha's with Elsie, back by nine. House dark. Locked, front and back. Kitchen table, coat on, #212, first sitting. Laura and Elsie home at 9.20, Elsie asleep in the car seat with her mouth open. Laura went up with her. Second sitting. Then I went up too.

I went up because of the last line of the second sitting. If the answer is not Haldane, I wanted the acknowledgement. Three years ago the Treasury sent one. Two lines and a reference number, no name. It is in the folder for that year, right-hand drawer of the desk in the study. First floor, back. I keep things where I can find them in the dark.

I know what that room looks like the way you know your own hand. I leave the chair out, at an angle, because nobody else sits in it. It was pushed in. Square to the desk.

Left drawer: three pens, the chequebook, Elsie's red book, the house insurance. Front to back, in that order. Still in that order. Chequebook the wrong way up. Right drawer: folders by year, oldest at the front. Someone had put them back in order. Just not mine. Bookshelf: fine. The box of Elsie's photographs on top of the cupboard: lid on, straight. I never get the lid on straight.

Window catch down, the way I leave it. Nothing forced. Someone had a key, or didn't need one.

Nothing missing. Counted twice. The acknowledgement was where it should be. Two lines. A number. No name.

Then the bottom of the right drawer. The spares. A box of six, same make, black, A5, hardback, the kind with the elastic, still in the plastic. It lives under the folders. It was on top.

So now they know the shape of the thing. Black. A5. Hardback. And not here.

Eight full ones. Never in this house. Not for one night. They are in a garage with my father's name on the agreement, not mine; the rent goes out of my account and nothing else of mine touches it. Number nine was on my back, in my bag, on the Northern line, with a man in a grey jacket looking at it through the glass.

Put the chair back at its angle. Turned the chequebook over. Lid on crooked. Came down and stood in the kitchen with the light off and my hands on the counter until I could feel them. That is where I am writing this.

Laura is asleep. When she came in at 9.20 she looked at me and said, 'You look like a patient,' and I said long day, and she looked at my hands. She does that: looks at what the hands are doing while the mouth talks. I put them in my pockets. Which is not what you do if you're fine. I know that. She knows that. I had not been near the study yet, and she had already seen it on me.

I haven't told her. Reasons, in order. One: she would want the police, and the police would ask what was taken, and I would say nothing, and they would ask what someone wanted, and I would have to say. Two: she would ask what is in them. Three: I would have to tell her that it was not the first lie. "Work" was the first lie. I have been telling it for four years, one word at a time.

Who. Graham. Haldane. The board, because that is what boards are for. Carrow, since nine this morning, and whoever Carrow talks to. On Wednesday morning the number of people who knew the record existed was one. Me. It is Friday. I can't count them any more. That is the first time I have written that sentence about anything.

One more thing. Then I'll stop.

They came between 6.40 and 8.20. Laura is home every Friday evening. Every one. Tonight was the first Friday in months she wasn't, and she decided at six, and she texted me at 6.40, and they were already on their way.

They knew when the house would be empty.

They weren't watching me. I was on a train with the one they sent for me. They were watching her.
